Joseph McGee Knott (~1851 OH – 1932 Cedar Rapids IA)

Ahnentafel #26 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph McGee Knott was born on 5 March 1851 at Lowell, in Washington County, Ohio, on the Muskingum, the son of Benjamin and Margaret Nott. The family spelled the name both ways for a century; he was born a Nott and died a Knott.

He grew up on his father’s farm in Adams Township, with the post office at Lowell: Benjamin and Margaret, both forty-three in 1860, and the boys — P. J. fifteen, William F. thirteen, and “Jos M” eleven. A brother, John A. Nott, born in 1846, died in 1851, the year Joseph was born, and lies in the Mason cemetery near the village. In the summer of 1859 diphtheria went through Adams Township and carried off a houseful of five-year-olds, among them a Thomas H. Nott, aged five. Joseph, then eight, came through it.

By about 1868 he was at work in a steel mill, and he stayed at it: a heater, fifty-seven years in the trade, last working in 1925. The work took him to Terre Haute, Indiana, about 1881, and he was a resident of the city for the next fifty years. About 1897 he married Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford, a widow with a young son, Percy; their daughters were Mildred and Helen. The family lived at 3500 Wabash Avenue.

He was not only a mill hand. By the summer of 1915 Joseph Knott of 623 North Ninth Street was one of the City of Terre Haute’s four park superintendents, in charge of Steeg Park; on 1 October 1915, with the formal close of the park season, he and the other three superintendents and nine labourers were dropped from the city payroll. Two days later he was in the witness chair. The city council was taking evidence in the great Terre Haute election-fraud inquiry into Mayor Donn M. Gossom, and “Joseph Knott, of 623 North Ninth street, until recently superintendent of Steeg park, was next called.” He testified that the mayor had asked him to wake him on the morning of the election to go down and organise the board, that Gossom then left the precinct in Knott’s charge and drove off saying “he had a lot off ‘hunkies’ he wanted to run in” — to be picked up “around the Malleable works or stamping mill” — and that Gossom told him that night, “I finished up across the river this evening.”

In November 1914 he and his wife, then living on Rose Avenue, announced their elder daughter Mildred’s engagement to Emmet C. Cooper, and in July 1916 their first grandson was born in their house and went onto the front page of the Tribune as the first war baby born in Terre Haute.

On 1 September 1931, at eighty, he left Terre Haute for Cedar Rapids, Iowa, to live with Mildred — Mrs. E. C. Cooper, of 2505 First Avenue. He had been there four and a half months when he died, in her house at 10:40 in the morning on Friday 15 January 1932, of arteriosclerosis, with a cerebral haemorrhage on the 7th and bronchopneumonia on the 14th among the causes; a doctor had been attending him since 24 October. He left a wife, two daughters and four grandchildren. There was no service in Iowa: the family set off for Terre Haute the next day, and on 18 January the Ryan funeral home buried him at Highland Lawn, Section 14, Lot 386. His flat granite marker reads simply Joseph Knott 1851 1932. Thirteen years later his widow was laid in the same grave, of the same cause, by the same undertaker.

Martha Ellen Lamb (1865 Richmond – 1945 Terre Haute)

Ahnentafel #27 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Martha Ellen Lamb was born on 27 August 1865 at Richmond, in Wayne County, Indiana, the second of the four children of Erwin R. Lamb and his first wife Melissa J. Bailey. She was ten years old when her mother died at Newport, after a few days’ illness, on the evening of 29 November 1875.

Her father married again in May 1879, to Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, a Civil War widow with five children of her own, and in 1880 the blended household was living on Main Street in Vermillion, Indiana: Erwin forty, his new wife, “Mattie E.” the eldest girl at home, her brother Frank, her sister Bell, and a nine-year-old stepsister, Effie Hall. Her father eventually left Indiana altogether and died at Jonesboro, Arkansas, in 1919.

She married twice. Her first husband was George Ford, and their son was Percy. By May 1890 she was keeping house as Mrs. Mattie Ford on north Third Street in Terre Haute together with her brother Frank, and her father came down from Plainfield to visit the pair of them. Mattie was what the family called her.

About 1897, at Terre Haute, she married Joseph McGee Knott, a steel-mill heater fourteen years her senior, and had two daughters, Mildred and Helen. One small document in her own hand survives: the back of a real-photo postcard of the two girls, pencilled “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen” and signed “Mama.” The spelling is phonetic, and it is the only specimen of her writing anyone has found.

She and Joseph announced Mildred’s engagement to Emmet C. Cooper in November 1914, and in July 1916 her first grandchild was born in her house. Joseph died in January 1932 at Cedar Rapids, in Mildred’s house; Martha was with him, and the family travelled home to Terre Haute together for the funeral.

Widowed, Martha moved in with her daughter Helen and son-in-law Gifford Tuttle, and was still in that household in 1940 — so Alec’s grandfather, Richard Clark Tuttle, then ten, grew up under the same roof as his grandmother. She died on 18 January 1945 at Terre Haute of a cerebral haemorrhage, aged seventy-nine, and two days later the Ryan funeral home buried her in Highland Lawn Cemetery, Section 14, Lot 386 — the same grave as Joseph, under a flat granite marker matching his: Martha E. Knott 1865 1945.

Harry B. Frost (~1860 Macclesfield – 1930 Wyckoff)

Ahnentafel #28 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Harry B. Frost was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire, the English silk town, about March 1860, the second son of George Frost and Frances W. Bell, and was baptised Harry Bell Frost in the town’s parochial chapel on 11 April. He was four years old when the family crossed: they came to America in 1864 and settled at Paterson, the silk-weaving capital of the country, built on English hands. He went through the Paterson public schools, finishing at the J. Waters school.

He began work with his father in the throwing business — the twisting of raw silk into yarn — while still a boy. At twenty he was living at 309 Straight Street and working in the silk mill, in a house with his father (fifty-eight, silk manufacturer, rheumatism set down against his name), his mother, a sister and three brothers. That same year, 1880, his father took him and his brother Frank into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, a hundred feet of frontage on Madison Street; the firm was the first house in Paterson to bring in the celebrated Atwood machinery. The mill he was taken into was, as it stood in 1887, a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street, Nos. 34 to 52, with GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING lettered along the pavement, three hundred hands at work in it, one engine of 350 horsepower driving the whole of it, and an artesian well sunk in the yard.

On 10 June 1896, aged thirty-six, giving his trade as silk manufacturer and his home as Paterson, he married at Detroit, Michigan. The bride was Kathaline Chalmers, thirty, a schoolteacher living in Detroit but born at Orange, New Jersey. A pastor named John Reed performed the ceremony, with Mary N. Reed and Edith May Reid as witnesses. Why a Paterson man and a New Jersey woman were married in Michigan nobody in the family can now say.

Three children followed: Katherine King, born in September 1898; Harry Bell, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900; and Alan Chalmers, born at Paterson in February 1903. There was a live-in servant in the house all through those years. About 1908 he moved the family out of the city to Wyckoff Avenue, in Wyckoff, Bergen County, and lived there the last twenty-two years of his life. He was in silk for fifty years — George Frost & Sons, then the Frost & Van Riper Co., with mills at Paterson and at Scranton and Pittston in Pennsylvania — at the end of 1903 the Luzerne County papers noted him as proprietor of his own mill, the West Side Silk Mill at West Pittston — and at the end the Special Rayon Throwing Company. He was a Mason of Joppa Lodge and a life member of Mecca Temple of the Mystic Shrine.

He was ill through the second half of 1930 and died at home on the evening of Thursday 18 December, in his seventy-first year. The funeral was held at the house on the Saturday afternoon at half past two, taken by the rector of St. Paul’s Episcopal Church, Paterson, and the minister of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and he was buried at Cedar Lawn in Paterson, where his father and his brother Frank already lay. “He was of a kindly and generous disposition,” the Wyckoff paper said, “and was always happiest when he had a group of friends around him.” His widow Katharine outlived him by thirteen months.

Katharine Chalmers (1866 Orange NJ – 1932 Paterson)

Ahnentafel #29 · his mother’s mother’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Katharine Chalmers was born in January 1866 at Orange, in Essex County, New Jersey — East Orange, by one later account — one of the four daughters of Mathew Chalmers, a Scot who had retired from dealing in stores, and Ellen King, also a Scot. She became a schoolteacher, and by 1896 she was living in Detroit; it was there, on 10 June that year, that she married Harry B. Frost of Paterson, an English-born silk manufacturer six years her senior.

She named her children from both sides of the house. Katherine King Frost, born in September 1898, carried her own mother’s maiden name; Harry Bell Frost, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900, carried his father’s mother’s; and Alan Chalmers Frost, born at Paterson in February 1903, carried hers. About 1908 the family left the city for Wyckoff Avenue, in Bergen County, and she lived there for the rest of her life, a quarter of a century.

She was a figure in both towns: second vice-president of the Paterson Woman’s Club in 1930 and, until her death, a member of its board of directors and chairman of its reception committee; a charter member and past president of the Wyckoff Woman’s Club, and chairman of its garden committee, which ran the annual flower shows; an active member of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and for many years before that of the Baptist church at East Orange, her mother’s denomination. A former schoolteacher, she served several terms on the Wyckoff Board of Education, and the local paper credited her with many improvements in the town’s schools.

Harry died at the house on 18 December 1930, and she was a widow for thirteen months. In January 1932 her youngest son came up from Wilmington for a weekend; three weeks after that she went into the Paterson General Hospital with a short illness, underwent a serious operation, and died there at a quarter to eleven on the night of Sunday 31 January. The service was held the following Wednesday afternoon at the house on Wyckoff Avenue, taken by the curate of St. Paul’s, Paterson, and the Paterson Woman’s Club postponed its regular meeting and went to the funeral in a body. She left a daughter and two sons, and two sisters at East Orange — Mrs. Margaret Jordan and Miss Sarah Chalmers. She was buried at Cedar Lawn in Paterson, beside Harry.

John G. Roush (1876 Mason Co. WV – 1908)

Ahnentafel #30 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John G. Roush was born in September, in 1876 or in 1877 — his gravestone and his birth entry disagree by a year, and the family never settled it — the third son of Moses B. Roush, a Union veteran, and Sarah Duckworth, the daughter of a Lancashire collier. In 1880 he was down as a two-year-old in his parents’ house in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio. He was three when his mother died, and his father took the four children across the river into Mason County, West Virginia.

On 22 February 1897 he took out a licence at the Mason County courthouse to marry Bertha Ellen Nease. He gave his age as twenty and hers as eighteen; the man who swore the information was her own father, L. J. Nease. They were married two days later at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven by the Rev. D. W. Files. By June 1900 the young family were at Hartford village in Graham District, a few doors from his father Moses, with two small daughters, Nola and a baby.

He was not only a miner — he had been in the insurance business at Mason — but about September 1907 he moved his family to Broad Run to work in Claude Ryan’s mine below Hartford. A year later, at about seven on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, Bertha had been baking and a gallon oil can stood near the hot stove. He set a miner’s lamp on the table where a daughter was ironing, and in trying to fill the lamp from the can touched off an explosion that threw burning oil in every direction. The girl was enveloped in flames and died within a few hours; a second daughter died five days later at New Haven; the house burned with everything in it. His fellow workmen gave up a day’s wages for the family and the mine operator gave fifteen dollars.

He was badly burned, worst about the legs, and nobody expected him to live. He died on 21 October 1908 in Graham District of the effects of the burns, certified by Dr. D. A. Sayre — a married coal miner of thirty-two. The Weekly Register gave him a paragraph two weeks afterwards: “John D. Roush, who was injured several weeks ago at Broad Run, near Hartford, by the explosion of a lamp, died last week from injuries received at that time.” It got his initial wrong, and it said he left “a wife and two children”; in fact three daughters survived him and a fourth was born after his death. In May 1909 his widow qualified in the Mason County court as guardian of “Iva Roush, Florence Roush, Velm Roush and Della Roush, infant children of John G. Roush, deceased,” on a $500 bond, with his father Moses standing surety.

He lies at Broad Run Cemetery under a low granite marker that says only FATHER, and the years 1876 - 1908.

Bertha Ellen Nease (b. 1878 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #31 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Bertha Ellen Nease was born on 12 January 1878 in Mason County, West Virginia, the eldest child of Lewis J. Nease, a farmer, and Martha Jane Love, who had married the April before.

On 22 February 1897, at eighteen, she took out a marriage licence with John G. Roush at the Mason County courthouse, her father giving the clerk her age and birthplace, and two days later they were married at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven. She was marrying back into her own grandmother’s family: her father’s mother had been born a Roush.

By 1900 the couple were keeping house at Hartford village with two small daughters, a few doors from John’s father. In 1907 they moved to Broad Run so that John could go into the mine. On the evening of 12 September 1908 Bertha had been baking when the oil can by the stove took fire; she fought the flames and was burned about the hands and arms. Her eldest daughter died that night, a second five days later, and her husband on 21 October. The house, the furniture and the family’s clothes were all gone, and she was carrying another child.

She did not go back to either set of grandparents. In May 1909 she went to the Mason County court and qualified as guardian of her four daughters on a $500 bond, her father-in-law Moses Roush standing surety. In April 1910 she was the head of her own household at Wagner, Mason County — a widow of thirty-three with Iva Mae, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia under her roof. On 2 March 1914 she married William L. Knopp at Mason City, he thirty-two and she thirty-four, by the Rev. C. H. Cox of the Church of the United Brethren in Christ. In March 1947, when she was ill at Mason, two of her daughters travelled down from Akron and Indiana to see her.

Bertha Ellen Knopp died on 5 April 1950 at St. Joseph’s Hospital, Parkersburg, aged seventy-one, her home still at Mason, West Virginia. Her daughter Iva buried her at Broad Run on 7 April, under a stone cut MOTHER / BERTHA E. ROUSH KNOPP / 1878 - 1950: she carried both her husbands’ names to the grave.
