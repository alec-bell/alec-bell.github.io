Katharine Chalmers (1866 Orange NJ – 1932 Paterson)

Ahnentafel #29 · his mother’s mother’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Katharine Chalmers was born in January 1866 at Orange, in Essex County, New Jersey — East Orange, by one later account — one of the four daughters of Mathew Chalmers, a Scot who had retired from dealing in stores, and Ellen King, also a Scot. She became a schoolteacher, and by 1896 she was living in Detroit; it was there, on 10 June that year, that she married Harry B. Frost of Paterson, an English-born silk manufacturer six years her senior.

She named her children from both sides of the house. Katherine King Frost, born in September 1898, carried her own mother’s maiden name; Harry Bell Frost, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900, carried his father’s mother’s; and Alan Chalmers Frost, born at Paterson in February 1903, carried hers. About 1908 the family left the city for Wyckoff Avenue, in Bergen County, and she lived there for the rest of her life, a quarter of a century.

She was a figure in both towns: second vice-president of the Paterson Woman’s Club in 1930 and, until her death, a member of its board of directors and chairman of its reception committee; a past president of the Wyckoff Woman’s Club; an active member of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and for many years before that of the Baptist church at East Orange, her mother’s denomination.

Harry died at the house on 18 December 1930, and she was a widow for thirteen months. In January 1932 her youngest son came up from Wilmington for a weekend; three weeks after that she went into the Paterson General Hospital with a short illness and died there at a quarter to eleven on the night of Sunday 31 January. The service was held the following Wednesday afternoon at the house on Wyckoff Avenue, taken by the curate of St. Paul’s, Paterson, and the Paterson Woman’s Club postponed its regular meeting and went to the funeral in a body. She left a daughter and two sons, and two sisters at East Orange — Mrs. Margaret Jordan and Miss Sarah Chalmers. Where she lies is not known; very likely it is beside Harry at Cedar Lawn.

John G. Roush (1876 Mason Co. WV – 1908)

Ahnentafel #30 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John G. Roush was born in September, in 1876 or in 1877 — his gravestone and his birth entry disagree by a year, and the family never settled it — the third son of Moses B. Roush, a Union veteran, and Sarah Duckworth, the daughter of a Lancashire collier. In 1880 he was down as a two-year-old in his parents’ house in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio. He was three when his mother died, and his father took the four children across the river into Mason County, West Virginia.

On 22 February 1897 he took out a licence at the Mason County courthouse to marry Bertha Ellen Nease. He gave his age as twenty and hers as eighteen; the man who swore the information was her own father, L. J. Nease. They were married two days later at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven by the Rev. D. W. Files. By June 1900 the young family were at Hartford village in Graham District, a few doors from his father Moses, with two small daughters, Nola and a baby.

He was not only a miner — he had been in the insurance business at Mason — but about September 1907 he moved his family to Broad Run to work in Claude Ryan’s mine below Hartford. A year later, at about seven on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, Bertha had been baking and a gallon oil can stood near the hot stove. He set a miner’s lamp on the table where a daughter was ironing, and in trying to fill the lamp from the can touched off an explosion that threw burning oil in every direction. The girl was enveloped in flames and died within a few hours; a second daughter died five days later at New Haven; the house burned with everything in it. His fellow workmen gave up a day’s wages for the family and the mine operator gave fifteen dollars.

He was badly burned, worst about the legs, and nobody expected him to live. He died on 21 October 1908 in Graham District of the effects of the burns, certified by Dr. D. A. Sayre — a married coal miner of thirty-two. The Weekly Register gave him a paragraph two weeks afterwards: “John D. Roush, who was injured several weeks ago at Broad Run, near Hartford, by the explosion of a lamp, died last week from injuries received at that time.” It got his initial wrong, and it said he left “a wife and two children”; in fact three daughters survived him and a fourth was born after his death. In May 1909 his widow qualified in the Mason County court as guardian of “Iva Roush, Florence Roush, Velm Roush and Della Roush, infant children of John G. Roush, deceased,” on a $500 bond, with his father Moses standing surety.

He lies at Broad Run Cemetery under a low granite marker that says only FATHER, and the years 1876 - 1908.

Bertha Ellen Nease (b. 1878 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #31 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Bertha Ellen Nease was born on 12 January 1878 in Mason County, West Virginia, the eldest child of Lewis J. Nease, a farmer, and Martha Jane Love, who had married the April before.

On 22 February 1897, at eighteen, she took out a marriage licence with John G. Roush at the Mason County courthouse, her father giving the clerk her age and birthplace, and two days later they were married at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven. She was marrying back into her own grandmother’s family: her father’s mother had been born a Roush.

By 1900 the couple were keeping house at Hartford village with two small daughters, a few doors from John’s father. In 1907 they moved to Broad Run so that John could go into the mine. On the evening of 12 September 1908 Bertha had been baking when the oil can by the stove took fire; she fought the flames and was burned about the hands and arms. Her eldest daughter died that night, a second five days later, and her husband on 21 October. The house, the furniture and the family’s clothes were all gone, and she was carrying another child.

She did not go back to either set of grandparents. In May 1909 she went to the Mason County court and qualified as guardian of her four daughters on a $500 bond, her father-in-law Moses Roush standing surety. In April 1910 she was the head of her own household at Wagner, Mason County — a widow of thirty-three with Iva Mae, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia under her roof. On 2 March 1914 she married William L. Knopp at Mason City, he thirty-two and she thirty-four, by the Rev. C. H. Cox of the Church of the United Brethren in Christ. In March 1947, when she was ill at Mason, two of her daughters travelled down from Akron and Indiana to see her.

Bertha Ellen Knopp died on 5 April 1950 at St. Joseph’s Hospital, Parkersburg, aged seventy-one, her home still at Mason, West Virginia. Her daughter Iva buried her at Broad Run on 7 April, under a stone cut MOTHER / BERTHA E. ROUSH KNOPP / 1878 - 1950: she carried both her husbands’ names to the grave.

John T. Bell (1841 – 1915 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #32 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John T. Bell was born on 10 June 1841 in Barbour County, Alabama, eleven years after his parents came down from South Carolina. As his obituary put it, “his parents moved to Alabama from Darlington, S. C., about 1830, and settled in the neighborhood where he lived at his death” — and he never left that neighbourhood in seventy-four years. He married Sarah Ann “Sallie” Cox in March 1861, weeks before the war, and they raised eleven children, of whom eight outlived him.

He farmed, and he prospered, on a place six miles south of Clayton that was known on the map simply as Bell’s. On 8 July 1867, with the Reconstruction acts new on the statute book, he came in to Precinct No. 11 and registered as a qualified voter, and the clerk wrote him down White. In April 1888 he and Sallie sold a right of way across their land to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway. He was a Freemason of thirty-two years’ standing, raised to the sublime degree of Master Mason in Royal White Hart Lodge No. 10 on 16 March 1883; when the lodge met a fortnight after his death to pass resolutions of respect, it regretted that the distance he lived from town and his last illness had kept him from its meetings. The town he traded in had been mapped for the insurance companies in February 1910, five years before he died: three cotton warehouses on the Public Square — J. C. Thomas’s, the Alliance’s and the Farmers Union’s — the court hall and fire department, the Fryer Hotel, the post office, and a water works and electric light plant beside the Central of Georgia tracks.

Sallie died at home in March 1910, and the county paper called him John T. Bell, Sr., for by then his eldest son was a man of standing in Clayton in his own right. By that year he was sixty-nine and living in his son Ben T.’s household, in his son Tom’s own precinct. He died at his house on 6 November 1915. His sons told the court that what he left consisted “chiefly of lands, cotton, insurance and money, all of said real and personal estate being estimated to be worth about fifty thousand dollars” — something like a million and a half in modern money, to be divided among ten children’s and grandchildren’s lines.

His will names his children in his own voice: “I give, devise and bequeath to my beloved children John T. Bell, Jr., Mack Bell, Will A. Bell, Sallie Stallings, Ed Bell, Tom Bell, Mary McPherson and Ben F. Bell.” Letters testamentary were granted on 19 November 1915 by Thomas D. Grubbs, judge of probate, to four of those sons — John T. Jr., Mack, Will A. and Ben F. — who advertised for claims against the estate in the Clayton Record three weeks running that winter. The eldest, John T. Bell Jr., became an undertaker at Clayton and buried a good many of his neighbours, including his own uncle William nine years later. The Tom of that list is Thomas Jefferson Bell, and he is the step in Alec’s own line; he died six years after his father, leaving nothing at all to administer.

He lies at Bethlehem Baptist churchyard inside a low wrought-iron rail, under a marble shaft whose pediment is carved with the square and compasses of the Freemasons. Sallie’s stone stands a few feet from his across the same plot.

Sarah Ann Cox (1841 – 1910 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #33 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Ann Cox — Sallie — was born on 24 August 1841 in Barbour County, Alabama, and married John T. Bell there on 7 March 1861. He was twenty, she was nineteen, and the war began the month after the wedding. Two of the thirteen people who had founded Bethlehem Baptist Church up the road in April 1824 were a William and an Elizabeth Cox, though whether they were hers nobody knows.

She had eleven children and raised them on the farm known as Bell’s, six miles south of Clayton. Eight outlived their father and were named in his will — six sons and two daughters; one of the sons was Thomas Jefferson Bell, and a daughter married in 1886 was named Sallie for her. In April 1888 she put her name beside her husband’s on the deed selling a right of way to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway, giving up her dower in some forty-six acres of it as a wife had to do. She signed it Sarah A Bell.

Her photograph survives, and it is the oldest likeness of anyone on the Bell side of this family: an oval vignette of an old woman with white hair swept up and a lace jabot at the throat of a dark dress, on an embossed cream cabinet-card mount that somebody in the family labelled in blue ink — “Mrs. John T. Bell. Mother of Sallie Bell Stallings.”

She died on the morning of Friday 11 March 1910, five years before John, at home, after what the county paper called a long and “linquering” illness. Under the headline AGED LADY DIES NEAR CLAYTON it called her “one of the most estimable and widely known women in the county … a lady who has raised a large family and maintained the high respect and goodwill of all who knew her,” and said she was survived by her husband, many children, grandchildren and some great-grandchildren. The funeral was preached at Bethlehem church by her former pastor, the Rev. W. R. Bickerstaff of Florala, and she was buried in the adjoining cemetery. Her headstone stands beside her husband’s inside the Bells’ iron-railed plot — a white marble tablet under a carved egg-and-dart band — and it gives her no surname at all: SALLIE A., WIFE OF J. T. BELL.

Henry Bradley (1836 – 1887 Clayton AL)

Ahnentafel #34 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Henry Bradley was born in Alabama about 1836, of South Carolina parents, and spent his life in Barbour County. He fought for the Confederacy and carried a wound out of the war that would kill him twenty-odd years later. On 8 July 1867, in the first registration taken in Alabama under the Reconstruction acts, he signed the Board of Registration’s return of qualified voters for Precinct No. 9 — line 97, “Bradly Henry” — on a page where his neighbours are set down White and Colored alternately down the column, and a tick against his name says he came back and used the vote. A Bell and a Cox registered at the same table: the two families his daughters would marry into were in the same room.

That December he married. Barbour County issued a licence to “Henry Bradly and Elvira Loveless, both of full age” on 18 December 1867, and the judge of probate signed the authorisation the following day. He was thirty-one; she was eighteen. By June 1880 there were five children in the house at Clayton — Ida J. nine, Anna V. seven, Henry R. five, Alice three, and Katie seven months old — and he was farming then as well as keeping the court’s books.

He was also clerk of the circuit court of Barbour County, and he died in office. On Monday 21 February 1887 erysipelas set into the old war wound; congestion of the lungs, stomach and bowels followed, and he died at his home in Clayton at one o’clock on the morning of Sunday 27 February, aged fifty. He was buried at Clayton at ten the next morning with Masonic honours. He had left his family well provided for by the standards of the place and the time — nine thousand dollars of life insurance in all: two thousand with the Knights of Honor, two thousand with the A.O.U.W. and five thousand with the New York Life. Petitions for a successor were circulating within two days of the funeral, four men applied for the unexpired term, and in the second week of March the governor appointed Capt. John C. McNab to the clerkship.

Four years later his widow died too, and in the autumn of 1891 the county took his two youngest, Alice at fourteen and Katie at twelve, into guardianship. In the summer of 1893 a Eufaula weekly printed, four times over, the Barbour County probate court’s settlement of that guardianship, and with it the names of all five of his surviving children — the account of the guardian of “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased, to-wit: Ida Parker, wife of Lee Parker; Annie Fortner, wife of W. J. Fortner; Henry Bradley, Alice Bradley and Katie Bradley.” Five children, two of them married off already, two of them still wards of the court.

He lies at Clayton City Cemetery, inside a rusting iron rail in the old part of the burying ground, under a draped marble tablet carved with swags and a wreath and with the square and compasses of the Freemasons cut into its arch. It reads HENRY BRADLEY, BORN APR. 25, 1836, DIED FEB. 27, 1887, though the day-figures are worn now past certainty. Beside him, under the same rail, stands a second stone: ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, who outlived him by four years. A draped monument with a Masonic emblem inside its own iron enclosure is not a poor man’s grave.
