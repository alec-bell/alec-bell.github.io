John G. Roush (1876 Mason Co. WV – 1908)

Ahnentafel #30 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John G. Roush was born in the autumn of 1876, the third son of Moses B. Roush and Sarah Duckworth. The 1900 census puts his birth in September 1876 in West Virginia; a Meigs County, Ohio birth register records a son John born to Moses Roush and Sarah Duckworth on 20 September 1877, and the family was certainly in Sutton Township, Ohio when the census taker called in 1880 and wrote him down as two years old. He was three when his mother died, and his father took the four children across the river into Mason County.

On 22 February 1897 he took out a licence at the Mason County courthouse to marry Bertha Ellen Nease. He gave his age as twenty and hers as eighteen; the party who swore the information was her own father, L. J. Nease. They were married two days later at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven by the Rev. D. W. Files. In June 1900 the young family were at Hartford village in Graham District, five lines down the same census page from his father Moses.

He was not only a miner. He had been in the insurance business at Mason, and moved his family to Broad Run about September 1907 to work in Claude Ryan's mine below Hartford. A year later, at about seven on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, his wife had been baking and a gallon oil can stood near the hot stove. He set a miner's lamp on the table where a daughter was ironing, and in trying to fill the lamp from the can touched off an explosion that threw burning oil in every direction. The girl was enveloped in flames and died within a few hours; a second daughter died five days later at New Haven; the house burned with everything in it. His fellow workmen gave up a day's wages for the family and the mine operator gave fifteen dollars.

Two weeks after his death the Weekly Register added that he left "a wife and two children" — a miscount: three daughters survived him and a fourth was born after his death.

He left four small daughters. In May 1909 his widow Bertha qualified in the Mason County court as guardian of "Iva Roush, Florence Roush, Velm Roush and Della Roush, infant children of John G. Roush, deceased," on a $500 bond with Moses Roush as surety.

He was badly burned, especially about the legs, and the newspaper doubted he would live. He died on 21 October 1908 in Graham District - “effects of burns,” certified by Dr. D. A. Sayre - a married coal miner of thirty-two. Two weeks later the Weekly Register noted it in a paragraph: “John D. Roush, who was injured several weeks ago at Broad Run, near Hartford, by the explosion of a lamp, died last week from injuries received at that time.” He lies at Broad Run Cemetery under a low granite marker that says only FATHER, and the years 1876 - 1908.

Bertha Ellen Nease (b. 1878 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #31 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Bertha Ellen Nease was born on 12 January 1878 in Mason County, West Virginia, the eldest child of Lewis J. Nease, a farmer, and Martha Jane Love, who had married the April before. Her father reported the birth himself.

On 22 February 1897, at eighteen, she took out a marriage licence with John G. Roush at the Mason County courthouse - her father stood as the informant who gave the clerk her age and birthplace - and two days later they were married at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven. In 1897 she was marrying back into her own grandmother's family: her father's mother had been born a Roush.

By 1900 the couple were at Hartford village with two small daughters, living a few doors from John's father. In 1907 they moved to Broad Run so that John could go into the mine. On the evening of 12 September 1908 Bertha had been baking when the oil can by the stove took fire; she fought the flames and was burned about the hands and arms. Her eldest daughter died that night, a second five days later, and her husband on 21 October. The house, the furniture and the family's clothes were all gone, and she was carrying another child.

She did not go back to either set of grandparents. In April 1910 she was the head of her own household at Wagner, Mason County - a widow of thirty-three with Iva Mae, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia under her roof. On 2 March 1914 she married William L. Knopp at Mason City, he thirty-two and she thirty-four, by the Rev. C. H. Cox of the Church of the United Brethren in Christ. In March 1947, when she was ill at Mason, two of her daughters travelled down from Akron and Indiana to see her.

The county paper printed the licence among the week's news: "Marriage licenses issued yesterday: Andrew C. Grimm and Desta D. Clark, John Roush and Bertha E. Nease."

Widowed with four daughters under ten, she went to the Mason County court in May 1909 and qualified as their guardian on a $500 bond, her father-in-law Moses Roush standing surety.

Bertha Ellen Knopp died on 5 April 1950 at St. Joseph's Hospital, Parkersburg, aged seventy-one, her usual residence still Mason, West Virginia. Her daughter Iva gave the information for the certificate, which names her parents as Lewis Nease and Martha Jane Love. She was buried at Broad Run on 7 April, under a stone cut MOTHER / BERTHA E. ROUSH KNOPP / 1878 - 1950: she carried both her husbands' names to the grave.

John T. Bell (1841 – 1915 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #32 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John T. Bell was born on 10 June 1841 and died on 6 November 1915 near Louisville, in Barbour County, Alabama — his death recorded not in a newspaper but in the petition his sons filed two days later, which says he died “at his house in Barbour Co. on or about the 6th day of November 1915.” He married Sarah Ann “Sallie” Cox in March 1861, weeks before the war, and they raised eleven children of whom eight outlived him.

His obituary ran in the Montgomery Advertiser at the end of that November and again, unchanged, in the hometown Clayton Record a few days later. It gives his birthday to the day, names all eight surviving children and two brothers, W. W. and C. A. Bell — and then says the sentence the rest of this family history hangs from: “His parents moved to Alabama from Darlington, S. C., about 1830, and settled in the neighborhood where he lived at his death.” He was born in Alabama eleven years after that move. The journey was his father and mother’s, not his, and Darlington District is where everything above them has to be looked for.

He farmed, and he prospered, on a place six miles south of Clayton that was known on the map simply as Bell’s. In April 1888 he and Sallie sold a right of way across their land to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway. When Sallie died in 1910 the county paper called him John T. Bell, Sr. — five years before the will, and a useful sign that the two Johns were already being told apart in print. By 1910 he was sixty-nine and living in his son Ben T.’s household, in his son Tom’s own precinct. When he died his executors told the court that the estate consisted “chiefly of lands, cotton, insurance and money, all of said real and personal estate being estimated to be worth about fifty thousand dollars” — something like a million and a half in modern money, to be divided among ten children and grandchildren’s lines.

His will names those children in his own voice, and the first of them carried his name: John T. Bell Jr., who became an undertaker at Clayton and who, nine years after his father’s death, paid for the burial of his uncle William. For a long time this file could not tell the two Johns apart, and the confusion sat on the chart as a warning that would not go away. It was the record that settled it, not the argument: the 1925 clerk wrote the word Junior, and the 1929 bond that closed the son’s own estate wrote it again.

Among his children was Tom — Thomas Jefferson Bell — and that is the step in Alec’s own line. Tom died six years after his father, leaving nothing at all to administer.

The county town he traded in was mapped for the insurance companies in February 1910, five years before he died. Three cotton warehouses stood on the Public Square — J. C. Thomas's, the Alliance's and the Farmers Union's — with the court hall and fire department, the Fryer Hotel, the post office and a town water works and electric light plant beside the Central of Georgia tracks.

He was a Freemason for thirty-two years, raised to the sublime degree of Master Mason in Royal White Hart Lodge No. 10 on 16 March 1883. The lodge met and passed resolutions of respect a fortnight after his death, regretting that the distance he lived from town and his last illness had kept him from its meetings. Letters testamentary on his will were granted on 19 November 1915 by Thomas D. Grubbs, judge of probate, to four of his sons - John T. Bell Jr., Mack Bell, Will A. Bell and Ben F. Bell - who advertised for claims against the estate in the Clayton Record that winter.

He lies at Bethlehem Baptist churchyard inside a low wrought-iron rail, under a marble shaft whose pediment is carved with the square and compasses of the Freemasons. Sallie’s stone stands a few feet from his across the same plot.

Sarah Ann “Sallie” Cox (1841 – 1910 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #33 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Ann Cox — Sallie — was born on 24 August 1841 and married John T. Bell on 7 March 1861 in Barbour County, Alabama. He was twenty, she was nineteen; the war began the following month.

They had eleven children, of whom eight outlived their father and were named in his will — six sons and two daughters. One of the sons was Thomas Jefferson Bell. A daughter married in 1886 was named Sallie for her. The 1880 census of Louisville is the one record that states it plainly: Thomas J. Bell, aged about four, with his parents named on the same lines, Sarah and John T.

In April 1888 she signed her name beside her husband’s on a deed selling a right of way to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway, giving up her dower in about forty-six acres of it. She signed it Sarah A Bell. A wife joined her husband’s deed in order to release dower, and that formality is the reason her name is on the paper at all.

Her photograph survives, and it is the oldest likeness of anyone on the Bell side: an oval vignette portrait of an old woman with white hair swept up, a lace jabot at the throat of a dark dress, on an embossed cream mount that somebody in the family has labelled in blue ink “Mrs. John T. Bell. Mother of Sallie Bell Stallings.”

She died on the morning of Friday 11 March 1910, five years before John, at the home that was known on the map simply as Bell’s, six miles south of Clayton, after what the county paper called a long and lingering illness. It called her “one of the most estimable and widely known women in the county … a lady who has raised a large family and maintained the high respect and goodwill of all who knew her,” and recorded that she was survived by her husband, many children, grandchildren and some great-grandchildren. The funeral was preached at Bethlehem church by her former pastor, the Rev. W. R. Bickerstaff of Florala, and she was buried in the adjoining cemetery. By the time John’s will was probated no widow was named anywhere in it. Her headstone stands beside his inside the Bells’ iron-railed plot at Bethlehem Baptist churchyard, a white marble tablet under a carved egg-and-dart band, and it does not give her a surname at all: SALLIE A., WIFE OF J. T. BELL.

Henry Bradley (1836 – 1887 Clayton AL)

Ahnentafel #34 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Henry Bradley lived in Barbour County, Alabama, and was dead before October 1891 — the month a county court appointed a guardian for his daughters Alice, fourteen, and Katie, twelve, recording that the minors had no father living. He is named as their father on both girls’ own death records, thirty-two years apart, one clerk writing him Henery; and that, with the silence in the guardianship, is all this family’s own papers hold of him.

In the summer of 1893 a Eufaula weekly printed, four times over, a notice from the Barbour County probate court that hands him back most of a family. His children’s guardian, a Bradley woman, had herself died, and her administratrix was settling the account of her guardianship of “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased, to-wit: Ida Parker, wife of Lee Parker; Annie Fortner, wife of W. J. Fortner; Henry Bradley, Alice Bradley and Katie Bradley.” Five children, two of them married off already, two of them still the wards the court had taken in two years before — and the phrase that matters, deceased, printed in his own county six years after he died.

He lies at Clayton City Cemetery, a few miles up the road from the farm, inside a rusting iron rail in the old part of the burying ground. His stone is a draped marble tablet carved with swags and a wreath, with the square and compasses of the Freemasons cut into its arch; the inscription reads HENRY BRADLEY, BORN APR. 25, 1836, DIED FEB. 27, 1887, though the figures are worn and those days of the month are the cemetery record’s reading rather than anything that can now be made out with certainty. He was fifty, and he died at Louisville — the Bells’ own village. Beside him, under the same rail, stands a second stone: ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, who outlived him by four years.

He was clerk of the circuit court of Barbour County, Alabama, and he died in office. He was a Confederate soldier, and the wound he took in the war eventually killed him: on Monday 21 February 1887 erysipelas set into the old wound, congestion of the lungs, stomach and bowels followed, and he died at his home in Clayton at one o'clock on the morning of Sunday 27 February 1887. He was buried at Clayton at ten the next morning with Masonic honours. He left his family well provided for by the standards of the place and time - nine thousand dollars of life insurance, two thousand in the Knights of Honor, two thousand in the A.O.U.W. and five thousand in the New York Life. Petitions for a successor were circulating within two days of his funeral, and four men applied for the unexpired term before the governor appointed Capt. John C. McNab to the office in the second week of March.

He married Elvira Loveless on a licence issued at Barbour County on 18 December 1867, the probate judge signing the authorisation the following day. He was thirty-one; she was eighteen. In June 1880 the census found the family at Clayton, Beat 7, Barbour County: Henry Bradley, 44, farmer, born Alabama, both his parents born in South Carolina; Elvira, 31, keeping house, both her parents born in Alabama; and five children -- Ida J. 9, Anna V. 7, Henry R. 5, Alice 3, and Katie, seven months old.

He very probably appears, white and of age, on the Board of Registration's “Return of Qualified Voters” for Precinct No. 9 of Barbour County in July 1867 — line 97, “Bradly Henry”, oath book 5, page 468 — among the first voter rolls taken in Alabama under the Reconstruction acts, on a page where his neighbours are listed White and Colored alternately down the column. A tick in the ‘Check when Voting’ column says he came back and used it. The same precinct return carries a Bell and a Cox: the two families his daughters would marry into were registering at the same table.

So Alice was ten when he died, and the county took charge of her four years later, after her mother had gone too. And the grave argues with the way this family history has described him. A draped marble monument with a Masonic emblem, standing inside its own iron enclosure, is not a poor man’s grave.

Elvira Loveless (fl. 1876–1891 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #35 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Her name was Elvira, and that much is cut in marble. She married Henry Bradley in Barbour County, Alabama, by about 1871, when their eldest daughter Ida was born; no marriage return has been found, and she had at least five children who grew up and three who did not. A court notice printed in the Eufaula paper in 1893 names the survivors: Ida, married to Lee Parker; Annie, married to W. J. Fortner; a son Henry; and the two youngest, Alice, born 8 October 1876, and Katie, born in 1879.

She lies at Clayton City Cemetery, beside her husband and inside the same iron rail. Her headstone is a draped marble tablet with a star cut into the arch and a worn verse on the plinth, and it gives her no surname at all — only ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, born in August 1849 and dead in June 1891, the day-figures too worn now to be certain of. That last date answers a question this family could not: she was dead four months before the October 1891 order that made Alice and Katie wards of the Barbour County court. The order speaks only of the father because that is the form of words the clerk used. The girls had lost both parents.

She was forty-one, and she had buried at least three of her children already — one at birth in 1870, a boy of eleven in 1879, and a baby in 1884.

In January 1890, three years a widow, she bought Jake Fortner's house at Mt. Andrew and moved the family there; her daughter Annie was married in that house the following September. She died at Clayton on the afternoon of Sunday 14 June 1891, of cholera morbus, a few hours after dressing her children for Sunday school and sending for the doctor too late. She left five children, the youngest of whom were made wards of the Barbour County court that autumn.

Barbour County, Alabama issued a marriage licence to “Henry Bradly and Elvira Loveless, both of full age” on 18 December 1867, and the Judge of Probate, J. S. Williams, signed the authorisation to marry them the next day. She was eighteen. The minister never sent the licence back: the printed return underneath — ‘I hereby certify that the above named persons were duly married by me on the ___ day of ___’ — was left blank, so the county book records the authority to marry them and never the wedding itself.

Loveless is how this family has her, and it is not yet proved. It comes from the index entries of her two youngest daughters’ death records, half a lifetime apart — Elveria Loveless on Katie’s in 1924, Elvira Loveless on Alice’s in 1956 — and the screenshot taken of them turned out to be a different document altogether, so neither has been checked against the record itself. Two informants a generation apart agreeing on so unusual a name is worth a good deal. It is not the same as a marriage return, and the marriage return has not been found.

Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins (~1838 NC – 1902 MS)

Ahnentafel #36 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph William Eakins — “Joel W.” on most of the paper and “William J.” on some of it — was born in December 1838 or 1839 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, a son of John Eakins Sr. and Nancy Costin. He is the “Jos., 12” enumerated in his father's house on the Moores Creek land in 1850, one of nine children there.

By the end of the 1850s the family's reach had extended to Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where an uncle, William Eakins, had already settled, and where four orphaned Eakens boys born in North Carolina were being raised in the household of William Payne. On 13 May 1861, a month after the war began, Joel married Payne's daughter Sarah Hudson Payne. The marriage happened inside a house that had already fused the two families for a decade.

Two of his North Carolina kin went the other way that same May: his brother John J. B. Eakins and his cousin Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in the Moore's Creek Rifle Guards, Company E of the 18th North Carolina, and both were dead within two years — John at Sharpsburg, Owen at Chancellorsville.

He and Sarah farmed in Lauderdale County and had ten or more children. The 1880 census lists Mary A., Ada J., Jno. N., Minnie, Cassie E., Orgun, Joel P., Nancy and the newborn Andrew J.; a son Sam follows by 1900. Two of those names, Orgun and Joel, are Sarah's own younger brothers' names, carried over from the Payne household she had grown up in. In 1900 the enumerator wrote him down as “William J Eakins, born December 1838, North Carolina, married 1861.”

He died in 1902 in Mississippi. His other lasting mark on the family was a piece of handwriting: he stopped dotting the “i” in Eakins, and his descendants have been Eakens, and then Eaken, ever since.
