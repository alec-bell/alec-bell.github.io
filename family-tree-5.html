Elvira Loveless (fl. 1876–1891 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #35 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Her name was Elvira, and that much is cut in marble. She married Henry Bradley in Barbour County, Alabama, by about 1871, when their eldest daughter Ida was born; no marriage return has been found, and she had at least five children who grew up and three who did not. A court notice printed in the Eufaula paper in 1893 names the survivors: Ida, married to Lee Parker; Annie, married to W. J. Fortner; a son Henry; and the two youngest, Alice, born 8 October 1876, and Katie, born in 1879.

She lies at Clayton City Cemetery, beside her husband and inside the same iron rail. Her headstone is a draped marble tablet with a star cut into the arch and a worn verse on the plinth, and it gives her no surname at all — only ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, born in August 1849 and dead in June 1891, the day-figures too worn now to be certain of. That last date answers a question this family could not: she was dead four months before the October 1891 order that made Alice and Katie wards of the Barbour County court. The order speaks only of the father because that is the form of words the clerk used. The girls had lost both parents.

She was forty-one, and she had buried at least three of her children already — one at birth in 1870, a boy of eleven in 1879, and a baby in 1884.

In January 1890, three years a widow, she bought Jake Fortner's house at Mt. Andrew and moved the family there; her daughter Annie was married in that house the following September. She died at Clayton on the afternoon of Sunday 14 June 1891, of cholera morbus, a few hours after dressing her children for Sunday school and sending for the doctor too late. She left five children, the youngest of whom were made wards of the Barbour County court that autumn.

Loveless is how this family has her, and it is not yet proved. It comes from the index entries of her two youngest daughters’ death records, half a lifetime apart — Elveria Loveless on Katie’s in 1924, Elvira Loveless on Alice’s in 1956 — and the screenshot taken of them turned out to be a different document altogether, so neither has been checked against the record itself. Two informants a generation apart agreeing on so unusual a name is worth a good deal. It is not the same as a marriage return, and the marriage return has not been found.

Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins (~1838 NC – 1902 MS)

Ahnentafel #36 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph William Eakins — “Joel W.” on most of the paper and “William J.” on some of it — was born in December 1838 or 1839 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, a son of John Eakins Sr. and Nancy Costin. He is the “Jos., 12” enumerated in his father's house on the Moores Creek land in 1850, one of nine children there.

By the end of the 1850s the family's reach had extended to Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where an uncle, William Eakins, had already settled, and where four orphaned Eakens boys born in North Carolina were being raised in the household of William Payne. On 13 May 1861, a month after the war began, Joel married Payne's daughter Sarah Hudson Payne. The marriage happened inside a house that had already fused the two families for a decade.

Two of his North Carolina kin went the other way that same May: his brother John J. B. Eakins and his cousin Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in the Moore's Creek Rifle Guards, Company E of the 18th North Carolina, and both were dead within two years — John at Sharpsburg, Owen at Chancellorsville.

He and Sarah farmed in Lauderdale County and had ten or more children. The 1880 census lists Mary A., Ada J., Jno. N., Minnie, Cassie E., Orgun, Joel P., Nancy and the newborn Andrew J.; a son Sam follows by 1900. Two of those names, Orgun and Joel, are Sarah's own younger brothers' names, carried over from the Payne household she had grown up in. In 1900 the enumerator wrote him down as “William J Eakins, born December 1838, North Carolina, married 1861.”

He died in 1902 in Mississippi. His other lasting mark on the family was a piece of handwriting: he stopped dotting the “i” in Eakins, and his descendants have been Eakens, and then Eaken, ever since.

Sarah Hudson Payne (1842 MS – 1908)

Ahnentafel #37 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Hudson Payne was born in Mississippi in 1842 — in May 1842, according to the 1900 census, which asked. She first appears aged eight, in the 1850 census of Lauderdale County, in a crowded house: William Payne, fifty-nine, born in Virginia; Mary, thirty, born in North Carolina; four boys born in North Carolina carrying the Eakens name, aged ten to sixteen, who were orphans being raised there; and four younger children born in Mississippi, of whom Sarah was the eldest. Two of the little ones were called Origon and Joel.

Whether William Payne was her father or her stepfather is the one thing about her childhood the records have not settled. What is certain is the household — and what came of it. On 13 May 1861 she married Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins, of the very family whose orphaned boys had grown up under that roof.

They farmed in Lauderdale County for forty years. She bore ten or more children: Mary A., Ada J., John N., Minnie, Cassie, Nancy, a Sam, and two she named Orgun and Joel after the brothers she had been raised with. Her youngest, Andrew Jackson Eaken, born in 1880 or 1881, is the one through whom the line runs down to Alec.

Her stone survives, leaning in a fenced Mississippi burying ground, and it gives her life to the day: SARAH H. EAKINS, born May 2, 1842, died May 1, 1908, aged 65 years 11 months and 29 days — one day short of her sixty-sixth birthday. Her middle name, Hudson, has never been explained, and no record yet found says who her mother was.

Joseph L. Swain (1861 MS – 1940 York AL)

Ahnentafel #38 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lee Swain was born on 23 December 1861, the eldest child of Joseph Tolbert Swain and Nancy E. Jane, who had married in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, the year before. He was four or five when his parents crossed the state line into Sumter County, Alabama in the first months after the war, and he grew up at Cuba; the 1880 census finds him there, nineteen, at the head of a row of eight younger brothers and sisters.

He married twice. On 10 February 1884 the Sumter County register has him marrying a Eugenia Simmons, with J. M. Patton the minister — and the couple would name a son for him. He had an earlier wife named Leila Eugenia whose stone, in the churchyard at Salem Baptist across the line in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, gives 1861–1885 and calls her “wife of J. L. Swain”: she died before the end of 1885. On 23 December 1885, his own twenty-fourth birthday, he married the woman the register enters as Rebecca A. D. Simmons and everyone else called Dillon. The minister that day was D. C. Langford, and they named a son for him too: Joseph Langford Swain.

They farmed at York and Cuba. The 1900 census of Election Precinct 6 lists the children at home as Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford, and gives his years married as sixteen — counting, it seems, from the first wedding rather than the second. He stood for public office: at a meeting of the voters of the third commissioners district at Livingston on 28 August 1911 the vote went fifty-seven to twenty-seven in his favour, and in September he announced himself from Cuba as a candidate for county commissioner, “subject to the action of the Democratic party.” The Livingston paper thought he would make a good commissioner.

The family burying ground at Salem Baptist holds a hard chapter of their life. Its roster records five of their children there: Marvin, dead at two in 1892; Fletcher, dead at two in 1905; and then Patton, dead at nineteen in January 1916, Lucille Kirkland, dead at eighteen in January 1917, and Myrtle, dead at sixteen the following month. Three grown children in thirteen months.

Cuba was drawn out building by building on 4 May 1928 by L. L. Parker for the state rating bureau — the Walker-Shaw planing mill and saw mill at one end, a grist mill and feed store, G. W. McElroy's blacksmith and carpenter shop, the Cuba Mercantile Co., the Southern Railway depot with its covered platform, a Methodist church and two Holiness churches.

He lived out his life on the land three miles west of York, near the old McConnell Station — a six-room house on twenty acres, with running water and a telephone. He died there at a quarter to one on the afternoon of Thursday 21 November 1940, aged seventy-nine, after a long illness, and the funeral was held in the house the next afternoon. The county paper listed his widow, “Mrs. Dillon Simmons Swain,” four daughters — Leila Collier of Meridian, Maggie Bloebaum of Wheaton, Illinois, Pinkie McElroy of Fort Knox and Merle Cobb of Geiger — one son, Langford of Cuba, a brother and two sisters. He was buried at Salem Baptist. His stone gives his name as JOE L. SWAIN and, under the dates, one word: PAPA.

Dillon Simmons (1863 Sumter AL – 1952 Sumter AL)

Ahnentafel #39 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Dillon Simmons was born on 1 December 1863 in Sumter County, Alabama, a daughter of Ben and Adeline Simmons of Cuba. She appears in her father's household in 1880, aged sixteen, written down by the enumerator as “Dellon” and stated to be his daughter.

She married Joseph L. Swain of the same small village on 23 December 1885, the county register entering her as Rebecca A. D. Simmons. He had been married before: an earlier wife, Leila Eugenia, had died by the end of that year. The Swains farmed at York and Cuba, three miles out near the old McConnell Station, in a six-room house on twenty acres. The 1900 census lists her simply as “Dillon Swain, 36,” with six children at home: Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford.

Five of her children lie in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, across the state line in Lauderdale County, Mississippi: two who died as infants, and Patton, Lucille Kirkland and Myrtle, who died grown within thirteen months of one another in 1916 and 1917.

Her daughter Maggie Dillon Swain carried her name forward, and it travelled two more generations as a middle name nobody could explain. The documents give it back in her own lifetime. Her husband's obituary of November 1940 calls her, in print, “his wife, Mrs. Dillon Simmons Swain.” In May 1951 the county paper reported her Wheaton daughter coming down to visit “her mother, Mrs. Dillon Swain.” And her son Joseph Langford Swain's death certificate, issued at New Orleans in 1946, names his mother as Dillon Simmons.

She outlived her husband by twelve years and died on 1 November 1952, aged eighty-eight. She left a will: on 6 February 1953 Judge Wilbur E. Dearman of the Sumter County probate court granted letters testamentary on it to three of her daughters — Maggie S. Bloebaum, Pinkie S. McElroy and Merle S. Cobb — and they published the notice to creditors through that February and March. She lies beside her husband at Salem Baptist under a matching stone: DILLON S. SWAIN, DEC. 1, 1863 – NOV. 1, 1952, and beneath the dates, answering his PAPA, the single word MAMA.

She died in the first days of November 1952; her four daughters thanked their neighbours in the Sumter County Journal a fortnight later, calling her 'our precious mother'.

One question about her is still open. The register records Joseph L. Swain marrying a Eugenia Simmons in February 1884 and this woman in December 1885, and the two brides have long been supposed sisters — but no document yet seen says so, and none puts “Rebecca A. D.” and “Dillon” on the same woman either. The research keeps them apart.

Caswell Massey Stephens was born on 8 January 1852 in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of Henry H. Stephens and Sarah Ann Jones. He was named, at the very least, for a neighbour: in 1850 a twenty-four-year-old North Carolina man called Caswell Massey farmed the dwelling that stood between the Stephens house and the Joneses’. Whether he was kin to either family nobody has yet established. His mother died before he was three, and his father married her sister Elizabeth in October 1854. By 1860 the family had moved to Newton County, Mississippi, where the census catches him at eight.

On the last day of 1869, not yet eighteen, he stood in the Sumter County probate court before Judge J. A. Abrahams, said he was over fourteen, and chose his own father as his guardian — he and his brothers Calvin and John were the “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” their mother’s father, and his bond was set at $800. There was enough property in it that his father was filing annual settlements two years later.

In October 1871, aged nineteen, he married a widow thirteen years his senior. The register enters her as “Mrs. A. M. James,” and the officiant was H. H. Stephens, Minister — his own father. Her gravestone never gives her a first name either: it calls her A. M., wife of C. M. Stephens. Their children included Martha Alice, born 14 April 1874, who married John William Knight, and John Willis, born 12 March 1876; a son Rufus L. belongs to one marriage or the other. She died on 8 January 1891 — his thirty-ninth birthday — and on 19 April that year he married again, near Cuba. The county paper gave the whole of it in eleven words: “Married — Near Cuba, on the 19th, Mr C M Stephens and Mrs I Dearman.” The bride was Isabella, a Britt by birth and a Dearman by her first marriage, and she was about forty-five.

He farmed on into old age at Halsell, in Choctaw County, just over the Sumter line. In May 1928 his son Rufus died intestate at Livingston leaving about a thousand dollars; the probate papers record the only heir as “The Father Caswell M Stephens of Livingston Ala., age 76 years,” and he declined to administer it. He died at eight o’clock on the morning of Sunday 10 February 1935 — the county paper of the 14th already reports him buried, though his stone was later cut with the 17th —, at the home of his daughter Mrs. Alice Knight, after a short illness. The Sumter County Journal ran the notice on its front page. Funeral services were at eleven on the Monday at Siloam Church, Revs. Ezell and Dearman officiating, and he was buried in the church cemetery at Siloam, where his first wife had lain for forty-four years. He left two sons, J. W. of Livingston and H. R. of Halsell, one daughter, two brothers — Bob Stephens of Kewanee and Bill Stephens of Cuba — and thirteen grandchildren and fifteen great-grandchildren.

William Curtis Truett was born on 25 July 1867, the son of John Wesley Truett and Emily Caroline Ward. He grew up at Chilton in Clarke County, Alabama, and the 1880 census catches the whole family on one page: his father, a Confederate veteran who could not write his own name; his mother; his brothers and sister; and his grandmother Alcey, widow of the Reverend William Hampton Truett, living in the house at sixty-seven. On 17 June 1886, not yet nineteen, he married Amanda Webb in Clarke County. The 1900 census finds them at Clarksville with fourteen years of marriage behind them and six children in the house — Tela, Jane, Bigy, Lillie, Tamie and the baby Bessie — and it records both parents as able to read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do. Thomas William, the “Tamie” of that schedule, was Alec's great-grandfather.

Amanda is gone from the record after 1900, and he married again, to Zella Shewmake, born 1 June 1878. All the places of his life sit within a few miles of each other on the 1928 county map — Chilton, where he grew up; Clarksville, where the census found him in 1900; and New Prospect, where he farmed, with Harris Creek running between them.

Amanda Webb (1868 – aft. 1900 Clarke Co.)

Ahnentafel #45 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Amanda Webb was born in July 1868 in Alabama and married William Curtis Truett in Clarke County on 17 June 1886, when she was not quite eighteen and he not quite nineteen.

The 1900 census is the one full look anyone gets at her. On 19 June 1900 the enumerator James A. Coate Jr. called at dwelling 118 at Clarksville and wrote her down as Amanda, wife, born July 1868, aged thirty-one, fourteen years married, mother of six children and six living — and all six were in the house with her: Tela, thirteen; Jane, twelve; Bigy, ten; Lillie, eight; Tamie, six; and Bessie, one. Tamie is Thomas William, born that September of 1893, and Alec descends from him. Both she and her husband are marked as able to read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do. Two dwellings down the same page lived a Tom P. Webb, born September 1862, a farmer — the right surname at the right door, though nothing yet says he was her brother.

After that she is gone from the record. Her husband married again, to Zella Shewmake, and it is Zella's name, not hers, on the other half of his gravestone at New Prospect. No grave has been found for Amanda anywhere.

The Webbs were old Clarke County people: a William Webb came into the county about 1812 and died at his home there in February 1853 at about seventy-eight, the Webb mill on the Tallahatta was built about 1836, and Webbs were among the families forted up at McGrew's Fort during the Indian troubles of 1810. Whether Amanda belonged to them has not been shown.
