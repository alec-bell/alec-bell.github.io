Isabel Catlin (d. 1676 Hadley Mass.)

Ahnentafel #6165 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Isabel was married three times and outlived two husbands before she was fifty. The first was a Catlin of Wethersfield, who died by the summer of 1644 leaving her a small estate and an infant son, John. She married second James Northam of Hockanum, on the Hartford side of the river. The town of Hadley granted him a home lot, but, as a neighbour later swore, “it pleased god to take him away by death before he came,” and by December 1661 the town had granted his widow a hundred-pound allotment of her own. Her son John broke and fenced that land for her. She married third Joseph Baldwin of Milford, by then settled at Hadley on the Connecticut River, whose own first wife had died in 1661.

That third marriage closed a circle. Her son John Catlin had married Mary Baldwin, Joseph's daughter, in 1662; so her son's wife became her stepdaughter, and Mary Baldwin found herself married to the son of her own stepmother. The two families interlock twice in a single generation.

She held land at Hadley in her own right: a court pleading of 1692 names her as one of the town’s first settlers, granted an allotment rated at a hundred pounds, about 1661 as far as a torn date can be read. After her death her son John sued her third husband’s grandson for that land, naming her in one breath as “Isabel Catlin, alias Isabel Northam, alias Isabel Baldwin.” She was probably a sister of Lawrence Ward of New Haven, Branford and Newark, which is likely how the family came to be in Newark at all. Her maiden name was never written down. She died at Hadley, Massachusetts, on 5 December 1676, holding the land in her own name. Her husband Baldwin kept it, and left it to his grandson. Sixteen years later her son John, by then of Deerfield, sued for it. Old neighbours swore that she had always “owned” him as her son, and in October 1693 a jury at Boston gave him his mother’s land.

Joseph Baldwin (Milford 1639 – d. 1684 Hadley)

Ahnentafel #6166 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Joseph Baldwin was among the first settlers of Milford, Connecticut, in 1639, one of three brothers who came over together and were given home lots there after a first stop at New Haven. He married Hannah, who joined the Milford church on 23 June 1644, when their first four children were all baptized at once; Mary, who married John Catlin, was one of the four. Hannah died in 1661, and about 1663 Joseph moved north to Hadley in Massachusetts, where he was made a freeman in 1666. He married twice more: Isabella, the widow of the elder John Catlin and afterwards of James Northam, who died in 1676; and Elizabeth, the widow of Luke Hitchcock and then of William Warriner.

On 20 December 1680 he made his will, and its first words say exactly where he was and how he felt: “In the Name of God Amen the twentieth day of december, One thousand Six hundred and eighty, I Joseph Baldwin Senr of Hadley in the Countie of Hampshire within the Colony of the Massachusetts: being together with old age and of life now Sick and weake of body but of good and perfect Memory, thanks and praise be to Almighty God for it.” He left his wife the use of his house and land at Hadley for her life, excepting the half of his lands he had already made over to his son Joseph; the other half, with the houses and the moveables, went to that son. Then he went through the daughters one by one — five pounds to his “Loveing Daughter Hanah Hull” of New Haven and five pounds to her daughter Mary Hull when she should reach eighteen; forty shillings to “my Daughter Mary Cattlin of Arthur Cull”, across the water on the Arthur Kill in East Jersey; seventeen pounds to Elizabeth Warriner of Springfield; ten pounds to Sarah Bartlett of Northampton; and, for a daughter already dead, Mrs Hawks, five pounds each to her children John and Hannah Hawks when they came of age — and confirmed to his three sons Benjamin, Jonathan and David the gifts he had made them out of his Milford lands. He signed it before two witnesses, Chiliab Smith, who made his mark, and Samuel Partrigg.

Nearly four years later, on 30 October 1684, he had the will read over to him again and ratified it, appointing his grandson Joseph Baldwin junior his sole executor in place of the son, who had died before him. He lived only four days more, and died on 3 November 1684. The inventory of his estate was taken on the 8th by Lieutenant Smith, Chiliab Smith and Samuel Partrigg: £165 8s 7d in all, of which his house, barn and home lot at Hadley stood at £50 and ten acres of land at £45, with debts of £10 14s 3d, leaving a net estate of £154 14s 4d.

In the town he had helped to found at the other end of the colony, Milford set a granite boulder by the Wepawaug bridge among its founders' memorials: JOSEPH BALDWIN, OBIT 1690, HANNAH HIS WIFE. The date cut on it is wrong by six years.

John Moss (New Haven 1639 – 1707 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #12302 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Moss was at New Haven by 1639, in the first years of the colony, and he is one of the three names — with Doolittle and Peck — that between them made Wallingford. He served the older town first. He was one of the commissioners New Haven chose to settle its disputed boundary with Branford: they met on 5 October 1669 and, after full debate, set the bounds and ended the case for ever. Through the 1640s, 1650s and 1660s the colony's records carry him as a trainband corporal, a representative and a deputy. In 1670, when Wallingford was incorporated, he moved inland with the rest, and the town's historian, writing in 1870, called him flatly “the ancestor of all who bear the name in these parts.”

He and his wife had eleven children baptized at New Haven between 1640 and 1655. Among them were Mary, baptized 11 April 1647, who married John Peck in 1664 and is the one in this line; Abigail, who married Abraham Doolittle as his second wife; and a second John, born 1650, who carried his father's name and has been taken for him ever since.

He died in 1707, and the striking thing said of him was said everywhere: that he was 103 years old, and the oldest man who had ever died in Connecticut. The claim was local and old — the stonecutter carved it, and it was repeated from a Wallingford pulpit at the town's centennial in 1770 — and whether he truly reached it is another matter. His stone stands in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, a heart cut into its face, and inside the heart: “Mr John Moss, born 1604, died 1707, Æ 103.”
