Bertha Ellen Nease (b. 1878 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #31 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Bertha Ellen Nease was born on 12 January 1878 in Mason County, West Virginia, the eldest child of Lewis J. Nease, a farmer, and Martha Jane Love, who had married the April before. Her father reported the birth himself.

On 22 February 1897, at eighteen, she took out a marriage licence with John G. Roush at the Mason County courthouse - her father stood as the informant who gave the clerk her age and birthplace - and two days later they were married at the Evangelical Lutheran parsonage at New Haven. In 1897 she was marrying back into her own grandmother's family: her father's mother had been born a Roush.

By 1900 the couple were at Hartford village with two small daughters, living a few doors from John's father. In 1907 they moved to Broad Run so that John could go into the mine. On the evening of 12 September 1908 Bertha had been baking when the oil can by the stove took fire; she fought the flames and was burned about the hands and arms. Her eldest daughter died that night, a second five days later, and her husband on 21 October. The house, the furniture and the family's clothes were all gone, and she was carrying another child.

She did not go back to either set of grandparents. In April 1910 she was the head of her own household at Wagner, Mason County - a widow of thirty-three with Iva Mae, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia under her roof. On 2 March 1914 she married William L. Knopp at Mason City, he thirty-two and she thirty-four, by the Rev. C. H. Cox of the Church of the United Brethren in Christ. In March 1947, when she was ill at Mason, two of her daughters travelled down from Akron and Indiana to see her.

Bertha Ellen Knopp died on 5 April 1950 at St. Joseph's Hospital, Parkersburg, aged seventy-one, her usual residence still Mason, West Virginia. Her daughter Iva gave the information for the certificate, which names her parents as Lewis Nease and Martha Jane Love. She was buried at Broad Run on 7 April, under a stone cut MOTHER / BERTHA E. ROUSH KNOPP / 1878 - 1950: she carried both her husbands' names to the grave.

John T. Bell (1841 – 1915 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #32 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John T. Bell was born on 10 June 1841 and died on 6 November 1915 near Louisville, in Barbour County, Alabama — his death recorded not in a newspaper but in the petition his sons filed two days later, which says he died “at his house in Barbour Co. on or about the 6th day of November 1915.” He married Sarah Ann “Sallie” Cox in March 1861, weeks before the war, and they raised eleven children of whom eight outlived him.

His obituary ran in the Montgomery Advertiser at the end of that November and again, unchanged, in the hometown Clayton Record a few days later. It gives his birthday to the day, names all eight surviving children and two brothers, W. W. and C. A. Bell — and then says the sentence the rest of this family history hangs from: “His parents moved to Alabama from Darlington, S. C., about 1830, and settled in the neighborhood where he lived at his death.” He was born in Alabama eleven years after that move. The journey was his father and mother’s, not his, and Darlington District is where everything above them has to be looked for.

He farmed, and he prospered, on a place six miles south of Clayton that was known on the map simply as Bell’s. In April 1888 he and Sallie sold a right of way across their land to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway. When Sallie died in 1910 the county paper called him John T. Bell, Sr. — five years before the will, and a useful sign that the two Johns were already being told apart in print. By 1910 he was sixty-nine and living in his son Ben T.’s household, in his son Tom’s own precinct. When he died his executors told the court that the estate consisted “chiefly of lands, cotton, insurance and money, all of said real and personal estate being estimated to be worth about fifty thousand dollars” — something like a million and a half in modern money, to be divided among ten children and grandchildren’s lines.

His will names those children in his own voice, and the first of them carried his name: John T. Bell Jr., who became an undertaker at Clayton and who, nine years after his father’s death, paid for the burial of his uncle William. For a long time this file could not tell the two Johns apart, and the confusion sat on the chart as a warning that would not go away. It was the record that settled it, not the argument: the 1925 clerk wrote the word Junior, and the 1929 bond that closed the son’s own estate wrote it again.

Among his children was Tom — Thomas Jefferson Bell — and that is the step in Alec’s own line. Tom died six years after his father, leaving nothing at all to administer.

He lies at Bethlehem Baptist churchyard inside a low wrought-iron rail, under a marble shaft whose pediment is carved with the square and compasses of the Freemasons. Sallie’s stone stands a few feet from his across the same plot.

Sarah Ann “Sallie” Cox (1841 – 1910 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #33 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Ann Cox — Sallie — was born on 24 August 1841 and married John T. Bell on 7 March 1861 in Barbour County, Alabama. He was twenty, she was nineteen; the war began the following month.

They had eleven children, of whom eight outlived their father and were named in his will — six sons and two daughters. One of the sons was Thomas Jefferson Bell. A daughter married in 1886 was named Sallie for her. The 1880 census of Louisville is the one record that states it plainly: Thomas J. Bell, aged about four, with his parents named on the same lines, Sarah and John T.

In April 1888 she signed her name beside her husband’s on a deed selling a right of way to the Eufaula & East Alabama Railway, giving up her dower in about forty-six acres of it. She signed it Sarah A Bell. A wife joined her husband’s deed in order to release dower, and that formality is the reason her name is on the paper at all.

Her photograph survives, and it is the oldest likeness of anyone on the Bell side: an oval vignette portrait of an old woman with white hair swept up, a lace jabot at the throat of a dark dress, on an embossed cream mount that somebody in the family has labelled in blue ink “Mrs. John T. Bell. Mother of Sallie Bell Stallings.”

She died on the morning of Friday 11 March 1910, five years before John, at the home that was known on the map simply as Bell’s, six miles south of Clayton, after what the county paper called a long and lingering illness. It called her “one of the most estimable and widely known women in the county … a lady who has raised a large family and maintained the high respect and goodwill of all who knew her,” and recorded that she was survived by her husband, many children, grandchildren and some great-grandchildren. The funeral was preached at Bethlehem church by her former pastor, the Rev. W. R. Bickerstaff of Florala, and she was buried in the adjoining cemetery. By the time John’s will was probated no widow was named anywhere in it. Her headstone stands beside his inside the Bells’ iron-railed plot at Bethlehem Baptist churchyard, a white marble tablet under a carved egg-and-dart band, and it does not give her a surname at all: SALLIE A., WIFE OF J. T. BELL.

Henry Bradley (d. bef. 1891 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #34 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Henry Bradley lived in Barbour County, Alabama, and was dead before October 1891 — the month a county court appointed a guardian for his daughters Alice, fourteen, and Katie, twelve, recording that the minors had no father living. He is named as their father on both girls’ own death records, thirty-two years apart, one clerk writing him Henery; and that, with the silence in the guardianship, is all this family’s own papers hold of him.

In the summer of 1893 a Eufaula weekly printed, four times over, a notice from the Barbour County probate court that hands him back most of a family. His children’s guardian, a Bradley woman, had herself died, and her administratrix was settling the account of her guardianship of “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased, to-wit: Ida Parker, wife of Lee Parker; Annie Fortner, wife of W. J. Fortner; Henry Bradley, Alice Bradley and Katie Bradley.” Five children, two of them married off already, two of them still the wards the court had taken in two years before — and the phrase that matters, deceased, printed in his own county six years after he died.

He lies at Clayton City Cemetery, a few miles up the road from the farm, inside a rusting iron rail in the old part of the burying ground. His stone is a draped marble tablet carved with swags and a wreath, with the square and compasses of the Freemasons cut into its arch; the inscription reads HENRY BRADLEY, BORN APR. 25, 1836, DIED FEB. 27, 1887, though the figures are worn and those days of the month are the cemetery record’s reading rather than anything that can now be made out with certainty. He was fifty, and he died at Louisville — the Bells’ own village. Beside him, under the same rail, stands a second stone: ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, who outlived him by four years.

So Alice was ten when he died, and the county took charge of her four years later, after her mother had gone too. And the grave argues with the way this family history has described him. A draped marble monument with a Masonic emblem, standing inside its own iron enclosure, is not a poor man’s grave.

Joseph William Eakins — “Joel W.” on most of the paper and “William J.” on some of it — was born in December 1838 or 1839 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, a son of John Eakins Sr. and Nancy Costin. He is the “Jos., 12” enumerated in his father's house on the Moores Creek land in 1850, one of nine children there.

By the end of the 1850s the family's reach had extended to Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where an uncle, William Eakins, had already settled, and where four orphaned Eakens boys born in North Carolina were being raised in the household of William Payne. Two of his North Carolina kin went the other way that same May: his brother John J. B. Eakins and his cousin Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in the Moore's Creek Rifle Guards, Company E of the 18th North Carolina, and both were dead within two years — John at Sharpsburg, Owen at Chancellorsville.

He died in 1902 in Mississippi. His other lasting mark on the family was a piece of handwriting: he stopped dotting the “i” in Eakins, and his descendants have been Eakens, and then Eaken, ever since.

Caswell Massey Stephens (1852 Sumter Co. – 1935)

Ahnentafel #40 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Caswell Massey Stephens was born on 8 January 1852 in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of Henry H. Stephens and Sarah Ann Jones. He was named, at the very least, for a neighbour: in 1850 a twenty-four-year-old North Carolina man called Caswell Massey farmed the dwelling that stood between the Stephens house and the Joneses’. Whether he was kin to either family nobody has yet established. His mother died before he was three, and his father married her sister Elizabeth in October 1854. By 1860 the family had moved to Newton County, Mississippi, where the census catches him at eight.

On the last day of 1869, not yet eighteen, he stood in the Sumter County probate court before Judge J. A. Abrahams, said he was over fourteen, and chose his own father as his guardian — he and his brothers Calvin and John were the “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” their mother’s father, and his bond was set at $800. There was enough property in it that his father was filing annual settlements two years later.

In October 1871, aged nineteen, he married a widow thirteen years his senior. The register enters her as “Mrs. A. M. James,” and the officiant was H. H. Stephens, Minister — his own father. Her gravestone never gives her a first name either: it calls her A. M., wife of C. M. Stephens. Their children included Martha Alice, born 14 April 1874, who married John William Knight, and John Willis, born 12 March 1876; a son Rufus L. belongs to one marriage or the other. She died on 8 January 1891 — his thirty-ninth birthday — and on 19 April that year he married again, near Cuba. The county paper gave the whole of it in eleven words: “Married — Near Cuba, on the 19th, Mr C M Stephens and Mrs I Dearman.” The bride was Isabella, a Britt by birth and a Dearman by her first marriage, and she was about forty-five.

He farmed on into old age at Halsell, in Choctaw County, just over the Sumter line. In May 1928 his son Rufus died intestate at Livingston leaving about a thousand dollars; the probate papers record the only heir as “The Father Caswell M Stephens of Livingston Ala., age 76 years,” and he declined to administer it. He died at eight o’clock on a Sunday morning in February 1935, at the home of his daughter Mrs. Alice Knight, after a short illness. The Sumter County Journal ran the notice on its front page. Funeral services were at eleven on the Monday at Siloam Church, Revs. Ezell and Dearman officiating, and he was buried in the church cemetery at Siloam, where his first wife had lain for forty-four years. He left two sons, J. W. of Livingston and H. R. of Halsell, one daughter, two brothers — Bob Stephens of Kewanee and Bill Stephens of Cuba — and thirteen grandchildren and fifteen great-grandchildren.

Ava Marilla Shelton (1839 – 1891 Siloam AL)

Ahnentafel #41 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

The woman the family remembers as Ava Marilla Shelton was born on 21 November 1839, and nothing she left behind gives her a first name. Her gravestone calls her A. M.; the marriage register calls her Mrs. A. M. James; the initials are all there is.

She came to Caswell Massey Stephens as a widow. On 2 October 1871 — the alphabetical copy of the same register types the 5th — the Sumter County register entered “C. M. Stephens · A. M. James · Married by H. H. Stephens, Minister” — the groom’s own father officiating. She was thirty-one and he was nineteen, which has always looked like a strange match until you know that she had been married before; a descendant’s account posted to Find a Grave says her first husband was an Isaac James who served in Company C of the 27th Mississippi Infantry and may not have come home from the war, and that she had a daughter by him, Dartha, born about 1863. That is family memory rather than record, but it fits the register exactly.

With Caswell she had Martha Alice, born 14 April 1874, and John Willis, born 12 March 1876, and probably others. She died on 8 January 1891, her husband’s thirty-ninth birthday, aged fifty-one. He remarried near Cuba on 19 April that year, which fixes her death before that date from a second direction. She lies at Siloam Cemetery in Sumter County under a marble headstone carved with a dove above a spray of flowers: In memory of A. M., wife of C. M. Stephens, born Nov. 21, 1839, died Jan. 8, 1891, and below it four lines of verse now almost weathered away. Her husband’s flat granite marker lies in the grass beside her.
