Sennie E. Lawrence (1899 – 1975)

Ahnentafel #23 · his father’s mother’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sennie E. Lawrence was born at Coffeeville, Clarke County, Alabama, on 5 January 1899 — the date cut on her gravestone; as a small child her age was returned a year or two older, 1897 at one count and 1898 at the next, which is the ordinary drift of a child’s age reported by a parent. She was the fourth of seven children of “Calm” Lawrence, a farmer, and his wife Eliza. The man who called at the house in 1900 wrote her baby brother Alfonso down as nought days old: he had come on the day the child was born.

Her father was gone by 1910, and her mother Eliza kept the house with six children in it and no man. Clerks wrote the family name Lawrence, Larance and Laurence, and her own given name Sennie, Senie, Cenis and Zennie.

About 1916 she married Thomas William Truette. Their daughters Grace Estelle and Gladys were with them at Coffeeville in 1920, and a third, Jewel Rae, followed. Thomas died before 1930 and she was a widow at about thirty. On 24 June 1934 she married again, at Chatom in Washington County: Sol L. James Sr., born 18 April 1891. He died in 1953 and she was widowed a second time.

She died on 13 September 1975, aged seventy-six, and lies beside him at Ulcanush Cemetery, Coffeeville, under a double granite marker with the name JAMES on the base and a cross and spray of flowers cut on each stone. Her own slab reads SENNIE E., JAN. 5, 1899, SEPT. 13, 1975. The graveyard runs out into open field behind a chain-link fence, with a grain silo on the skyline. Her parents lie in the same ground, about forty yards off, under two slabs of rough concrete.

Dennis Clark Tuttle (1884 Peoria IL – 1975)

Ahnentafel #24 · his mother’s father’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Dennis Clark Tuttle was born at Peoria, Illinois, on 25 January 1884. His parents, Richard B. Tuttle and Emma S. Fuerstenberger, had married at Terre Haute two years earlier, and what took them to Peoria nobody now knows; they were home again within the year. On 17 December 1884, before Dennis could walk, his father died of consumption at twenty-six. He was named for his father’s elder brother Dennis and for his grandfather Clark Strong Tuttle, the miller of Otter Creek — so the “Clark” he carried had come down from a Connecticut bride of 1788, and he would pass it on to a grandson.

His mother married twice more, and the man who raised him was her third husband, Gifford H. Spear. Dennis repaid that in the plainest way a man of his time could: he gave his own first son the name Gifford.

On New Year’s Eve 1902, at the house of the bride’s widowed mother on North Sixth Street, he married Leona Marie Alexander of Clinton, Indiana. Terre Haute’s Daily Tribune had told the city four days earlier that “The marriage of Miss Leona Alexander and Mr. Dennis Tuttle will take place Wednesday evening at the home of the bride’s mother on North Sixth street,” and that “Mr. Tuttle is an employe of the Van shops”; the next day’s paper made him “a well known employe of the Van shops.” The Van shops were the Vandalia Railroad’s Terre Haute works, which the Pennsylvania Railroad absorbed in 1917 — so the forty-three years he eventually gave the Pennsylvania as a freight-car repairman began in a railroad town four days before his wedding. He retired onto the road’s Roll of Honor.

Gifford was born nine months and six days after the wedding, and a daughter, Dorothy, followed. Leona’s widowed mother Mary moved in with them and never left, dying in their house in 1934.

What he carried in his pockets survives: his Social Security card, issued on 22 April 1937; a courtesy card from Kerman Grotto, where he was the Grotto’s Prophet; an accident policy from the Terre Haute Tribune-Star dated 7 May 1952; and the card from an Indiana unemployment claim that paid him twenty dollars a week in his sixties. In April 1942 he registered for the “Old Man’s Draft” with Vigo County Board 1. In 1934, from 2626 Fenwood Avenue, he took $1,813 as his late mother’s one-fifth share of a family estate.

By the early 1950s he and Leona were at 2438 North Eleventh Street. On New Year’s Eve 1952 they had been married fifty years, and the Terre Haute Tribune printed their photograph two days beforehand under the heading FIFTIETH ANNIVERSARY — a stiff-collared old railroader beside his wife. They issued no invitations; the paper simply told the city that the Tuttles would be at home on New Year’s Day to their friends, and that all were invited. Their son Gifford came down from Evanston with his wife, their daughter Dorothy Stephens was living at home, and their two grandsons sent word, one from Rochester, New York, the other from an army post in Germany.

He and Leona were married sixty-seven years. She died in 1970, his son Gifford in December 1974, and he himself on 22 August 1975, at ninety-one. He lies with Leona at Highland Lawn Cemetery in Terre Haute, in the same ground as the Bavarian shoemaker who was his grandfather.

Leona Marie Alexander (1883 Clinton IN – 1970)

Ahnentafel #25 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Leona Marie Alexander was born at Clinton, in Vermillion County, Indiana, on 16 February 1883, the only child William L. Alexander and Mary Elizabeth Fletcher ever had. Her father died while she was small. By the summer of 1900 she was seventeen and living with her widowed mother at 314 Mulberry Street in Terre Haute, in a house her mother owned outright and paid for by keeping house for other people.

On New Year’s Eve 1902 she married Dennis Clark Tuttle, a young man out of the Vandalia railroad shops; the wedding was in her mother’s house on North Sixth Street. Their son Gifford arrived nine months and six days later, and a daughter, Dorothy, followed. Her mother moved in with them and stayed until her death in 1934.

She was a member of St. Stephen’s Episcopal Church in Terre Haute — the first Episcopalian in a family that had been Congregational for six generations, and the change may well have come in with her. She was also a life member of the Daughters of Mokanna, Kerman Caldron No. 19, the women’s side of her husband’s Grotto; her 1964 membership card is among the family papers.

On New Year’s Eve 1952 she and Dennis had been married fifty years. The Terre Haute Tribune printed their photograph — Leona in a dark lace-shouldered dress, smiling, her glasses catching the flash — above a caption inviting the whole city to call on them on New Year’s Day. Her daughter Dorothy Stephens was living at home with them that winter; her son Gifford came from Evanston.

She died at three o’clock on the afternoon of Sunday 13 September 1970 at St. Anthony Hospital, aged eighty-seven, out of the house at 501 North Seventh Street she and Dennis had lived in. She left a husband of sixty-seven years, a daughter in Terre Haute, a son at Highland Park, Illinois, two grandsons and seven great-grandchildren. The funeral was at St. Stephen’s, the Rev. Harold Taylor officiating, and she was buried at Highland Lawn, where Dennis joined her five years later.

Joseph McGee Knott (~1851 OH – 1932 Cedar Rapids IA)

Ahnentafel #26 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph McGee Knott was born on 5 March 1851 at Lowell, in Washington County, Ohio, on the Muskingum, the son of Benjamin and Margaret Nott. The family spelled the name both ways for a century; he was born a Nott and died a Knott.

He grew up on his father’s farm in Adams Township, with the post office at Lowell: Benjamin and Margaret, both forty-three in 1860, and the boys — P. J. fifteen, William F. thirteen, and “Jos M” eleven. A brother, John A. Nott, born in 1846, died in 1851, the year Joseph was born, and lies in the Mason cemetery near the village. In the summer of 1859 diphtheria went through Adams Township and carried off a houseful of five-year-olds, among them a Thomas H. Nott, aged five. Joseph, then eight, came through it.

By about 1868 he was at work in a steel mill, and he stayed at it: a heater, fifty-seven years in the trade, last working in 1925. The work took him to Terre Haute, Indiana, about 1881, and he was a resident of the city for the next fifty years. About 1897 he married Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford, a widow with a young son, Percy; their daughters were Mildred and Helen. The family lived at 3500 Wabash Avenue.

He was not only a mill hand. By the summer of 1915 Joseph Knott of 623 North Ninth Street was one of the City of Terre Haute’s four park superintendents, in charge of Steeg Park; on 1 October 1915, with the formal close of the park season, he and the other three superintendents and nine labourers were dropped from the city payroll. Two days later he was in the witness chair. The city council was taking evidence in the great Terre Haute election-fraud inquiry into Mayor Donn M. Gossom, and “Joseph Knott, of 623 North Ninth street, until recently superintendent of Steeg park, was next called.” He testified that the mayor had asked him to wake him on the morning of the election to go down and organise the board, that Gossom then left the precinct in Knott’s charge and drove off saying “he had a lot off ‘hunkies’ he wanted to run in” — to be picked up “around the Malleable works or stamping mill” — and that Gossom told him that night, “I finished up across the river this evening.”

In November 1914 he and his wife, then living on Rose Avenue, announced their elder daughter Mildred’s engagement to Emmet C. Cooper, and in July 1916 their first grandson was born in their house and went onto the front page of the Tribune as the first war baby born in Terre Haute.

On 1 September 1931, at eighty, he left Terre Haute for Cedar Rapids, Iowa, to live with Mildred — Mrs. E. C. Cooper, of 2505 First Avenue. He had been there four and a half months when he died, in her house at 10:40 in the morning on Friday 15 January 1932, of arteriosclerosis, with a cerebral haemorrhage on the 7th and bronchopneumonia on the 14th among the causes; a doctor had been attending him since 24 October. He left a wife, two daughters and four grandchildren. There was no service in Iowa: the family set off for Terre Haute the next day, and on 18 January the Ryan funeral home buried him at Highland Lawn, Section 14, Lot 386. His flat granite marker reads simply Joseph Knott 1851 1932. Thirteen years later his widow was laid in the same grave, of the same cause, by the same undertaker.

Martha Ellen Lamb (1865 Richmond – 1945 Terre Haute)

Ahnentafel #27 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Martha Ellen Lamb was born on 27 August 1865 at Richmond, in Wayne County, Indiana, the second of the four children of Erwin R. Lamb and his first wife Melissa J. Bailey. She was ten years old when her mother died at Newport, after a few days’ illness, on the evening of 29 November 1875.

Her father married again in May 1879, to Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, a Civil War widow with five children of her own, and in 1880 the blended household was living on Main Street in Vermillion, Indiana: Erwin forty, his new wife, “Mattie E.” the eldest girl at home, her brother Frank, her sister Bell, and a nine-year-old stepsister, Effie Hall. Her father eventually left Indiana altogether and died at Jonesboro, Arkansas, in 1919.

She married twice. Her first husband was George Ford, and their son was Percy. By May 1890 she was keeping house as Mrs. Mattie Ford on north Third Street in Terre Haute together with her brother Frank, and her father came down from Plainfield to visit the pair of them. Mattie was what the family called her.

About 1897, at Terre Haute, she married Joseph McGee Knott, a steel-mill heater fourteen years her senior, and had two daughters, Mildred and Helen. One small document in her own hand survives: the back of a real-photo postcard of the two girls, pencilled “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen” and signed “Mama.” The spelling is phonetic, and it is the only specimen of her writing anyone has found.

She and Joseph announced Mildred’s engagement to Emmet C. Cooper in November 1914, and in July 1916 her first grandchild was born in her house. Joseph died in January 1932 at Cedar Rapids, in Mildred’s house; Martha was with him, and the family travelled home to Terre Haute together for the funeral.

Widowed, Martha moved in with her daughter Helen and son-in-law Gifford Tuttle, and was still in that household in 1940 — so Alec’s grandfather, Richard Clark Tuttle, then ten, grew up under the same roof as his grandmother. She died on 18 January 1945 at Terre Haute of a cerebral haemorrhage, aged seventy-nine, and two days later the Ryan funeral home buried her in Highland Lawn Cemetery, Section 14, Lot 386 — the same grave as Joseph, under a flat granite marker matching his: Martha E. Knott 1865 1945.

Harry B. Frost (~1860 Macclesfield – 1930 Wyckoff)

Ahnentafel #28 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Harry B. Frost was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire, the English silk town, about March 1860, the second son of George Frost and Frances W. Bell. He was four years old when the family crossed: they came to America in 1864 and settled at Paterson, the silk-weaving capital of the country, built on English hands. He went through the Paterson public schools, finishing at the J. Waters school.

He began work with his father in the throwing business — the twisting of raw silk into yarn — while still a boy. At twenty he was living at 309 Straight Street and working in the silk mill, in a house with his father (fifty-eight, silk manufacturer, rheumatism set down against his name), his mother, a sister and three brothers. That same year, 1880, his father took him and his brother Frank into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, a hundred feet of frontage on Madison Street; the firm was the first house in Paterson to bring in the celebrated Atwood machinery. The mill he was taken into was, as it stood in 1887, a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street, Nos. 34 to 52, with GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING lettered along the pavement, three hundred hands at work in it, one engine of 350 horsepower driving the whole of it, and an artesian well sunk in the yard.

On 10 June 1896, aged thirty-six, giving his trade as silk manufacturer and his home as Paterson, he married at Detroit, Michigan. The bride was Kathaline Chalmers, thirty, a schoolteacher living in Detroit but born at Orange, New Jersey. A pastor named John Reed performed the ceremony, with Mary N. Reed and Edith May Reid as witnesses. Why a Paterson man and a New Jersey woman were married in Michigan nobody in the family can now say.

Three children followed: Katherine King, born in September 1898; Harry Bell, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900; and Alan Chalmers, born at Paterson in February 1903. There was a live-in servant in the house all through those years. About 1908 he moved the family out of the city to Wyckoff Avenue, in Wyckoff, Bergen County, and lived there the last twenty-two years of his life. He was in silk for fifty years — George Frost & Sons, then the Frost & Van Riper Co., with mills at Paterson and at Scranton and Pittston in Pennsylvania, and at the end the Special Rayon Throwing Company. He was a Mason of Joppa Lodge and a life member of Mecca Temple of the Mystic Shrine.

He was ill through the second half of 1930 and died at home on the evening of Thursday 18 December, in his seventy-first year. The funeral was held at the house on the Saturday afternoon at half past two, taken by the rector of St. Paul’s Episcopal Church, Paterson, and the minister of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and he was buried at Cedar Lawn in Paterson, where his father and his brother Frank already lay. “He was of a kindly and generous disposition,” the Wyckoff paper said, “and was always happiest when he had a group of friends around him.” His widow Katharine outlived him by thirteen months.
