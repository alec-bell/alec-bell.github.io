Dennis Clark Tuttle (1884 Peoria IL – 1975)

Ahnentafel #24 · his mother’s father’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Dennis Clark Tuttle was born on 25 January 1884 at Peoria, Illinois, where his parents Richard B. Tuttle and Emma S. Fuerstenberger were living for reasons nobody has yet explained — they had married at Terre Haute two years before, and they were back there within the year. On 17 December 1884, before Dennis was a year old, his father died of consumption. He was named for his father's elder brother Dennis, and for his grandfather Clark Strong Tuttle, the miller of Otter Creek — so the “Clark” he carried had come down from a Connecticut bride of 1788 and would go on to his own grandson.

His mother married twice more, and the man who raised him was her third husband, Gifford H. Spear. Dennis repaid that in the plainest way a man of his time could: he gave his own first son the name Gifford.

On New Year's Eve 1902 he married Leona Marie Alexander of Clinton, Indiana; Gifford was born nine months and six days later, and a daughter, Dorothy, followed. Leona's widowed mother Mary lived in their house through the censuses of 1910, 1920 and 1930 — forty years under their roof.

He spent forty-three years as a freight-car repairman for the Pennsylvania Railroad and retired onto its Roll of Honor. His Social Security card was issued on 22 April 1937, and he kept it in his wallet, which survives with the rest of what he carried: a Kerman Grotto courtesy card — he was the Grotto's Prophet — an accident policy from the Terre Haute Tribune-Star dated 7 May 1952, and the card from an Indiana unemployment claim that paid him twenty dollars a week in his sixties. In April 1942 he registered for the “Old Man's Draft” with Vigo County Board 1. In 1934 the probate of an in-law's estate found him at 2626 Fenwood Avenue, Terre Haute, taking $1,813 as his late mother's one-fifth share.

By the early 1950s Dennis and Leona were living at 2438 North Eleventh Street in Terre Haute. On New Year's Eve 1952 they marked fifty years of marriage, and the Terre Haute Tribune printed their photograph two days before: a stiff-collared old railroader beside his wife, under the heading FIFTIETH ANNIVERSARY. They issued no invitations - the paper simply told the city that the Tuttles would be at home on New Year's Day to their friends, and that all were invited. Their son Gifford came down from Evanston with his wife, their daughter Dorothy Stephens was living at home, and their two grandsons sent word, one from Rochester, New York, the other from an army post in Germany. He outlived his son Gifford, who died in December 1974, by eight months.

Terre Haute's Daily Tribune carried the wedding twice in the week it happened. On 27 December 1902 it told readers that "The marriage of Miss Leona Alexander and Mr. Dennis Tuttle will take place Wednesday evening at the home of the bride's mother on North Sixth street" — Wednesday being New Year's Eve — and added that "Mr. Tuttle is an employe of the Van shops." A second notice the next day called him "a well known employe of the Van shops." The Van shops were the Vandalia Railroad's Terre Haute works, which the Pennsylvania Railroad absorbed in 1917; so the forty-three years he eventually gave the Pennsylvania begin in these two paragraphs, in a railroad town, four days before he married.

He and Leona were married sixty-seven years. She died in 1970; he died on 22 August 1975, and the two of them lie at Highland Lawn Cemetery in Terre Haute, in the same ground as the Bavarian grandfather he never knew.

Leona Marie Alexander (1883 Clinton IN – 1970)

Ahnentafel #25 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Leona Marie Alexander was born on 16 February 1883 at Clinton, in Vermillion County, Indiana, the only child William L. Alexander and Mary Elizabeth Fletcher ever had. Her father died while she was small — somewhere between her birth and 1900 — and by the summer of 1900 she was seventeen and living with her widowed mother at 314 Mulberry Street in Terre Haute, in a house her mother owned outright and paid for by keeping house for other people. The census-taker wrote her name down as “Leon.”

On New Year's Eve 1902 she married Dennis Clark Tuttle, a young railroad man. Their son Gifford arrived nine months and six days later — which is how the date of the wedding was finally pinned down against a family tradition that had it a year later. A daughter, Dorothy, followed. Her mother moved in with them and stayed: Mary appears in their household in 1910, in 1920 and again in 1930, and died there in 1934.

She was a member of St. Stephen's Episcopal Church in Terre Haute — the earliest sign of a family that had been Congregational for six generations turning Episcopalian, and the change may well have come in with her. She was a life member of the Daughters of Mokanna, Kerman Caldron No. 19, the women's side of her husband's Grotto; her 1964 membership card is among the family papers.

On New Year's Eve 1952 she and Dennis had been married fifty years, and the Terre Haute Tribune printed their photograph - Leona in a dark lace-shouldered dress, smiling, her glasses catching the flash - above a caption inviting the whole city to call on them on New Year's Day. Her daughter Dorothy Stephens was living at home with them that winter; her son Gifford came from Evanston.

Her wedding was announced in the Terre Haute Daily Tribune on 27 and 28 December 1902. The ceremony was to be "Wednesday evening at the home of the bride's mother on North Sixth street" — so the family tradition of a New Year's Eve wedding is confirmed by the newspapers of the week, and the house it happened in was her mother's.

She died at three o'clock on the afternoon of Sunday 13 September 1970 at St. Anthony Hospital, aged eighty-seven, from the house at 501 North Seventh Street she and Dennis had lived in. Her notice records a husband of sixty-seven years, a daughter in Terre Haute, a son at Highland Park, Illinois, two grandsons and seven great-grandchildren. The funeral was at St. Stephen's, the Rev. Harold Taylor officiating; she was buried at Highland Lawn, where Dennis joined her five years later.

Joseph McGee Knott (~1851 OH – 1932 Cedar Rapids IA)

Ahnentafel #26 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph McGee Knott was born on 5 March 1851 at Lowell, in Washington County, Ohio, on the Muskingum, the son of Benjamin and Margaret Nott. The 1851 is what his death certificate says, what his Cedar Rapids death notice said, and what is cut on his stone; the 1900 census is the dissenter, giving March 1855. The family spelled the name both ways for a century; he died a Knott and was indexed a Nott, which is why it took this project four counties and two states to find his death.

He grew up on his father’s farm in Adams Township. The census of 1860 found the household on page one, dwelling six, with the post office given as Lowell: Benjamin forty-three, Margaret forty-three, P. J. fifteen, William F. thirteen, and “Jos M” eleven. A brother, John A. Nott, had been born in 1846 and died in 1851, the year Joseph was born, and lies in the Mason cemetery near the village; in the summer of 1859 diphtheria went through Adams Township and filled the mortality schedule with five-year-olds, among them a Thomas H. Nott, aged five. Joseph, then eight, came through it.

By about 1868 he was at work in a steel mill, and he stayed at it: his death certificate records “Heater — Steel Mill,” fifty-seven years in the trade, last worked in 1925. The work took him to Terre Haute, Indiana, about 1881 — the city paper that reported his death said he had been a resident there for fifty years — and he appears in four consecutive censuses of it. About 1897 he married Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford, a widow with a young son, Percy; their daughters were Mildred and Helen. He is named as their father on Helen’s 1923 marriage application. The family lived at 3500 Wabash Avenue.

He was not only a mill hand. By the summer of 1915 Joseph Knott of 623 North Ninth Street was one of the City of Terre Haute's four park superintendents, in charge of Steeg Park; on 1 October 1915, with the formal close of the park season, he and the other three superintendents and nine labourers were dropped from the city payroll. Two days later he was in the witness chair. The city council was taking evidence in the great Terre Haute election-fraud inquiry into Mayor Donn M. Gossom, and "Joseph Knott, of 623 North Ninth street, until recently superintendent of Steeg park, was next called." He testified that the mayor had asked him to wake him on the morning of the election to go down and organise the board, that Gossom then left the precinct in Knott's charge and drove off saying "he had a lot off 'hunkies' he wanted to run in" — to be picked up "around the Malleable works or stamping mill" — and that Gossom told him that night, "I finished up across the river this evening." In November 1914 he and his wife announced their elder daughter's engagement, and in July 1916 their first grandson was born in their house and the Tribune put him on page one as the first war baby born in Terre Haute.

On 1 September 1931, at eighty, he left Terre Haute for Cedar Rapids, Iowa, to live with his elder daughter Mildred — Mrs. E. C. Cooper, of 2505 First Avenue. He had been there four and a half months when he died, in her house at 10:40 in the morning on Friday 15 January 1932, of arteriosclerosis, with a cerebral haemorrhage on the 7th and bronchopneumonia on the 14th among the causes; a doctor had attended him since 24 October. The informant who gave the particulars — a Mrs. Nott of Cedar Rapids — signed with a mark, and left the line for his mother’s maiden name dashed out. He left a wife, two daughters and four grandchildren. There was no service in Iowa: the family set off for Terre Haute the next day, and on 18 January the Ryan funeral home buried him at Highland Lawn, Terre Haute, Section 14, Lot 386. His flat granite marker reads simply Joseph Knott 1851 1932. Thirteen years later his widow was laid in the same grave, of the same cause, by the same undertaker.

Martha Ellen Lamb (1865 Richmond – 1945 Terre Haute)

Ahnentafel #27 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Martha Ellen Lamb was born on 27 August 1865 at Richmond, in Wayne County, Indiana — the date her 1945 death certificate gives, and the year cut on her gravestone, though the 1880 census suggests a birth closer to 1862 or 1863. She was the second of the four children of Erwin R. Lamb and his first wife Melissa J. Bailey, and she was ten years old when her mother died at Newport, after a few days’ illness, on the evening of 29 November 1875.

Her father married again in May 1879, to Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, a Civil War widow with five children of her own, and the census of 1880 found the blended household on Main Street in Vermillion, Indiana: Erwin forty, his new wife, “Mattie E.” seventeen, her brother Frank fifteen, her sister Bell thirteen, and a nine-year-old stepsister, Effie Hall. It is the last record that shows Martha at home. Her father eventually left Indiana altogether and died at Jonesboro, Arkansas, in 1919.

She married twice. Her first husband was George Ford, and their son was Percy. About 1897, at Terre Haute, she married Joseph McGee Knott, a steel-mill heater fourteen years her senior, and had two daughters — Mildred and Helen. One small document in her own hand survives: the back of a real-photo postcard of the two girls, pencilled “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen” and signed “Mama.” The spelling is phonetic, and it is the only specimen of her writing anyone has found.

A single line of Terre Haute social news from 1890 very probably puts her father in her house: "Erwin Lamb, of Plainfield, Ind., is visiting his daughter, Mrs. Mattie Ford, and son, Frank Lamb, on north Third street." Mattie was what the family called her, Ford was her first husband's name, and Frank was her brother — both of them are the children aged 17 and 15 in Erwin Lamb's 1880 household. She announced her elder daughter Mildred's engagement with her husband in November 1914, and in July 1916 her first grandchild was born in her house.

Joseph died in January 1932 at Cedar Rapids, in their daughter Mildred’s house; Martha was there — the Iowa paper’s notice says he was survived “besides his wife” by two daughters — and the family travelled home to Terre Haute together for the funeral. Widowed, Martha moved in with her daughter Helen and son-in-law Gifford Tuttle, and the 1940 census still has her in that household — so Alec’s grandfather, Richard Clark Tuttle, then ten, grew up under the same roof as his grandmother. She died on 18 January 1945 at Terre Haute of a cerebral haemorrhage, aged seventy-nine, and two days later the Ryan funeral home buried her in Highland Lawn Cemetery, Section 14, Lot 386 — the same grave as Joseph, under a flat granite marker matching his: Martha E. Knott 1865 1945.

Harry B. Frost (~1860 Macclesfield – 1930 Wyckoff)

Ahnentafel #28 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Harry B. Frost was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire, the English silk town, about March 1860, the second son of George Frost and Frances W. Bell. He was four years old when the family crossed: he “came to America in 1864 and settled with his parents in Paterson” — the silk-weaving capital of America, built on English hands. He went through the Paterson public schools, finishing at the J. Waters school.

He began work with his father in the throwing business — the twisting of raw silk into yarn — while still a boy. In June 1880 the census found him at 309 Straight Street, aged twenty, “works in silk mill”, with his father (fifty-eight, silk manufacturer, with “rheumatism” written against his name in the health column), his mother, a sister and three brothers. That same year his father took him and his brother Frank into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, a hundred feet of frontage on Madison Street. An industrial history of Paterson published in 1882 credits the firm with being the first in the city to bring in the celebrated Atwood machinery.

On 10 June 1896, aged thirty-six, giving his trade as silk manufacturer and his home as Paterson, he married at Detroit, Michigan. The bride was Kathaline Chalmers, thirty, a schoolteacher living in Detroit but born at Orange, New Jersey. Why a Paterson man married a New Jersey woman in Michigan has never been explained. A pastor named John Reed performed the ceremony, with Mary N. Reed and Edith May Reid as witnesses.

Three children followed: Katherine King, born in September 1898; Harry Bell, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900; and Alan Chalmers, born at Paterson in February 1903. The household kept a live-in servant in every census that catches it. About 1908 he moved the family out of the city to Wyckoff Avenue, in Wyckoff, Bergen County, and lived there the last twenty-two years of his life. He was in silk for fifty years — George Frost & Sons, then the Frost & Van Riper Co., with mills at Paterson and at Scranton and Pittston in Pennsylvania, and at the end the Special Rayon Throwing Company. He was a Mason of Joppa Lodge and a life member of Mecca Temple of the Mystic Shrine.

The mill he was taken into partnership in can be seen on paper: Paterson's 1887 fire-insurance survey draws it as a brick block on the north side of Madison Street, Nos. 34 to 52, lettered GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING, with three hundred hands, a 350-horsepower engine and an artesian well.

He was ill through the second half of 1930 and died at home on the evening of Thursday 18 December, in his seventy-first year. The funeral was held at the house on the Saturday afternoon at half past two, taken by the rector of St. Paul’s Episcopal Church, Paterson, and the minister of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and he was buried at Cedar Lawn in Paterson, where his father and his brother Frank already lay. “He was of a kindly and generous disposition,” the Wyckoff paper said, “and was always happiest when he had a group of friends around him.” His widow Katharine outlived him by thirteen months.

Katharine Chalmers (1866 Orange NJ – 1932 Paterson)

Ahnentafel #29 · his mother’s mother’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Katharine Chalmers was born in January 1866 at Orange, in Essex County, New Jersey — her obituary says East Orange — the daughter of Mathew Chalmers and Ellen King, both of them Scots. She became a schoolteacher, and by 1896 she was living in Detroit; it was there, on 10 June that year, that she married Harry B. Frost of Paterson, an English-born silk manufacturer six years her senior.

She named her children from both sides of the house. Katherine King Frost, born in September 1898, carried her own mother’s maiden name; Harry Bell Frost, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900, carried his father’s mother’s; and Alan Chalmers Frost, born at Paterson in February 1903, carried hers. About 1908 the family left the city for Wyckoff Avenue, in Bergen County, and she lived there for the rest of her life — twenty-five years, the papers said.

She was a figure in both towns. She was second vice-president of the Paterson Woman’s Club in 1930, and until her death a member of its board of directors and chairman of its reception committee; she was a past president of the Wyckoff Woman’s Club; she was an active member of the Wyckoff Reformed Church, and for many years before that of the Baptist church at East Orange.

Harry died at the house on 18 December 1930, and she was a widow for thirteen months. In January 1932 her youngest son came up from Wilmington for a weekend; three weeks after that she went into the Paterson General Hospital with a short illness and died there at a quarter to eleven on the night of Sunday 31 January. The service was held the following Wednesday afternoon at the house on Wyckoff Avenue, taken by the curate of St. Paul’s, Paterson, and the Paterson Woman’s Club postponed its regular meeting and went to the funeral in a body. She left a daughter and two sons, and two sisters at East Orange — Mrs. Margaret Jordan and Miss Sarah Chalmers.
