Henry Bradley (1836 – 1887 Clayton AL)

Ahnentafel #34 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Henry Bradley was born in Alabama about 1836, of South Carolina parents, and spent his life in Barbour County. He fought for the Confederacy. A Henry Bradley, aged twenty-four, is on the roll of the Clayton Guards, the company raised in his own town for the 1st Alabama Infantry and mustered at Pensacola in 1861, alongside a brother, Abraham; and the state’s register of maimed soldiers lists “H. Bradley, clerk” of Barbour County, 1st Alabama, wounded at Atlanta on 28 July 1864 — very probably him, and the wound that would kill him twenty-odd years later. On 8 July 1867, in the first registration taken in Alabama under the Reconstruction acts, he signed the Board of Registration’s return of qualified voters for Precinct No. 9 — line 97, “Bradly Henry” — on a page where his neighbours are set down White and Colored alternately down the column, and a tick against his name says he came back and used the vote. A Bell and a Cox registered at the same table: the two families his daughters would marry into were in the same room.

That December he married. Barbour County issued a licence to “Henry Bradly and Elvira Loveless, both of full age” on 18 December 1867, and the judge of probate signed the authorisation the following day. He was thirty-one; she was eighteen. By June 1880 there were five children in the house at Clayton — Ida J. nine, Anna V. seven, Henry R. five, Alice three, and Katie seven months old — and he was farming then as well as keeping the court’s books.

He was also clerk of the circuit court of Barbour County, and he died in office. On Monday 21 February 1887 erysipelas set into the old war wound; congestion of the lungs, stomach and bowels followed, and he died at his home in Clayton at one o’clock on the morning of Sunday 27 February, aged fifty. He was buried at Clayton at ten the next morning with Masonic honours. He had left his family well provided for by the standards of the place and the time — nine thousand dollars of life insurance in all: two thousand with the Knights of Honor, two thousand with the A.O.U.W. and five thousand with the New York Life. Petitions for a successor were circulating within two days of the funeral, four men applied for the unexpired term, and in the second week of March the governor appointed Capt. John C. McNab to the clerkship.

Four years later his widow died too, and in the autumn of 1891 the county took his two youngest, Alice at fourteen and Katie at twelve, into guardianship. In the summer of 1893 a Eufaula weekly printed, four times over, the Barbour County probate court’s settlement of that guardianship, and with it the names of all five of his surviving children — the account of the guardian of “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased, to-wit: Ida Parker, wife of Lee Parker; Annie Fortner, wife of W. J. Fortner; Henry Bradley, Alice Bradley and Katie Bradley.” Five children, two of them married off already, two of them still wards of the court.

He lies at Clayton City Cemetery, inside a rusting iron rail in the old part of the burying ground, under a draped marble tablet carved with swags and a wreath and with the square and compasses of the Freemasons cut into its arch. It reads HENRY BRADLEY, BORN APR. 25, 1836, DIED FEB. 27, 1887, though the day-figures are worn now past certainty. Beside him, under the same rail, stands a second stone: ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, who outlived him by four years. A draped monument with a Masonic emblem inside its own iron enclosure is not a poor man’s grave.

Elvira Loveless (fl. 1876–1891 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #35 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Elvira Loveless was born in Alabama in August 1849, of Alabama parents. At eighteen she married Henry Bradley of Barbour County on a licence issued at the courthouse on 18 December 1867 — “Henry Bradly and Elvira Loveless, both of full age” — and J. S. Williams, judge of probate, signed the authorisation to marry them the next day. Whoever married them never sent the licence back: the printed return underneath it, “I hereby certify that the above named persons were duly married by me on the ___ day of ___”, was left blank, so the county book records the authority and never the wedding.

She bore at least eight children and buried three of them — one at birth in 1870, a boy of eleven in 1879, a baby in 1884. Five grew up: Ida, born about 1871, who married Lee Parker; Annie, who married a Fortner of Dothan; a son Henry; and the two youngest, Alice, born 8 October 1876, and Katie, born in 1879. In 1880 the census-taker found her at Clayton, thirty-one and keeping house, with all five of them under her roof.

Her husband, the clerk of the circuit court, died of an old war wound in February 1887. Three years a widow, in January 1890 she bought Jake Fortner’s house at Mt. Andrew and moved the family there, and her daughter Annie was married in that house the following September. Then, on a Sunday afternoon in the summer after, it was her turn. She had dressed her children for Sunday school that morning; when she was taken ill they sent for the doctor, and sent too late. She died at Clayton on 14 June 1891 of cholera morbus, aged forty-one, and the paper called her “Mrs. Bradley, relict of former Clerk Henry Bradley,” leaving five children.

Four months later a Barbour County court took the two youngest, Alice and Katie, into guardianship. The order speaks only of the father, because that is the form of words the clerk used; in fact the girls had lost both parents.

She lies at Clayton City Cemetery beside her husband, inside the same iron rail, under a draped marble tablet with a star cut into the arch and a worn verse on the plinth. It gives her no surname at all — only ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, born in August 1849 and dead in June 1891, the day-figures too worn now to be certain of.

Joseph William Eakins (~1838 NC – 1902 MS)

Ahnentafel #36 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph William Eakins — “Joel W.” on most of the paper and “William J.” on some of it — was born in December 1838 or 1839 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, a son of John Eakins Sr. and Nancy Costin. In 1850 he was twelve, one of nine children in his father’s house on the Moores Creek land the family had farmed since before the Constitution was ratified.

By the end of the 1850s the family’s reach had extended to Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where an uncle, William Eakins, had already settled, and where four orphaned Eakens boys born in North Carolina were being raised in the household of William Payne. On 13 May 1861, a month after the war began, Joel married Payne’s daughter Sarah Hudson Payne. The marriage happened inside a house that had already fused the two families for a decade.

Caswell Massey Stephens was born on 8 January 1852, in Sumter County, Alabama by the family’s reckoning, though a later hand put his birth in Mississippi. He was the son of Henry H. Stephens, a farmer and Minister of the Gospel, and Sarah Ann Jones. He was named at the very least for a neighbour: in 1850 a twenty-four-year-old North Carolina man called Caswell Massey farmed the dwelling that stood between the Stephens house and the Joneses’, though whether he was kin to either family nobody knows. His mother died before he was three, and his father married her sister Elizabeth in October 1854. By 1860 the family had moved to Newton County, Mississippi, where he was eight.

On the last day of 1869, not yet eighteen, he stood in the Sumter County probate court before Judge J. A. Abrahams, said he was over fourteen, and chose his own father as his guardian: he and his brothers Calvin and John were the “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” their mother’s father, and his bond was set at $800. There was enough property in it that his father was still filing annual settlements two years later.

In October 1871, aged nineteen, he married a widow thirteen years his senior, and his own father performed the ceremony. The register enters her as “Mrs. A. M. James”; her gravestone never gives her a first name either, calling her only A. M., wife of C. M. Stephens. The family remembers her as Ava Marilla Shelton. Their children included Martha Alice, born 14 April 1874, who married John William Knight, and John Willis, born 12 March 1876; a son Rufus L. belongs to one marriage or the other. She died on 8 January 1891 — his thirty-ninth birthday — and on 19 April that year he married again, near Cuba. The county paper gave the whole of it in eleven words: “Married — Near Cuba, on the 19th, Mr C M Stephens and Mrs I Dearman.” The bride was Isabella, a Britt by birth and a Dearman by her first marriage, and she was about forty-five. She died in 1917.

He farmed all his life. At twenty-three he registered to vote in Precinct No. 5 of Sumter County on 19 June 1875, entry 67, signing “C M Stephens” in his own hand and giving his occupation, like almost every man on the page, as farmer. In old age he was at Halsell, in Choctaw County, just over the Sumter line. In May 1928 his son Rufus died at Livingston without a will, leaving about a thousand dollars, and the only heir was “The Father Caswell M Stephens of Livingston Ala., age 76 years,” who declined to administer it.

A photograph kept in the family as him shows a bearded old man in wire spectacles and a dark coat, sitting on a huge heap of split firewood with a small fair-haired child in a white dress leaning against his knee — said to be his granddaughter Clara Mae.

He died a widower at eight o’clock on the morning of Sunday 10 February 1935, aged eighty-three, after a short illness, at the home of his daughter Mrs. Alice Knight; his stone was cut a week out, with the 17th. The Sumter County Journal ran the notice on its front page. Funeral services were at eleven on the Monday at Siloam Church, Revs. Ezell and Dearman officiating, and he was buried in the church cemetery at Siloam, where his first wife had lain forty-four years. He left two sons, J. W. of Livingston and H. R. of Halsell, one daughter, two brothers — Bob Stephens of Kewanee and Bill Stephens of Cuba — and thirteen grandchildren and fifteen great-grandchildren.
