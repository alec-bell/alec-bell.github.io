Clark Strong Tuttle was born in April 1811 in Jefferson County, New York, and died in Vigo County, Indiana, between June 1876 and May 1880. He was a prosperous miller at Otter Creek Township — Tuttle’s Mills was the town of Ellsworth, and he was its postmaster — a county commissioner from 1858, worth $14,000 in land by 1860. Orphaned of his father at twelve, he was counted at nineteen in the household of his sister’s husband Henry Markle, married Harriet M. Sweany two weeks after coming of age in 1832, and named his first recorded son Gersham and a later son Chester. Four of his and Harriet’s children died young and lie at Otter Creek Union Cemetery; Harriet died in June 1851 and he married Nancy A. Herbert that July.

Archibald Bell — “Archy” to the clerks who wrote down his name — was born about 1770 to 1775, and if the reconstruction holds he was a son of William Rasor Bell, a planter of Salem in Sumter District who died leaving nine sons and a widow named Mary.

He farmed on both banks of Lynches Creek, the water that divides Sumter District from Darlington. He bought land in Darlington in 1806 and sold a hundred and twenty-seven acres there in 1817, between Bay Branch and Newmans Swamp; he was witnessing deeds across the creek in Sumter as early as 1812. His wife Martha released her dower twice, in 1809 and 1817, before a justice named John Huggins — whose family would later marry into his.

Martha died, and he married Margaret, and had five more children with names that read like a roll-call of his neighbours: Benjamin Sumter, Ezekiel Dixon, Hugh McCallum, Columbus Campbell. He was sued in 1830 for a witness fee. He was still buying land from Robert Severance in 1836, at sixty. He died in the autumn of 1839, weak in body but of sound mind, leaving two hundred and seventy-five acres, a set of blacksmith’s tools, thirteen geese, and a boy named Limus whom he directed to be sold to pay his debts.

Whether the John Bell he named first among his older children is the John Bell who went to Alabama about 1830 is the question this family has not yet answered.

Martha Bell appears twice, briefly. In October 1809, and again in February 1817, her husband Archibald sold land in Darlington District, South Carolina, and each time she came before a justice of the peace — the same man both times, John Huggins — who examined her privately and separately from her husband, as the law required, to be sure a wife was not signing away her dower under his eye.
