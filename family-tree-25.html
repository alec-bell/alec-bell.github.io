Gershom Tuttle I (1714 Wallingford – 1777 Claremont)

Ahnentafel #768 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gershom Tuttle was born at Wallingford, Connecticut, on 11 August 1714, the fifth child of Timothy Tuttle — the deacon who had cleared his own farm west of the river — and Thankful Doolittle. He grew up in the Cheshire parish, and as a young man he left Connecticut altogether for the Massachusetts frontier above Northfield, where his father held a proprietor’s right in one of the new townships. He lodged for a time at Northfield with his mother’s brother, the Reverend Benjamin Doolittle, who was minister there. In 1736, when he was already settled up in Hampshire County, his father signed the whole of that right over to him — given, the deed said, for “love, good will and fatherly affection.”

A year later, on 17 October 1737, he married Lois Allis in her own family’s town of Sunderland. By 1739–40 he was buying land back at Farmington, Connecticut. He had gone home with a whole colony of Cheshire people who settled on Chippen’s Hill in the remote parish of New Cambridge — the place that is now Bristol — and there he was prominent in church and town affairs. In October 1742 he put his name to the petition of the inhabitants of New Cambridge asking for a winter preacher; it went down as “Gerfhum Tuttle.” His children came in a steady file: Gershom in 1738, Oliver in 1739, Eunice in 1743, Hannah in 1745, Samuel in 1747, Huldah in 1749, and a daughter Lois about 1751.

He served in the French and Indian Wars, and the rank he carried out of them stayed with him for the rest of his life: he was Ensign Gershom Tuttle at Bristol in 1761 and still Ensign in 1770, and that is how his daughter Lois’s gravestone names him when she died on 5 January 1769 in her eighteenth year — “Lois Daugtr of En[s]n Garshum & Mrs Lois Tutle.”

After that he moved the family north a second time, to Claremont, New Hampshire, and this time he stayed. The spring of 1777 took his small grandson Daniel, Oliver’s boy, buried at four years old on 2 March. On 9 May Gershom made his will. He left his “wellbeloved Wife Lois” the use of a third of his estate, half his household goods to his daughter Eunice, wife of Abner Matthews, and the other half to his “Four motherless Grandchildren Hannah Gurdon Asa and Louisa Johnson,” and divided everything else between his three sons, Gershom, Oliver and Samuel, with his son Gershom as sole executor. Lois Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 13 January 1707/8, the second daughter of William Allis and his wife Elizabeth. While she was small the family moved up the Connecticut River to Sunderland, and it was there, in her father’s own parish, that she married on 17 October 1737. She was twenty-nine. Her husband, Gershom Tuttle, was a Connecticut man who had come north to take up land his father held in one of the new frontier townships.

They went back to Connecticut together, to the New Cambridge parish of Farmington on Chippen’s Hill, and the next twelve years were given over to children: Gershom in 1738, Oliver in 1739, Eunice in 1743, Hannah in 1745, Samuel in 1747, Huldah in 1749. A daughter born about 1751 was given her mother’s name. That younger Lois died on 5 January 1769, in her eighteenth year, and the stone raised over her at Bristol names both her parents — “Lois Daugtr of En[s]n Garshum & Mrs Lois Tutle.”

The Allis tie held across two generations. Her elder sister Mary had married Joseph Mitchell of the same valley, and in 1757 Mary’s daughter Tabitha married Lois’s son Gershom — the cousins married, and the same Massachusetts grandparents stand twice in this family.

She was still living in May 1777, when her husband made his will at Farmington and left “my wellbeloved Wife Lois” the use of a third of his estate for life. Where she died, and when, is not known. She is not buried beside him at Claremont, New Hampshire, and she is not in the Bristol burying ground that holds her daughter.

Joseph Mitchell (1705 Deerfield – 1794 Ashfield)

Joseph Mitchell was born in 1705, the third son of Michael Mitchell and Sarah Catlin, into a household that had come through the Deerfield raid of the year before untouched while his mother’s family was all but destroyed. He was bred a weaver, not a farmer.

On 2 November 1726 he married Mary Allis, daughter of William Allis of Sunderland; their first child, a son Joseph, was born at Sunderland four months later. Then came daughters — Hannah, who married Zadock King in 1751; Mary, married in 1753; Thankful; Lydia; and, on 9 February 1736/7, Tabitha, who in 1757 married Gershom Tuttle of Farmington and so carried this line forward. There was a son Abner as well. The family lived a few years at Sunderland, then moved to Deerfield, and after that up into the hills to the west, to Ashfield.

At Ashfield he kept the first house of public entertainment in the town — or his son of the same name did; nothing in that town’s books ever says which Joseph Mitchell is meant. From about 1763 the inn stood on the east side of Bellows Hill, and it was to “the house of Joseph Mitchell, innholder” that the voters were summoned for Ashfield’s first legal town meeting on 6 January 1766. At that meeting Joseph Mitchell was chosen constable, and the town went on meeting in his tavern until the meeting house was partly finished in January 1771. He was also one of the handful of men who signed the covenant when the church there was gathered. His daughter Mary’s husband, Samuel Belding, was the town’s first selectman and the man who carried the warrant round. Long afterwards people in Ashfield said the old Deacon Ziba Smith house was the Mitchell tavern, moved.

Mary died on 8 November 1783. Nine years later, on 14 April 1792, describing himself as of Deerfield, Joseph made his will. He gave twelve pounds each to his daughters Mary, wife of Samuel Belding, Hannah Bigelow, and “my Daughter Tabitha Wife of Gersham Tuttle”; eight shillings each to five grandchildren, the children of “my late Son Joseph”; and the land and the residue to his son Abner. He died on 16 September 1794 at Ashfield, and the will was proved at Northampton on 25 November.
