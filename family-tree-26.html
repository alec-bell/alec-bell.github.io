John Peck (~1638 – 1724 Wallingford Conn.)

John Peck was born about 1638, the son of Deacon William Peck of New Haven, the London merchant who had come over on the Hector. He grew up in the colony his father helped found, and became a freeman there in 1669. On 3 November 1664 he married Mary Moss, daughter of John Moss, in New Haven.

In 1668, two years before Wallingford was incorporated, he and Abraham Doolittle were already on the ground there, and John Peck settled in the new inland town and stayed. The first of his ten children were born at New Haven and the last six at Wallingford; the eldest, Mary, born 4 March 1666, married John Doolittle, so that the two men who had broken the ground together became grandfathers to the same children.

John Peck died at Wallingford in 1724, about eighty-six years old; Mary outlived him by a year, dying 16 November 1725 aged seventy-eight. They lie in the Center Street Cemetery, in a plot that is now the ninth stop on the town's walking tour, but no stone for either of them survives — none was standing when the burying ground was surveyed stone by stone in 1934, and the two markers standing in the plot today belong to a later John Peck and his wife.

William Allis (fl. 1640 Braintree – 1678 Hatfield)

Ahnentafel #6152 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Allis was born in England, probably between 1613 and 1616; the family tradition puts his birthplace in Essex or London and brings him over with Winthrop's fleet in 1630, but nothing outside the family's own telling confirms it. He is certain from 13 May 1640, when he was made a freeman of Braintree, Massachusetts, and the Braintree town book then carries him through the 1650s as a selectman, as “Deacon Allis”, and as a commissioner, with Thomas Meekins holding land next to his. This family descends from him through one of his sons, John or Samuel: each had a son named William at Hatfield, and which of those cousins was the Sunderland William is not yet settled.

In 1641 he married a woman named Mary, whose surname has not survived. Their children were born at Braintree: John in 1642, Samuel on 24 February 1647, two boys named Josiah, two named William, Hannah, and Mary. About 1661 he moved the family west to Hatfield on the Connecticut River.

There his title stops being a courtesy and becomes a command. When King Philip's War set the valley towns to garrisoning themselves, the commands were parcelled out: thirty-nine men under Pynchon at Springfield, twenty-nine at Westfield, twenty-six at Northampton, thirty at Hadley — and thirty-six at Hatfield, under Lieut. Wm. Allis, the largest force in the valley after Springfield's. Six more soldiers were ordered to him, to be employed through the winter getting out timber for the fortifications at Hatfield and Deerfield. In the same war, on 19 May 1676, his son William, aged twenty, was killed in the Falls Fight at Turners Falls.

His wife Mary died on 10 August 1677. On 25 June 1678 he married Mary, daughter of John Bronson and widow of John Graves of Haddam — and died ten weeks later, in September 1678. A Deerfield land redistribution of 1680 notes it in brackets, as a thing everyone in the valley knew: “[Lieut. Wm. Allis had died September, 1678.]”

William Davis was born about 1617 and lived at Roxbury, Massachusetts, where he was made a freeman in 1673. His children come into view in a single entry in the Roxbury church book in 1649, when the congregation received the children of a newly admitted member: “John Davis, Samuel Davis, Joseph Davis — Children of William Davis whose wife was admitted & so brought her children to p'take of this blessed ordinance.” Samuel, born 21 February 1645, is the son through whom this family descends, and the same church book marks his going: “Samuel Davis dismissed to North-Hampton”, when he went west up the Connecticut valley.

He married twice. His first wife was Elizabeth, who died or was buried on 4 May 1658. He married second, that October, Alice Thorp, and the Roxbury clerk wrote her end in one unsparing line: “Alice wife of William Davis who dyed in child birth undelivered.” He was probably a brother of Tobias Davis of the same town — a household that has to be kept carefully apart from his, because it held a Samuel too.

He made his will on 6 December 1683 and died three days later, on the 9th, aged about sixty-six. That the Samuel who settled at Northampton is the same boy brought to the ordinance at Roxbury in 1649 is probable rather than certain.

James Allen was a blacksmith at Kempston, a parish of scattered hamlets just south-west of Bedford, where he was baptised in 1586 and where he raised his family. Two of his children crossed the Atlantic: his son Roger, who became treasurer of the New Haven Colony, and his daughter Joan, the wife of Abraham Doolittle. He was buried at Kempston on 29 December 1657. His son William and William's daughter Elizabeth followed him to the churchyard within weeks, and Roger sailed home from New Haven to settle the estate, back again by August 1658.
