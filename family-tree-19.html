Samuel Allis (1647 Braintree – 1691 Hatfield)

Ahnentafel #3076 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Samuel Allis was born at Braintree, Massachusetts, on 24 February 1647. The town register enters him under the year 1646, because the year then began in March and February was reckoned the twelfth month: “Samuel, sonne of William Alyes & Mary his wife was Borne the 24th. of ye 12 month.” In the Old Style of the time the date is written 24 February 1646/7. His father, William Allis, was a Braintree freeman of 1640 who removed about 1661 up the Connecticut River to the new town of Hatfield.

Samuel followed him there and took his own home lot as a young man of twenty-two. On 4 February 1669 the town granted him a house lot sixteen rods wide next to Samuel Marsh’s, with land in the Plain above the great meadow and ten acres in the swamp by the Mill river — “provided he build a sufficient dwelling house upon his lot by or before the last of May in the year 1671, otherwise to forfeit his land.” Daniel Belden had the lot on the other side. He built, and he stayed: a carpenter and builder, living on the east side of Hatfield street.

About 1675–6 he married a woman named Alice, whose surname no record has ever given. Seven children followed: Mehitable on 2 July 1677; Samuel on 20 February 1679; William on 19 October 1680, from whom this family descends; Mary on 6 July 1682; Thomas on 12 March 1684; Sarah about 1685; and Rebecca on 29 November 1687.

Samuel Allis died at Hatfield on 9 March 1691, aged forty-four, leaving Alice with seven children all under fifteen. She remarried in 1696 and was killed in the raid on Deerfield of 29 February 1704; their son Samuel was killed in the same attack, and their daughter Mary was carried to Canada and survived to come home.

Alice (m. 1675/6 – killed 1704 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #3077 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice married Samuel Allis, the Hatfield carpenter, about 1675–6, and bore him seven children between 1677 and 1687 — Mehitable, Samuel, William, Mary, Thomas, Sarah and Rebecca. When he died on 9 March 1691 she was left with all of them under fifteen.

On 20 November 1696 she married again, to Sergeant John Hawks of Deerfield: a soldier of King Philip’s war who had been in the Falls Fight and was wounded going to the relief of Hatfield on 30 May 1676, and who had become a permanent settler at Deerfield in 1683. She had a daughter by him, Elizabeth, born about 1697.

On the morning of 29 February 1704 the French and their allies came over the Deerfield stockade. Alice was killed in the village. Her son Samuel Allis, twenty-five years old, was killed in the meadow. Her little daughter Elizabeth Hawks, six or seven, was taken and died on the march to Canada. Her daughter Mary Allis, twenty-one, was also carried off, survived the march, was redeemed, and came home to marry another survivor of the same raid, Nathaniel Brooks, in 1710. Her husband lived, but as Sheldon wrote of him, all his children and grandchildren of the name were lost that day; he ended his life at Waterbury, Connecticut, with the one daughter he had left. This family’s line runs through her third child, William, who was at Hatfield and not in Deerfield that night.

She lies with the rest of the dead of that morning in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, under a stone that reads: “The grave of 48 men women and children, victims of the French and Indian raid on Deerfield, February 29, 1704.” Her own family name was never written down. The 1919 Allis genealogy leaves a literal blank where the surname should go; Boltwood and Sheldon, both working from the Hampshire County records, call her only “Alice, widow of Samuel Allis.” She is remembered by her first name alone.

Samuel Davis (~1645 Roxbury – 1690 Northampton)

Ahnentafel #3078 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Samuel Davis was born about 1645, probably at Roxbury, Massachusetts — Savage calls him “perhaps son of William of the same” — and a Roxbury church record of 1649 lists a Samuel Davis among William Davis’s children. He removed up to Northampton in 1668 and was made a freeman there in 1676.

He kept choosing the exposed edge. Besides his Northampton land he held shares in the valley’s silver and lead mine venture, which he sold to Medad Pomeroy, and he put his name down as a subscriber to Harvard College. In 1682 he was a grantee of Northfield — a town that had already been burned twice — and in 1685 he was among the men who went back to rebuild it. In March 1676 his wife was one of a long list of Northampton women fined for wearing silk “in a flaunting manner.”

He married twice. His first wife is named in Northfield’s town history as Hannah Edwards, married 4 March 1668 and dead on 11 September 1680; his second was Elizabeth, daughter of William Smead, whom he married in 1681. Savage records two sons — Samuel, born 1672, and John, born 1677 — and three daughters whose names he does not give. Samuel Davis died at Northampton on 26 July 1690, in the epidemic that swept the town that summer.

He is on this chart as the father of Elizabeth Davis, who married William Allis of Hatfield in 1703–4. That link is probable rather than proved: he is the only Davis household of his generation in Northampton, which is where the Allis genealogy says Elizabeth came from, but no record yet names her as his daughter.

Mr. John Catlin (~1643 – killed 1704 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #3082 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Catlin was born about 1643, the son of a Catlin of Wethersfield, Connecticut, who died when the boy was an infant. He was of Wethersfield in 1662 — the year he married Mary Baldwin — and of Branford by 1665, and from there he went with the New Haven colony party that founded Newark in New Jersey. He was no farmer there. In 1676 he kept Newark's first school, in his own house, as the custom then was; the townsmen's vote engaged him to “do his faithful, honest and true endeavour to teach the children or servants of these as have subscribed … english and also arithmetick as much as they are capable to learn and he capable to teach them.” In 1678 he was the town's attorney — not a lawyer, but an “honest brother” charged to see that all town orders were executed and to punish any breach — and he was a selectman from 1676 to 1681.

About 1683 he moved north again, by way of Hartford, to the frontier town of Deerfield in Massachusetts, where he was one of the early permanent settlers. In 1685 he bought the Frary part of home lot 32, four acres on Deerfield street, and lot 33 from John Parsons in the same year; lot 36 was his as well by 1704 — three home lots on the one street. In the town records he is given the title “Mr.”, which in a New England town book is a rank and not a courtesy, and he took a prominent part in the town's affairs.

On the night of 28 February 1704 there were 291 people under Deerfield's roofs. Before the next morning was out, all but a hundred and twenty-six of them were dead or being marched through the snow towards Canada, three hundred miles away. No family in the town suffered more than John Catlin's. He and his son Jonathan were killed defending their house, which was then taken and burned with all his buildings; another son was killed later that morning in the Meadow Fight; a third son, John, and three of his daughters were carried off. Two of those daughters, Elizabeth, the wife of James Corse, and Mary, died on the march. The roll of the town's dead sets him down in four words: “John Catlin, (?) 60.”

The house that gave the raid its surviving relic still stood in Deerfield until 1848, and was photographed before it came down: Capt. John Sheldon's, built about 1698, which held out while half the village burned. Its front door, hacked through, is kept in Memorial Hall in the village to this day.

His line survived for one accidental reason. His daughter Sarah had married Michael Mitchell about 1694 and was in a household of her own that night. The captured son came home from Canada, and by about 1715 had built a new house on his father's ashes; he was a housewright, and was always known in Deerfield by tradition as “Master Catlin.” The forty-eight who died in the raid lie together in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, under a brownstone block raised over the common grave.

Mary Baldwin (bap. 1644 – aft. 1704)

Ahnentafel #3083 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Baldwin was baptized at Milford, Connecticut, on 23 June 1644 — the same day her mother Hannah joined the Milford church and the first four Baldwin children were all baptized together. Her father was Joseph Baldwin, one of the first settlers of the town. On 23 September 1662 she married John Catlin, and in a tangle that is rare even in a small colony she married the son of her own stepmother: her father's second wife, Isabella, was the widow of the elder John Catlin.

She went with her husband from Branford to Newark in New Jersey, where he kept the town's first school, and about 1683 to Deerfield on the Massachusetts frontier. They had ten children. Her father remembered her in his will of 20 December 1680: “I doe give to my Daughter Mary Cattlin … fortie shillings.”

On the morning of 29 February 1704 her husband and one son were killed defending their house at Deerfield and it was burned; another son was killed in the Meadow Fight; a son and three daughters were carried off towards Canada, and two of the daughters died on the march. Mary was taken with the other prisoners to the Sheldon house, the largest in the town, which the attackers had kept as a holding place for captives. A wounded French officer was brought in and laid on the floor, calling for water, and Mary Catlin fed him with water. Someone asked her how she could do that for her enemy. She answered, “If thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him water to drink.” The Frenchman was carried away, the captives were marched off, and Mrs. Catlin was left behind. Afterwards a little boy who had been hidden in the house came out, and she told him to run and hide. The boy said, “Mrs. Catlin, why don't you go and hide?” She said, “I am a captive; it is not my duty to hide, but you have not been taken, and it is your duty to hide.”

The story came down in her family and was written out a century later by a descendant, Lucy D. Shearer. Deerfield's historian printed it, and accepted its explanation of why she lived: this was the place “where Mrs. Catlin relieved the wounded French lieutenant, and secured her own freedom by her practical Christianity.” Her own people thought it was the water she gave the dying officer that left her standing in an emptied house. The house outlasted her by a century and a half — the “Old Indian House” stood until 1849, and its scarred and battered door is in Memorial Hall at Deerfield still. She did not long survive the raid, and she lies in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield.

William Tuttle (England → New Haven 1635 – d. 1673)

Ahnentafel #6144 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Tuttle came out of England on the ship Planter, which left the port of London in April 1635 and put into Boston about the first of July. He travelled with his wife Elizabeth, their first three children, and — in the same passage — two brothers and their households. The passenger list calls him a husbandman, a man who farmed his own ground. He had a merchant's interests too: a petition surviving in Boston shows him among the owners of the ketch Zebulon of Ipswich, asking the General Court for two guns to arm her for a voyage to the Indies. The court refused.

Two of his sons were baptized at Charlestown, Jonathan in 1637 and David in 1639, and by June of that year the family had moved to the new colony at Quinnipiac. On 4 June 1639 the planters met in Mr. Newman's barn and signed the church covenant that founded New Haven; William Tuttle's name stands on that list between Benjamin Ling and Ezekiel Cheever, near the head among the principal men. In the first division of land in 1640 he was rated at £100 of estate and seven persons in the family. In 1641 he held the home lot of Edward Hopkins, afterwards governor of Connecticut, and in 1656 he bought from Joshua Atwater a house, home lot and barn on the green. That became the Tuttle homestead, and the family held it for thirty years. Long after, the first building of Yale College was raised on the same ground.

He had land across the water at East Haven as well, enough of it that a stream there is still called Tuttle's Brook and the rise above it Tuttle's Hill. In 1651 he joined Jasper Crane and about fifty others in the New Haven colony's attempt to plant a settlement on the Delaware; the Dutch governor at Manhattan seized the party, held them prisoner and sent them home, and the petition of protest that followed — “the humble petition of Jasper Crane, William Tuttle and many others” — went to the commissioners of the United Colonies. The venture cost him money he never recovered, and after it he turned to farming and to town business.

At that business he was constant: fence viewer for Mr. Davenport's quarter in 1644, laying out allotments and settling boundaries; a juror again and again, his title of “Mr.” always carefully written, which in a New England town record is a rank and not a courtesy; sworn constable in March 1666-7; one of the commissioners who finally settled the line between Branford and New Haven in 1669 and between New Haven, Milford, Branford and Wallingford in 1672. When the meeting house was seated in 1646-7 — the seats graded by descent, public trust, piety and estate — Mr. Tuttle was voted into the first cross seat at the upper end, nearest the pulpit. He turns up in smaller things too. In 1646 he and Jeremy Watts were fined for falling asleep at the watch house. In 1664 a girl caught pilfering tried to drag his fourteen-year-old daughter Mercy into it; the charge collapsed, and before sentence William Tuttle asked the court, “with great affection,” to deal gently with the girl and place her in a pious family.

The town he helped found was drawn out, house by house, a lifetime after he died. A plan of New Haven ‘with all the buildings in 1748’, engraved in 1806 from General Wadsworth's survey, names every householder and his trade across the nine squares — and the Tuttle name is still scattered through it: a Widow Tuttle on the north road, a Noah Tuttle to the east, an Enos Tuttle, miller, by the harbour, and an Ab. Tuttle who was the sexton. The long hip-roofed building marked ‘Yale Colledge, Built 1717’ stands on the block beside the green where the family homestead had been.

He died suddenly in the first days of June 1673, leaving no will; his inventory was entered just after that of his neighbour Benjamin Ling, taken on the sixth. A deed he had agreed to was found unsigned. His widow Elizabeth took out the administration herself, and his Branford division lands were still being parcelled out to his children on 14 February 1705, thirty-two years after he died — his son Simon's deed of that year conveys “all my right in Branford division set to my father, Mr. William Tuttle, now (Feb. 14, 1705) laid out for his children.” The last remainder of the estate was not divided until 1709, thirty-six years after his death, when it was split into eleven parts and a grandson appealed the division to Hartford. He lies somewhere under the Old Green at New Haven; the exact spot is not known. Among his descendants is his great-grandson Jonathan Edwards, who studied and taught on the very ground where William Tuttle's house had stood.

Elizabeth Tuttle (~1609 England – 1684 New Haven)

Ahnentafel #6145 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Elizabeth Tuttle was born in England about 1609. She was about twenty-six when she crossed to New England in April 1635 on the ship Planter with her husband William and their first three children — John, Hannah and Thomas — in a party that also held her husband's two brothers and their families and his widowed mother. They landed at Boston about the first of July, and a year later, on 24 July 1636, “Mrs. Elizabeth Tuttell” united with the church in Boston, where she brought two sons to be baptized: Jonathan in July 1637 and David in April 1639. Within weeks of that second baptism the family had gone to the new plantation at Quinnipiac, which became New Haven.

She bore twelve children and, in a century when that was rare, every one of them lived to grow up: John, Hannah, Thomas, Jonathan, David, Joseph, Sarah, Elizabeth, Simon — from whom this family descends — Benjamin, Mercy and Nathaniel. Her own standing in the town is legible in the dullest of records. When New Haven seated its meeting house in 1646-7, the seats graded by rank, Mrs. Tuttle was placed in the fifth seat in the middle among the wives of the leading men; in 1655-6 in the fourth long seat; in 1661-2 the clerk simply wrote “Sisters Miles, Peck, Lindon, Tuttle and Gibbard.”

Her husband died suddenly in June 1673, leaving no will. Elizabeth took out the administration herself — at a court in June 1674 she appeared to confirm a sale he had made, and is entered as “widow and relict and lawful administratrix,” signing with her mark. She stayed on at the homestead on the green, where she had lived since 1656, and kept it another eleven years; in all she lived there twenty-eight.

The widowhood was hard. In November 1676 her daughter Sarah, married to John Slauson of Stamford, died — struck down, it was afterwards said, by her own younger brother Benjamin after a quarrel. Sarah was dead on 17 November 1676; Benjamin, unmarried, made over his property to his brother-in-law Richard Edwards on 18 June 1677, and was hanged at New Haven that same month.

Elizabeth spent her last years with her youngest son, Nathaniel, and died on the last day of 1684. The will she left was presented to the New Haven court on 28 July 1685 and refused: “the other children objected and the court would not allow it.” Her inventory, taken that February, was thought by a later reader to show “a ladylike refinement in apparel and household appointments.” Her gravestone, cut with the old-style date “Age 76: 31:10:1684 — Elisabeth Tutle” (month ten being December under the old calendar), was carried with the others from the Old Green to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821 and stands today in the row along the north wall. Part of its epitaph has crumbled away, but it was copied before it went: “The blest live as They are: the blest That live at Rest.”
