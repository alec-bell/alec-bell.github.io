William Allis (1680 Hatfield – 1763 Montague)

Ahnentafel #1538 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 19 October 1680, the third child of Samuel Allis, the carpenter and builder of Hatfield street, and his wife Alice. His father died when he was ten; his mother remarried and moved six miles up the road to Deerfield, and on the morning of 29 February 1704 she was killed there in the French and Indian raid, along with his brother Samuel. His sister Mary was carried off toward Canada and came home again. William was twenty-three, and he was not in Deerfield that night.

He had married Elizabeth Davis of Northampton in 1703 or 1704. Their children were Mary, born 18 February 1705, and Lois, born 13 January 1707/8, both at Hatfield; Eliphalet in 1710, also at Hatfield; then Zebediah in 1713 and Elizabeth in 1716, both at Sunderland — so the household moved upriver between the end of 1710 and the autumn of 1713. At Sunderland he appraised a neighbour’s estate in 1739 and deeded land to his son Zebadiah. Later the whole family moved again, across the river to Montague, where he was “Mr. William Allis”, a title of standing in a New England town. His wife died there on 1 May 1758. Montague could be a hard place to live: in January 1759 his son Eliphalet and his wife Mary buried four of their children in nine days — Mary on the 7th, Jonathan on the 10th, Samuel on the 12th, David on the 15th.

William Allis died at Montague on 20 February 1763, aged eighty-two. He stands in this family’s pedigree twice over: his daughter Lois married Gershom Tuttle in 1737, his daughter Mary married Joseph Mitchell in 1726, and in 1757 Mary’s daughter married Lois’s son, so two of his children’s lines run down to the same descendants.

Michael Mitchell (Wallingford Conn. → Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #1540 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Michael Mitchell was a Wallingford, Connecticut man — a proprietor of the town, casting lots with the other townsmen in 1695–6 and still there in 1706. Where he came from before that nobody has ever been able to say. About 1694 he married Sarah, daughter of John Catlin of Deerfield.

He moved his family to Deerfield, the most exposed town on the Massachusetts frontier. On the morning of 29 February 1704, when the French and their allies came over the stockade, his whole family escaped unharmed — which in that town, on that morning, was the exception. His wife’s father and brother were burned in their own house a few hundred yards away.

His children were William, born 1697; Michael, born 8 March 1700/1, afterwards a soldier in Father Rasle’s war and later of Wethersfield, Connecticut; Joseph, born 1705, the weaver, through whom this family descends; and Samuel, born 7 March 1712, very probably the Samuel Mitchell granted land at Sunderland in 1737 on condition that he stayed five years. Sarah died on 30 December 1736.

His Wallingford years matter more than they look. The Tuttles were farming west of the river at Wallingford in exactly those years, and Gershom Tuttle was born there in 1714 — so when Michael’s granddaughter Tabitha married Gershom’s son at Deerfield in 1757, it was not a chance meeting a hundred miles from home. It was two Wallingford families who had both gone north, marrying each other.

Sarah Catlin (m. ~1694 – d. 1736)

Ahnentafel #1541 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Catlin was a daughter of John Catlin — a Wethersfield man who taught school at Newark in New Jersey, served as that town’s attorney and five years its selectman, and then brought his family to Deerfield about 1683, where he was dignified with the title of “Mr.” — and of his wife Mary Baldwin. About 1694 she married Michael Mitchell, and her eldest child was born in 1697.

That marriage is probably why she lived. On 29 February 1704, when the raid came, she was under her own husband’s roof, and the Mitchell household came through untouched. Her father’s did not. His buildings were all burned and he and his son Jonathan were consumed with them; another son was killed in the Meadow Fight later the same morning; a son and three daughters were carried off toward Canada. Two of those daughters were her sisters — Elizabeth, wife of James Corse, and Mary, wife of Thomas Wells — and both were killed on the march. In a town where a hundred and twelve people were marched away in a single morning, it was said of her father that “no family suffered more than his.”

She bore at least four sons — William, Michael, Joseph and Samuel — and died on 30 December 1736, about two months before her granddaughter Tabitha was born. She lies in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, in the same ground as the common grave of the dead of 1704, her father and her brother among them; time and weather have taken the place of her own grave.

Simon Tuttle (1647 – 1719 Wallingford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #3072 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Simon Tuttle was baptised at New Haven on 28 March 1647, the ninth child of William Tuttle, the immigrant, and his wife Elizabeth. He was bred a cooper. When Wallingford was laid out in 1670 he put his name on the proprietors’ list and was assigned lot No. 13 with eight acres of out-land, but he never took the allotment up; he stayed on at New Haven, where he was still drawing his share in the third division in 1680. That list credits him with “two heads” — a wife, and no children yet — so he had married Abigail about 1679 or 1680. He was of New Haven through 1683 and of Wallingford from 1685, moving, it seems, after his mother died in December 1684.

At Wallingford he did well. He drew lot No. 52 with a hundred and four acres in 1686 and lot No. 14 in 1690, was rated at £79 on the grand list of 1701, and from 1708–9 served the town as gauger and packer, the officer who inspects goods sold by weight and measure. He spent thirty years selling off what his father had left at New Haven, and he named him in the papers every time: in 1677–8, land sold to a cordwainer that “my father, Mr. William Tuttle, bought of John Punderson”; in 1686, six acres “formerly belonging to my father, Mr. William Tuttle”; in 1705, to his cousin Joshua, all his right in the Branford division “set to my father, Mr. William Tuttle.” In 1673 he and five of his brothers signed a right-of-way agreement; the paper, with his signature on it, was still in existence two hundred years later.

He gave land to his sons in his own lifetime, as his son Timothy would in turn: nine and a half acres to Timothy on 10 July 1711, land “I bought of Roger Tyler,” and a fourteen-acre house lot to Daniel on 23 June 1715. Simon Tuttle died on 16 April 1719 at Wallingford, and is said to lie in the Center Street burying ground there. His widow Abigail outlived him by three years and died on 11 August 1722.

Alice (m. 1675/6 – killed 1704 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #3077 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice married Samuel Allis, the Hatfield carpenter, about 1675–6, and bore him seven children between 1677 and 1687 — Mehitable, Samuel, William, Mary, Thomas, Sarah and Rebecca. When he died on 9 March 1691 she was left with all of them under fifteen.

On 20 November 1696 she married again, to Sergeant John Hawks of Deerfield: a soldier of King Philip’s war who had been in the Falls Fight and was wounded going to the relief of Hatfield on 30 May 1676, and who had become a permanent settler at Deerfield in 1683. She had a daughter by him, Elizabeth, born about 1697.

On the morning of 29 February 1704 the French and their allies came over the Deerfield stockade. Alice was killed in the village. Her son Samuel Allis, twenty-five years old, was killed in the meadow. Her little daughter Elizabeth Hawks, six or seven, was taken and died on the march to Canada. Her daughter Mary Allis, twenty-one, was carried off too, survived the march, was redeemed, and came home to marry another survivor of the same raid, Nathaniel Brooks, in 1710. Her husband lived — but every child and grandchild of his name was lost that day, and he ended his life at Waterbury, Connecticut, with the one daughter he had left. This family’s line runs through her third child, William, who was at Hatfield and not in Deerfield that night.

She lies with the rest of the dead of that morning in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, under a stone that reads: “The grave of 48 men women and children, victims of the French and Indian raid on Deerfield, February 29, 1704.” Her own family name was never written down. Those who afterwards gathered up Hampshire County’s records could call her nothing but “Alice, widow of Samuel Allis”, and she is remembered by her first name alone.
