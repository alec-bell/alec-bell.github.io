Emanuel Nease (~1792 Va. – 1853/54 Mason Co.)

Ahnentafel #248 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emanuel Nease was born in Virginia about 1792, a son of Henry Nease, who on one spring day in 1817 gave him a hundred-acre farm in Mason County “for the natural love and affection which I bear” him and gave his brother Henry the farm next to it. Emanuel farmed in Mason County, on the Virginia bank of the Ohio, with his wife Katharine. In 1850 he was fifty-eight and she fifty-three, living next door to the Roushes. On 4 October 1853 he signed his will in his own hand. Every one of his sons, it says, had been given a horse, four sheep and two hogs on reaching twenty-one, and he left the same to his youngest, Levi, so that he would not be short; everything else went to Katharine, and after her to all his children, “sons and daughters Share and Share alike without any differance whatsoever.” He was dead by July 1854. In March 1865, a month before the war ended, his children met to sign over the home farm to the youngest, Levi, keeping their mother’s right to live on it for the rest of her life. George Nease signed among them, “as Heirs at law of Emanuel Nease deceased”. That deed settles whose son George was, though George’s own death record, filled in thirty-four years later, would call his father Samuel.

Anthony Roush (b. 1792 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #250 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Anthony Roush was born on 23 January 1792 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, near Solomon’s Church, and was baptised there on 18 March 1792 with Michael Levermore standing sponsor. He was the seventh child of Henry Roush and his wife Dorothy — a Henry who at twenty-three had served eighty-eight days as a private in Captain John Tipton’s company and been paid off at Romney in October 1775, and for whom his younger brother Jonas later stood substitute.

The family came down into the Ohio Valley, and Anthony with them. On 11 July 1812, when he was twenty, Henry Roush sold him seventy-five acres for three hundred dollars. About 1810 he had married Elizabeth Rickard, a sister of the Rickard brothers of that neighbourhood, and in 1820 and 1830 an Anthony Roush with a young family was living across the river at Letart, Ohio, among the Roush households there. Whether that was he is not yet settled. After Elizabeth he married Catherine Hoffman, in Mason County on 11 January 1837; she bore him Moses and Phoebe and was dead within four years, and on 21 March 1841 he married a third time, Lydia Pounds, probably the Lydia, born in New York, who was fifty-seven and his wife in 1850. Young men and women named Pound were living with them in 1860, probably her children. He had a long family, and Nancy, born on 16 February 1832, was the eleventh child; she married George Nease in 1849, at seventeen, and the consent for it carries the X mark of an Anthony Roush. In 1850 he was fifty-nine, a farmer who could not write his name, living in Mason County with a wife named Lydia, born in New York, and sharing his house with his son Henry and Henry’s young family.

He fell heir, in all likelihood, to a share of his father’s portion of the six-thousand-acre estate in Graham District, Mason County — land that had been fought over in the courts since a petition of 1806.

He lies in the Rickard cemetery, in the White Church community of Mason County, under a small stone with no epitaph cut on it.

William Rasor Bell appears out of nowhere in 1784, already styled planter, in Claremont County of old Camden District, South Carolina. He is here as the father of Archy Bell of Sumter, and that is the one step on this branch that nothing asserts — his will names a son Archibald, but no paper says that the Archibald of the will is the Archy who died in 1839.

In June 1784 he helped appraise Isaac Higgs’s estate in Kershaw District. In April 1787 he bought Micajah Stokes’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle, for £105 15s — and the very next day began giving it all back to Stokes’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in that century usually means family. Ann was conveyed twice in two days between two households that were probably kin; she is the only person he is known ever to have held, and he held her for a day. His own household in 1790 was ten people and no one in slavery.

He took two surveys in 1785 — a hundred and fifty acres on Bare Branch in April, four hundred on Lynches Creek in June — and the grants on both issued to Samuel Boykin, who had paid the treasury fee, as was the local habit. His land lay on two watersheds at once: a later grant describes eighty acres “on Charleston Road between Black River and Lynches Creek, bounded N.W. by William Rasor Bell and Fred:k Bells Land,” and a 1787 grant to Frederick Bell recites its north-east line as “Land Surveyed for Wm Rasor Bell, Frederick Bell, Jonathan Newman and Lewis Mathews” — one line of one document carrying three surnames that stay beside this family for three generations.

April 1784 To Mr. William Bell for Eleven pounds Eight Shillings and Seven pence Sterling for two Horses impressed for Militia Use in October 1780.” It is the only William Bell indent in the state that no man has ever been attached to, and no lineage society claims it; whether the two horses were his, or one of at least six other South Carolina William Bells’, nobody can say.

Ahnentafel #290 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

He was, if his family's own story is true, a small boy found alive on the North Carolina coast sometime between 1735 and 1745 after a shipwreck. He could tell them only that his name was Billie Pigford and that he had sailed from England with his parents, who were never found. A Dutch family took him in. In March 1740 or 1741 the county court dealt with him formally: Rebecca Perry petitioned to be allowed to keep the boy herself, and the court bound him instead to Benjamin Fussell. He was called Lost Billie for the rest of his life, and his great-granddaughter was still telling the story in 1896.

Whatever he came from, he ended a landowner on both banks of Sill's Creek, in the Penderlea country where New Hanover County runs up against Duplin — and in 1790 his was the only Pigford household in the whole state. He married a woman named Sarah, remembered in the family only as “a woman of Dutch descent,” and they raised a large family: William, Jacob, Timothy, Solomon, Edward, Martha and Margaret, and probably a daughter Elizabeth, who married an Eakins of Moores Creek.

On 27 March 1801 he had his will written out. It gives Sarah all the household furniture and moveable effects and everything else not otherwise left, “to be by her disposed of amongst her children as she may think proper,” together with the plantation he lived on and all the land east of Sill's Creek for the term of her life. Timothy got the land on the west side of the creek. Solomon got the home place after his mother's death — and in return was bound to help his brother Timothy build “a dwelling house, kitchen, corn crib, smoke house, dairy, and in clearing ten acres of land.” To the rest of the children he left one shilling sterling each. The filed original ends with his name written out, “William Pigford (Seal),” witnessed by William Jones; a later transcription that gives him an X and “his mark” is not borne out by the paper.

He died before March 1803, when the New Hanover Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions proved the will — and had to do it the slow way, since the witness himself was not there to swear to it: William Bloodworth stood up instead and swore that the signature was in William Jones's own hand. Both the Sons and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as a patriot for signing a 1779 petition from the inhabitants of New Hanover, Duplin and Bladen counties, asking that the county be divided so that militiamen could reach their musters; in 1779, though, there were two William Pigfords in the county, father and son, and which of them put his name to that sheet is not known. He and Sarah lie somewhere in the Penderlea area in unmarked graves.

Sarah Pigford (fl. 1801 New Hanover Co. NC)

Sarah was the wife of William “Lost Billie” Pigford of Sill's Creek, in the Penderlea country of New Hanover County, North Carolina. Family tradition, set down about 1896, says only that Lost Billie “married a woman of Dutch descent.” Her own surname was never written down anywhere that survives — the family's historian hunted through the marriage records of both New Hanover and Duplin counties for it and wrote in 1967 that “her family name continues to be beyond the reach of this recorder.”

They had a large family: William, Jacob, Timothy, Solomon, Edward, Martha, Margaret, and a daughter Elizabeth, who married an Eakins.

Her husband's will of 27 March 1801 begins with her, and it trusts her with everything: “First; I give and bequeath to Sarah, my dearly beloved wife, all my household furniture, moveable effects and all other of my estate… to be by her disposed of amongst her children as she may think proper; and also the plantation whereon I now live, with all the improvements thereon; and all the land I possess adjoining the east side of Sill's Creek, for the term of her life.” She was named executrix. Her son Solomon was to have the home place only after her death, and until then was under orders to help his brother Timothy put up a house. She is believed to lie in an unmarked grave in the Penderlea area, near the old Pigford burying ground.
