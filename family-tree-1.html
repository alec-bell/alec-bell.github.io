Robert Lee Bell Sr. (1935 Atlanta – 2001 Chattanooga)

Ahnentafel #4 · his father’s father (2 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Robert Lee Bell Sr. was born on 5 November 1935, the fourth child of John Henry Bell, a house painter, and Margaret Beatrice Eaken. His family was moving between Birmingham and Atlanta in the very year he was born, and the records never agreed about which city it had been: the Social Security file says Birmingham, his own obituary says Atlanta, and the 1940 census — which found him at four years old and gave the household’s 1935 address as Atlanta — says Georgia. He may not have been certain himself.

His father walked out about 1942, when he was six, and never came back. The family went north to Wheaton, Illinois, where in January 1944 his eldest brother Thomas was killed in a car crash at sixteen; the death notice in the Birmingham papers lists “Bobby, 8” among the brothers left.

He enlisted in the United States Air Force on 4 February 1957, at twenty-one, and served nine years and ten days, to 14 February 1966, as an Airman Third Class. The service note on his veteran’s record names two wars — Korea and Vietnam — and the marker later cut for him credits him with service in both branches, as A3 in the Air Force and as a staff sergeant in the Army. Not one word of any of it appears in the family’s own papers; it was recoverable only because he was commemorated in a national cemetery.

He married Willie Joyce Stephens at Fort Payne in August 1960, three and a half years into his enlistment. Their first son was born at Birmingham in 1961 — the Birmingham News printed the birth, giving the parents as “Mr. and Mrs. Robert Lee Bell (Willie Joyce Stephens)” and the second at Fort Worth, Texas, in 1964 — Carswell Air Force Base is at Fort Worth, which is why an Alabama family was in Texas at all. So the first six years of that marriage were service years, lived at postings, with a husband twice overseas. It ended about 1972–73, and he married again afterwards. In January 1983 his mother’s obituary placed him at Mobile, Alabama, named among her four surviving children.

The later years were not quiet ones. On 20 November 1989 police in Blount County, Tennessee, searched a pair of massage businesses called Sue Lynn’s Health Studio and the 129 Health Studio, a mobile home and a dumpster, and he was charged with running them as brothels. In March 1990 he was convicted on five counts of promoting prostitution, for offences between 1987 and 1989, and sentenced to five consecutive two-year terms — ten years — and a $3,000 fine. The appeals court, affirming every part of it in August 1991, described an operation reaching at least seven establishments across several states, from Sevierville and Chattanooga to Fort Lauderdale, Florida; it noted an earlier guilty plea to possessing cocaine, that he had declined to give the probation officers an employment history, and it upheld the consecutive terms on the finding that he had made crime a major source of his livelihood. The Tennessee prison record for Robert Bell, born 5 November 1935 — six foot two, 170 pounds, blue eyes, grey hair — shows the sentence running out on 29 April 1999. It is the likeliest reason he ended his life in Chattanooga.

He died at Chattanooga on 14 February 2001, aged sixty-five — thirty-five years to the day after his discharge. Four photographs survive from his last years: beside a Tennessee lake in winter, deep-sea fishing in a straw hat, a plain portrait in a blue polo shirt, and one taken in a spring yard about 2000 or 2001, where he stands with his son, his daughter-in-law and his two grandsons. His name is cut on a white marble marker in the memorial section at Chattanooga National Cemetery, Section MA, site 36, beneath a cross and the words IN MEMORY OF.

Willie Joyce Stephens (1942 York – 1982 Jefferson Co.)

Ahnentafel #5 · his father’s mother (2 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Willie Joyce Stephens was born on 19 March 1942, the daughter of Jess Steve Willard Stephens, a mechanic who went by “Willard” and “J. W.,” and Grace Estelle Truett of Coffeeville in Clarke County. She was a native of York, her father’s Sumter County town. Her name is in a Birmingham high-school graduating-class list printed on 27 May 1960, and three months later, on 26 August, aged eighteen, she married Robert Lee Bell at Fort Payne, in DeKalb County; the clerk wrote her father down as “J. W. Stephens.” Her first son was born eleven months later, in 1961, and a second followed in 1964 at Fort Worth. Her husband left the family about 1972 or 1973.

On 24 May 1980 she married again, to John Duncan Lammon, in the same town where she had married the first time. It was a home wedding, not a church one — a hired brass arch dressed with pink and blue flowers set up in somebody’s living room, white tapers in candelabra on either side, the bride in a full-length ivory gown with long sheer sleeves and the groom in a pale blue three-piece suit. She was thirty-eight.

By the end she was living at Jackson, in Clarke County, where she kept her own clothing shop. She died on Tuesday 27 July 1982, aged forty. The Birmingham News carried the notice the next day: her husband survived her, and so did her mother and stepfather, Charles L. and Grace Roberts of Birmingham, and two sons and a daughter. The funeral was at Latham Funeral Chapel that same day. She was taken south-west to Clarke County — the country her mother’s Truett people came from — and buried at Mount Gilead Cemetery, which is the Lammon family’s ground, not the Truetts’. Her second husband lies there, and so does the Lammon generation before him. Her stone calls her simply JOYCE STEPHENS LAMMON, and under the dates it carries Micah 6:8: “He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good: and what doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God.” In the lower corner, in smaller letters, someone had cut two more words: AN ANGEL.

For years she could not be found at all, because she did not die a Bell. Three photographs of her survive, all from her last two years: her wedding day; a restaurant above the water with her husband’s arm around her shoulders; and one of her alone in a panelled room hung with framed stamp collections. They are the only known pictures of her.

Richard Clark Tuttle (1929 Terre Haute – 2015 Cincinnati)

Ahnentafel #6 · his mother’s father (2 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Richard Clark Tuttle, known all his life as Dick, was born in 1929 in Terre Haute, Indiana, the second son of Gifford Herman Tuttle and Helen May Knott, into a family that had been in and around Terre Haute since the 1850s. The earliest photograph of him shows him at about nine, in a sweater and short trousers, standing beside his older brother Robert on a patch of spring grass. When he was ten the household also held his grandmother Martha — a woman he grew up under the same roof with, and whom his own daughter would later say she knew nothing at all about.

About 1944 or 1945, when he was fifteen, his father moved the family north to Evanston, Illinois — the moment this branch left Indiana after nearly a century. He graduated from Evanston Township High School in the class of 1947; the family still has his own copy of the yearbook, The Evanstonian of ’47, with his name printed in the corner. He took classes at Northwestern.

He enlisted in the United States Army and was certified at Fort Lee, Virginia, on 3 August 1951 as a quartermaster supply-records specialist. He served in occupied Germany: a photograph kept in the family shows him in uniform beside the sign for the Headquarters, Heilbronn Sub Post — the only evidence of where he was actually stationed.

At the end of 1952 he was a soldier in Germany, and the Terre Haute paper that announced his grandparents' golden wedding noted that he was sending his congratulations from there. His father's death notice in December 1974 named him simply as Richard C., one of two sons.

Back in Evanston he met Judith Frost, who had come up from Marion, Indiana, to take a degree at Northwestern; they were introduced, the family says, through a friend. They married in the middle fifties and in 1959, expecting their first child, bought a house on Dartmouth Avenue in Deerfield, Illinois. He worked for a Chicago label printer until the owner of the firm killed himself with carbon monoxide, and in 1971 he moved the family to Cincinnati to take a job with H. S. Crocker Co., another label company. He lived out his life there and died in Cincinnati in 2015, two years after Judith.

Judith Katharine Frost (1934 Parkersburg – 2013 Cincinnati)

Ahnentafel #7 · his mother’s mother (2 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Judith Katharine Frost was born on 5 July 1934 at Parkersburg, West Virginia, the only child of Alan Chalmers Frost, who managed dime stores, and Velma Gladys Roush, a Mason County girl who had run away from home at sixteen. A studio photograph of about 1936 survives of her at two and a half, grinning in a knitted sweater-dress, ankle socks and white lace-up shoes.

Her father’s work moved them. By 1940 the family was renting on Stocking Road in Mansfield, Ohio, for thirty dollars a month; by 1950 they were at Marion, Indiana, where he ran the S. S. Kresge store seventy hours a week. In March 1947, when she was twelve, her mother took her to Akron to stay with her aunt Florence, and the two of them went on to Mason, West Virginia, to the bedside of her grandmother Bertha, who lay ill at home; another aunt came up from Georgia to join them. The Marion paper printed it as a social note.

She graduated from Marion High School and went on to Earlham College and to Northwestern, and it was at Evanston that she met Richard Clark Tuttle. They took out a licence in Cook County on 26 June 1957 and were married at three o’clock on Saturday 29 June in St. Luke’s Chapel, Evanston. A photograph of her at a college formal in the early 1950s — a dark floor-length gown, a young man beside her in a dark suit with a white boutonnière — belongs to the few years before that.

They bought a house on Dartmouth Avenue in Deerfield, Illinois in 1959, and moved to Cincinnati in 1971 when Richard took a job with the label printer H. S. Crocker. There were four children. She taught at the Indian Hill Schools — “a positive early influence for hundreds of small children,” her obituary said — and she was a portrait painter and a maker of very fine dollhouse miniatures. To her grandchildren she was Mimi.

She died at Cincinnati on 31 October 2013, after fifty-six years of marriage. Her funeral was held on 5 November at St. Barnabas Episcopal Church on Montgomery Road.

John Henry Bell (1901/02 AL – 1944/56)

Ahnentafel #8 · his father’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Henry Bell was born on 4 August 1901 in Barbour County, Alabama — a date his family still remembered in 2009, and one he wrote down himself on a draft card in 1942. He was the eldest son of Thomas Jefferson Bell and Alice May Bradley, farmers near Louisville, and he appears in their household in 1910 and 1920 simply as “Henry.”

By 1925 he had left for Birmingham, and a Barbour County court recorded him there — “Henry Bell, Birmingham” — when his grandfather’s estate was divided seven ways among Tom’s children. Two years later, on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927, he married Margaret Beatrice Eaken. Both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date was worth a humorous column. Their first son arrived six months later, and he named the boy Thomas J., for his own father.

He drove a truck for a dry cleaner, then painted houses — in Birmingham, and in Atlanta through the middle thirties. There were six children. The second, a daughter named Marion, was born and lost in 1932 and buried at Forest Hill in Birmingham — his wife’s ground, and the one family burying place he would not be laid in either. In June 1940 he was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support, and served four months of a twelve-month sentence; the prison ledger records him at 163 pounds, physical condition good, teeth bad.

He was released on 17 October 1940 by order of Judge Emmett Perry, and about a year later he left for good. On 23 February 1942 he registered for the draft in Cincinnati — five foot nine, 160 pounds, blue eyes, brown hair, a knot on his left leg near the hip — and gave, as the person who would always know his address, his mother in Dothan. It is the last document he is known to have signed.

After that he vanishes. His family moved north to Wheaton, Illinois, where his sixteen-year-old son was killed in a car crash in January 1944; the certificate names the father simply as “Henry Bell.” By the time his mother died in December 1956 her obituary listed six surviving children, and he was not among them. He lies in neither family cemetery — not at Forest Hill with his wife and his baby daughter, and not at Bethlehem with his parents. The likeliest trace of him is a Henry Bell, fifty-one, white, born in Alabama and recorded as “separated,” lodging in a home for the homeless outside Cincinnati in April 1950 — the only man of that description in the entire county.

Margaret Beatrice Eaken (1908 Sumter AL – 1983 Fulton GA)

Ahnentafel #9 · his father’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Margaret Beatrice Eaken was born on 10 July 1908 at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama — her mother's home ground, the small town on the Mississippi line where her father would be buried fourteen years later. She was the eldest child of Andrew Jackson Eaken and Maggie Dillon Swain, and she appears aged one in the 1910 census of Mobile, where her father had found work and her young uncle Langford Swain was lodging with them.

She was eighteen when she married John Henry Bell on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927; both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date worth a humorous paragraph. Their first son was born that November. There were six children; a daughter, Marion, died in infancy in 1932 and was buried at Forest Hill. In 1940 her husband was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support and served four months, and about 1942 he left for good.

By January 1944 she had taken the family north to Wheaton, Illinois, where her mother and stepfather were living, and there her eldest son Thomas Jackson — sixteen, and named for her brother — was killed in a car crash. She remarried in 1948, to Claude B. Smith, and the 1950 census finds her at Lineville, Clay County, Alabama, with her four surviving Bell children in the household.

Her later years were spent in Georgia. In July 1962, when her stepfather died, she was living at Fitzgerald in south Georgia and the notice called her “Mrs. Beatrice E. Smith”; her three brothers were then at Providence, Rhode Island, in Germany with the Army, and at Birmingham. Later she was at Clarkston outside Atlanta, where she retired as a saleswoman at Rich's department store and belonged to Vestavia Alliance Church. A cousin who had known her all her life remembered that she “always had a larger-than-life personality”; her grandson remembered “such a refined lady.” She died on Sunday 16 January 1983, aged seventy-four, and was brought back to Birmingham to lie at Forest Hill, in Section 19, near the infant daughter she had buried half a century before. Her flat granite marker reads MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10, 1908 — JAN. 16, 1983, GONE TO REST. Obituaries ran in both cities, naming her husband, her four children, her mother and her two brothers; neither version mentioned John Henry Bell.

Her grave is marked under her second married name: a flat granite stone carved with praying hands and a flower, 'MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10 1908 - JAN 16 1983, GONE TO REST'. A snapshot survives of her out in the cold in a dark coat, sitting on a wooden trestle in a bare winter yard with an iron fence behind her.

For a long time the family believed she died in 1985. 1985 was the year her own mother died, and the two had quietly become confused.
