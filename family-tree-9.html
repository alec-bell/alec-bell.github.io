Laney Bell (~1806 SC – 1870/80 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #65 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

She was born in South Carolina about 1806 and came to Alabama as a young wife. In August 1840 she is a tally mark in a column — one woman aged thirty to forty in John Bell’s house in Barbour County, with a baby boy and a little girl. In 1850 she has a name, Laney, and five children. In 1860 she is fifty-three, and the family is prosperous: her husband returns four thousand dollars in land and ten thousand in personal estate. In 1870 she is sixty-four, keeping house, with her grown daughter Mary and her son Archibald still at home, and her son John T. Bell farming the next place along with a wife and four small children.

Three different enumerators in three different hands wrote her name down, and all three wrote Laney. The same 1860 man who wrote her name wrote “S. Carolina” in the birthplace column of the same line, so the capital in her name is his L and not his C.

Then she is gone. By June 1880 her husband is enumerated as a widower, living with a lodger and a girl of fourteen, his children grown and scattered. A transcription of the Beasley family’s little burying ground reads “Bell, Laney (1803 – 6-17-1873)”, which falls inside the window the censuses allow — but no one has photographed the stone, and the plot is a rail-fenced square of a dozen markers standing in second growth on the farm that bounded her husband’s.

Fifty-one years after she died, her eldest son’s death certificate called her Viney. Three census-takers had called her Laney. Nobody now alive knows which she answered to.

John Eakins Sr. (~1795 New Hanover NC – aft. 1880)

Ahnentafel #72 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Eakins was born about 1795 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, the eldest son of Joseph Eakins and the woman who was probably Elizabeth Pigford. He was about ten when his father died in the first months of 1805, leaving a widow, a houseful of small children and four hundred acres on Moores Creek, and an estate the county court had to superintend for the next fourteen years.

That estate paid for his upbringing. In February 1816 it was charged $3.83 for schooling Joseph and William Eakins and $10.50 for boarding them; his sisters Sarah and Deborah were put into calico and cambric and a pair of shoes. Part of the money came from hiring out the three people the estate held in slavery — Simon, Rachel and Cesar — who were still in the administrator Edward Pigford's hands in 1817.

At the February term of 1819, aged twenty-four, he took over from Timothy Pigford as guardian of his own younger brothers and sisters, and on 22 March that year he rendered his first account to the court and signed it “John Eakins, Guard'n.” One of the securities named in that return was Stephen Coston.

He married Nancy Costin — of that same Coston family — probably about 1828 or 1829. The 1850 census finds him in the first dwelling enumerated in the county, a farmer with $800 in real estate, aged fifty-five, with Nancy, forty-three, and nine children at home: Margaret, Ella, John, Andrew, Joseph, Susan, Presilla, Cornelia and Sarah. His younger brother Joseph was in the next house; the two brothers' tracts adjoined on the east side of Moores Creek, where their father had bought in 1790.

The war took two of the family. In May 1861 his son John J. B. Eakins and his nephew Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in Company E of the 18th North Carolina — the Moore's Creek Rifle Guards, a company named for the water the Eakinses had farmed since before the Constitution was ratified. John J. B. was killed at Sharpsburg; Owen at Chancellorsville. On 29 January 1863, in New Hanover County, John Eakins Sr. swore before a justice of the peace that he was the father of the dead soldier and claimed the $100.23 owed on his account. Daniel McAlister, who had known the boy for twenty-five years, witnessed it. The government paid in November 1864.

He was still living after 1880. No stone for him has been found in the Eakins family burying ground on the creek.

Nancy Costin (1807 – 1870)

Ahnentafel #73 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Nancy Costin was born in 1807 in North Carolina and married John Eakins Sr. of New Hanover County, probably about 1828 or 1829 — not in 1819, as a family record has it, because in 1819 she was twelve.

She bore at least nine children between about 1830 and 1848: Margaret, Ella, John J. B., Andrew, Joseph William, Susan, Presilla, Cornelia and Sarah. The household sat in the first dwelling the 1850 enumerator came to in the county, on the Moores Creek land her husband's father had bought in 1790.

Two of her sons went into the 18th North Carolina in 1861 and one of them, John J. B., was killed at Sharpsburg in September 1862; she was alive to know it, and her husband swore the affidavit that claimed the dead boy's pay.

Her own people were Costins or Costons — one family spread across two counties, rooted on Stump Sound in Onslow and moving down into New Hanover in the 1790s. A Stephen Coston stood security on her husband's guardian bond in 1819, which is as close as the record has yet come to naming her father. She died in 1870, and like her husband she has no marked grave in the Eakins burying ground.

J. T. Swain (1838 Wilcox AL – 1904 Sumter AL)

Ahnentafel #76 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Tolbert Swain was born on 24 May 1838 in Wilcox County, Alabama. By his early twenties he had moved west into Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where on 6 February 1860 he married Nancy E. Jane; their first child, Joseph Lee, was born there that December.

He went to the war as a private in Company C of the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry. A photograph of him survives, reproduced from a printed page — a man in a dark coat and a hard round hat, with a heavy beard down his chest and a direct, unsmiling stare.

Whatever happened to him between 1861 and 1865, he did not wait once it was over. His two eldest children were born in Mississippi, in 1861 and 1864; every child from 1866 onward was born in Alabama. He moved his wife and two small children across the state line into Sumter County in the first months after the surrender, when he was about twenty-seven, and settled at Cuba. The 1880 census finds the household there: J. T. Swain, 42, Nancy E. J., 42, and seven children at home, from Joseph L., nineteen, down to John M., six.

On 3 July 1875 he walked into the registrar's office at Cuba, in Precinct No. 18 of Sumter County, and took the registration oath ahead of that August's referendum on a new state constitution. The printed oath he swore made him promise that he would "never countenance or aid in the secession of this State from the United States" and that he "accept[ed] the civil and political equality of all men" — ten years after he had ridden with the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry. The registrar W. H. Lewis wrote his name for him and he made his mark: he could not write. The remarks column calls him a farmer near Cuba. Two lines below him on the same page, registering the same day, was B. A. Simmons, the miller and farmer whose daughters his son Joseph Lee would marry nine and ten years later.

Two more babies, Jesse and Hassiel, were born and died in 1876–79. Nine children in all are known, and four of them were still living in 1940: Joseph Lee, John M. Swain of York, Mattie Shamburger and Laura McDonald. Joseph Tolbert Swain died on 14 January 1904, aged sixty-five, in Sumter County; his son William T. followed him four months later, and in November 1905 his eldest son advertised a probate sale of a hundred and sixty acres at the post-office door at Cuba, for distribution among the heirs of the estate of “J. T. Swain, deceased.” He lies in Clay Memorial Cemetery at Cuba under a government-issue marble marker reading JOSEPH TOLBERT SWAIN, PVT CO C 2 MISS CAV, CONFEDERATE STATES ARMY. A visitor left a line on his memorial page in 2013: “Here rests one who so bravely fought for the land he could not save.”

Henry H. Stephens (1822 NC – 1879 Sumter Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #80 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Henry H. Stephens was born on 8 August 1822 in North Carolina, in Orange County by the family’s account, the son of Moore Stephens and Rebecca Moize. He was a small child when the family left for Alabama — they were in Greene County by 1824 — and he grew up on the Alabama side of the Tombigbee country, moving on with his father into Sumter County.

On 4 June 1846, at twenty-three, he married Sarah Ann Jones, born in North Carolina about 1833, whose family lived a few doors from his own. In the summer of 1850 the whole arrangement sat on one page of the census: in the “Intercourse” division of Sumter County, enumerated on 7 June, Henry was twenty-seven and farming at dwelling 1184 with Sarah, seventeen, and a son Calvin, aged one, and a boarder in the house, Redden Marshall, twenty-three and North Carolina-born. Two dwellings along lived Daniel Jones, Sarah’s father; six houses away, at dwelling 1190, lived Henry’s own father Moore. A son, Caswell Massey Stephens, was born on 8 January 1852.

Sarah died young, and nothing records her death. On 21 October 1854 Henry married again, to Elizabeth Jones — almost certainly his first wife’s younger sister — before Jesse B. Thorne, Justice of the Peace. He was a Minister of the Gospel as well as a farmer, and the Sumter County marriage registers have him performing weddings through the 1860s and seventies, his own son Caswell’s among them in October 1871.

By 1860 the family had followed Moore Stephens west into Newton County, Mississippi, where Henry was listed with Elizabeth, twenty-one, and the children Caroline, Caswell, James and William. He was forty when the war reached him, with a second wife and small children at home and no exemption — he was liable to Confederate conscription from September 1862. He served, and the company he most likely marched in was the Newton County company of the 5th Regiment, Mississippi Infantry (State Troops) — the Minute Men — where an H. H. Stephens appears as a private in 1863, with a W. Stephens and a Z. Stephens beside him on the roll and a cluster of Joneses: Daniel, Samuel, William, Riley, H. D., A. and W., the surname he had married into twice. The 5th mustered from 31 July to 1 October 1862 and again from 17 July to 21 September 1863, and it was at Vicksburg: the regiment’s papers afterwards count the men paroled there on 5 August 1863, with the killed, the dead of wounds and the dead of disease through the siege beside them. His widow, swearing to his service forty years later, remembered it differently — 2nd Lieutenant in Company I of the 4th Mississippi Infantry Volunteers — but that company was the Benela Sharpshooters of Calhoun County, two hundred miles from anywhere this family lived, and no Stephens of his initials is on its roll.

He came home. By 1870 the family was back at Cuba, in Township 17 of Sumter County, Alabama — the third leg of one continuous migration. On 31 December 1869 he stood in the Probate Court at a special term before Judge J. A. Abrahams and was appointed guardian of three of his own sons, Caswell M., Calvin D. and John M., each over fourteen and each described as “a minor heir of Daniel Jones deceased,” posting bonds of $800, $800 and $1,000; the property was real enough that he was filing annual settlements two years later. On 22 June 1875 he registered to vote at Thornville, Precinct No. 3 of Sumter County, entry 25, writing “H. H. Stephens,” farmer, in his own hand. Two lines down the same page a D. C. Stephens registered the same day, and immediately above him A. J. Shelton, “Farmer & Blk Smith.”

He died on 20 October 1879 at Gaston, in Sumter County. His gravestone reckons his life to the day — born 8 August 1822, died 20 October 1879, aged 57 years, 2 months and 12 days — and carries a Masonic square and compasses inside a carved wreath. The carver spelled the family name STEAVENS.

What the war and his death left behind shows in one document, sworn a quarter of a century later. On 4 July 1904, in Sumter County, Elizabeth Stephens went before the county pension examiner R. H. Hale and swore that she was “the widow of H. H. Stevens, who served as a 2[nd] Lieutenant in company I of the 4th Regiment Miss Infantry Volunteers,” that her husband “died on the 29 day of Oct, 1879,” and “that he did not desert the service of the State of Alabama, nor of the Confederate States.” Two men of the neighbourhood swore the same day that they had known him personally and that “the services set forth in the application were rendered by said H. H. Stephens” — W. H. Stephens, very probably one of his own family, and T. J. Tucker. The Sumter County board passed it, and on 17 August 1904 the State Board of Examiners at Montgomery placed her in class 4 under the pension act of 10 February 1899. With it she filed a Schedule of Property, a printed form with a line for every kind of thing a Black Belt household could own — acres of land, town property, horses and mules, cattle, hogs, sheep and goats, pistols and guns, watches and clocks and jewelry, household and kitchen furniture, bicycles and vehicles, goods and merchandise, mechanical and farming tools. She wrote a nought on every line, and a nought at the total, and the application says she had “no children upon whom she can depend for support.”

Sarah Ann Jones (b. ~1833 North Carolina)

Ahnentafel #81 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Ann Jones was born about 1833 in North Carolina, almost certainly the eldest daughter of Daniel Jones. Her younger brothers and sisters were all Alabama-born from about 1837, which puts the family’s move south in the middle thirties, with Sarah born just before it.

On 4 June 1846, at about thirteen — fourteen or fifteen if her recorded age runs light — she married Henry H. Stephens in Sumter County, Alabama. She had her first child, Calvin, at about sixteen. In 1850 she was “Sarah Stephens, 17,” keeping house at dwelling 1184 in the Intercourse division of Sumter County with her husband, their one-year-old son and a boarder. Her father’s house stood two doors away and her husband’s father’s six houses off; the whole connection fitted into one short row. Caswell Massey Stephens was born to her on 8 January 1852, and a son John M. probably followed.

She died somewhere between then and October 1854, when Henry remarried — the new wife, Elizabeth Jones, in all likelihood her own younger sister. Nothing records her death, her age at it, or where she lies. What survives instead is an inheritance: in December 1869 her three sons were bonded in the Sumter County probate court as “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” a share that could only reach grandsons through a parent already dead. That is the nearest thing she has to an epitaph.
