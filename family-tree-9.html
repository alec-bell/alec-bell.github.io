Erwin R. Lamb (1840 Wayne Co. IN – 1919 Jonesboro)

Ahnentafel #54 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Erwin R. Lamb — Irvin on his own gravestone, Irwin in the Jonesboro papers; the three spellings are one man — was born on 19 March 1840 in Wayne County, Indiana, the second of the ten children of Elias Lamb, carpenter and farmer, and Susannah (Bish) Lamb, who had married at her father’s house in Clay Township on 23 March 1837. His grandparents were Joseph and Lydia (Adamson) Lamb, Quakers who had brought the family from Randolph County, North Carolina, to Wayne County in April 1829, eleven years before he was born. He was reared on his father’s farm in Clay Township and stayed in the county until he was a grown man.

On 14 March 1860 he took out a licence in Wayne County to marry Melissa J. Bailey, the daughter of a neighbouring farming family; the minister married them the next day. It was a double season: his elder brother Azel had married Sarah Ann Bailey three months before, in the next township. That summer the newlyweds were still under his father’s roof in Clay Township, on a farm worth eleven thousand dollars. Four children came — Alma S., Martha E., Frank A. and May Belle — and then, on the evening of Monday 29 November 1875, after a few days’ illness, Melissa died at Newport in Vermillion County, aged thirty-one. The family had moved there in 1870 with his parents.

On 18 May 1879 he married Mrs. Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, the widow of a soldier of the 125th Illinois Infantry and the daughter of Arthur Jackson, an early Vermillion County settler who had given three sons to the Union and lost all three. She brought five Hall children with her. In 1880 the two families were together on Main Street in Vermillion — Erwin forty, “Mary E.” forty-one, his own Mattie, Frank and Bell, and her Effie. By 1888 he was living at Newport. In May 1890 he was very probably the Erwin Lamb of Plainfield, Indiana, who came down to Terre Haute to stay with two of his children — his daughter, by then Mrs. Mattie Ford, and his son Frank — who were keeping house together on north Third Street.

He did not stay in Indiana. He and his wife were settled at Jonesboro, in Craighead County, Arkansas, in a house on Krewson Avenue, by 1911 at the latest, and he kept the books for other men to the end of his working life: for many years he was clerk at the Old American Hotel. He was a member of the First Methodist church and a regular attender until his health failed. He spent seven weeks in St. Bernard’s Hospital in 1914.

His last illness is in the Jonesboro papers almost day by day. He had been ill at home for ten days in October 1918 and in poor health for eight months; on Monday 17 February 1919 an infected foot was amputated at St. Bernard’s, and the paper noted that at his age the operation was a shock to his nervous system; he was reported resting nicely, then continuing to improve; the surgeons went back and took the leg off below the knee for gangrene; and he died at half past twelve on the morning of 27 February 1919, aged seventy-eight. One paper called him “a splendid man”; the other, “an old resident of the city… highly respected by everyone, making a friend of every acquaintance.” Six men carried him. His survivors were given as his wife and his step-children — A. W. Hall and Mrs. C. C. Conger of Jonesboro, Mrs. Josephine Schweizer of Danville, Illinois, and Mrs. Effie Townsend of Richmond, Illinois. His own children, his daughter Martha among them, were not mentioned. He was buried at Jonesboro, under a plain marble block cut IRVIN R LAMB.

His widow Mary outlived him by two years. She had turned seventy-nine at a party at the First Methodist Church in August 1917; after his death she lived with her daughter Bertha Conger, and she died at the same hospital on 20 February 1921 after a long illness, and was buried from that church. Martha Ellen Lamb was his daughter, though her birth year was written down two or three years apart in different places, so that tie is not quite beyond doubt.

Melissa Bailey (1844 Wayne Co. IN – 1875 Newport)

Ahnentafel #55 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Melissa J. Bailey was born on 24 July 1844 in Wayne County, Indiana. In 1850 she was the youngest child in the Green Township house of her father, John Bailey, a Virginian, and his wife Martha, a North Carolinian. When her father died in August 1863 she was one of his thirteen heirs, and in January 1869 she received her share of his estate, $582.85, as Mallissa Lamb. The Baileys and the Lambs farmed adjoining ground in Clay Township — Ithamar Lamb, Milo Bailey and Lewis Strickler were living on one tract of school land there — and she married the boy from the next farm.

On 14 March 1860, aged fifteen, she took out a licence with Irvin R. Lamb, and the minister married them the next day. Three months before, Irvin’s elder brother Azel had married another Bailey, Sarah Ann — two brothers and two Bailey brides in one winter. That summer she was living with her husband in his father’s house in Clay Township. Four children followed: Alma S., Martha E., born at Richmond in August 1865, Frank A. and May Belle. About 1870 the whole Lamb connection moved west to Vermillion County.

She died at Newport at about eight o’clock on the evening of Monday 29 November 1875, after a few days’ illness, aged thirty-one. Her funeral was preached at two the following afternoon in the Methodist Episcopal church by the Rev. Milton B. Wood, and she was buried at Newport. The local paper, which knew her, called her “a United Brethren in belief” and “a kind mother, an affectionate wife, a noble woman and highly esteemed by every person in Newport.” Her daughter Martha was ten.

George Frost (1822 Macclesfield – Paterson)

Ahnentafel #56 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

George Frost was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire in the spring of 1822 and baptised there that July, the son of George Frost, a silkman, and his wife Sarah, a Somerset woman from Bridgwater — a family “engaged largely in the silk manufacture.” He entered the trade himself in 1838, while still in his early boyhood, and his department from the first was throwing — the twisting of raw silk into yarn.

By March 1851, at twenty-eight, he was already a widower, a journeyman silk throwster living in his mother’s house in Oxford Road with three small children of the family, Eliza Ann, Mary Elizabeth and George Thomas Hatton. He was still a widower, with a son and a daughter, when on 10 June 1857, at thirty-five, he married Frances Wright Bell, twenty-five, in Prestbury parish church, after banns. Both gave their residence as Macclesfield; he was a silk throwster like his father, George Frost, and her father, Nathaniel Bell, a silk manufacturer, stood witness and signed the register. Their children included Mary; Frank, born at 6 Lord Street, Macclesfield, on 4 April 1858 and baptised at the Wesleyan chapel on Park Green; Harry Bell, baptised at Macclesfield in April 1860; Maurice and Charlie, baptised together in July 1863, by which time George had become a trimming manufacturer. Charlie lived only five months and was buried at Christ Church that December; a later son, born in America, was given his name, and was Charles, the youngest, in the Paterson census of 1880. The family came over in 1864 and settled at Paterson, and he commenced business in this country about 1870 with but four hands.

By June 1880 he was at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, fifty-eight years old, a silk manufacturer with rheumatism, and two of his sons were working in the mill. That year he took Frank and Harry B. into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, with a frontage of a hundred feet on Madison Street and, as one visitor put it, “an air of taste too rarely seen in factory buildings.” They threw yarn exclusively on commission, and were the first house in Paterson to install the celebrated Atwood machinery. By the middle eighties the firm was advertising from 36 to 52 Madison Street as makers of “Organzine, Tram, Floss, etc. On Commission,” and in September 1885 a fire in Straight Street singed the back of a new mill they were putting up.

The Albion Mill in 1887 was a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street, running from No. 34 to No. 52, with GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING lettered along the pavement. Reeling was done on the first floor, spinning on the first and second, winding and doubling on the third, winding on the fourth, and there was a carpenter’s repair shop in the basement. One engine of 350 horsepower drove the whole of it, an artesian well was sunk in the yard, and about three hundred hands worked there. His neighbours were the rest of the trade — Wm. Strange & Co. and Doherty & Wadsworth across the street, Jno. Dunlop’s silk twist mill and Naef Bros. & Co. round the corner, Benj. Eastwood making silk machinery a block away.

In February 1887, when the Paterson silk dyers struck and the whole trade seized up, George Frost & Sons told their four hundred hands that the Albion Mills would not reopen until the strike was settled. The news went out on the wire and was reprinted as far away as Topeka, Grand Island and Victoria, British Columbia. It is the one day his name travelled.

He died four months later, at Paterson, on 27 June 1887, aged sixty-seven, and was buried — all but certainly — at Cedar Lawn Cemetery, remembered there in the terms he would have chosen: “senior member of the firm, George Frost & Sons, silk manufacturers; native of Macclesfield, England.”

Frances W. Bell (~1832 Macclesfield – 1899 Paterson)

Ahnentafel #57 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Frances Wright Bell was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire about 1832, the daughter of Nathaniel Bell, a silk weaver who rose to be a silk manufacturer. Almost certainly she is the Frances, born 1832, whom a Nathaniel Bell, silk weaver of Sutton, brought to St George’s, Sutton, to be baptised at five on 30 June 1837, on the same day as her brother James — though no record yet carries a second member of that family through to the Chestergate house of 1851. By 1851 her mother was dead, and Frances, nineteen, was living in her widowed father’s house on Chestergate, Macclesfield’s main street, with a younger brother and sister and a servant. On 10 June 1857, at twenty-five, she married George Frost, a widowed Macclesfield silk throwster, in Prestbury parish church, with her father as witness — thirty-nine years to the day before her son Harry married. She bore the sons who carried the firm on — Frank, born at 6 Lord Street, Macclesfield, on 4 April 1858 and baptised at the Wesleyan chapel on Park Green, where the minister wrote her out in full as Frances Wright Frost; Harry Bell, baptised at Macclesfield in April 1860; Maurice and Charlie, baptised together there in July 1863, when the clerk wrote her name in full as Frances Wright Frost. Charlie died at five months and was buried at Christ Church, Macclesfield, a week before Christmas; the son born to her in America was named Charles after him, the youngest in the Paterson census of 1880. The family crossed in 1864 and settled at Paterson.

In June 1880 she was at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, keeping house for a household of seven: her husband, his grown daughter Mary, and three sons, the two eldest boys already working in the family’s silk mill. Her maiden name went forward into the generation after her children’s: her grandson, born in Pennsylvania in July 1900, was christened Harry Bell Frost. It was that boy’s father, Harry B., who had married a Detroit schoolteacher in 1896.

She was not George’s first wife: he had a son George and a daughter by an earlier marriage, and the daughter went home to England. Frances outlived him by a dozen years. Her last home was 189 Tyler Street, Paterson, and she died there of cancer at nine on the morning of 5 January 1899, leaving her four sons, Frank, Harry, Maurice and Charles, and her two stepchildren; in all likelihood she was buried beside George at Cedar Lawn.

Mathew Chalmers was born in Scotland about 1821 and crossed to America as a young man; with him and his wife the Scottish side of this family begins. Ellen King was born in Scotland about 1823 — Ellen B. King, as her stone has it — and crossed to America as a young woman. She married Mathew Chalmers, a fellow Scot, and lived at Orange, in Essex County, New Jersey, for more than half a century.

They had four daughters, all born in New Jersey. In 1870 she was forty-four and keeping house at Orange with her husband, a retired dealer in stores, and the girls: Ellen B., thirteen, Margaret, seven, Catharine, four, and Sarah, a baby of one. Catharine — Katharine — born in January 1866, became a schoolteacher and married Harry B. Frost of Paterson at Detroit in 1896; her first child, born at Paterson in September 1898, was christened Katherine King Frost, carrying her grandmother’s maiden surname, which is how this family remembered its women. Margaret became Mrs. Jordan and Sarah stayed Miss Chalmers, and both were living at East Orange as late as 1932.

She was a member of the North Orange Baptist Church, and Katharine was a Baptist at East Orange for many years after her.

Mathew died in 1893 and she was a widow eleven years. She died at her home, 262 Centre Street, Orange, in 1904, after a long illness, eighty-one years old; the day is given both as 30 March and as the end of May. Four daughters survived her. She was buried at Rosedale Cemetery in Montclair, beside Mathew, under a stone cut ELLEN B. KING / WIFE OF / MATTHEW CHALMERS / 1823–1904.
