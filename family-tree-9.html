John (F.) Bell (1807 SC – 1890 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #64 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Bell was born on 13 January 1807 in South Carolina, and came to Alabama a young man of about twenty-three, when his parents brought the family out of Darlington District about 1830 and settled in the Barbour County neighbourhood the family never left. He was in Alabama by 1836, because in that year he served as a private in Captain White’s company, Regiment of Alabama Volunteers, in the Creek War; he took his pay for it in land. He was settled in Barbour County before 1837, and every one of his children was born in Alabama — the eldest, Mary, about 1836 or 1837.

He began buying early. On 19 July 1838 John H. Miller sold him about 189 acres in Township 10, Range 26 for five hundred dollars, Ann Caroline Miller releasing her dower the same afternoon. In January 1841, from Alabama, he sold off a forty-acre Georgia gold-lottery lot in what had just become Forsyth County for thirty dollars, and signed the deed himself — John F Bell — in a generation where a great many men made their mark. In April 1853 President Franklin Pierce signed his bounty-land patent: warrant 4615, forty acres and eleven hundredths of an acre, for the Creek War service. He sold that same forty to William Bounds in 1858, and for a generation afterwards his neighbours’ deeds navigated by “the east corner of the Bell forty.”

His household can be followed across half a century. In 1840 there is a boy and a girl under five in the house. In 1850 he is forty-three and farming, with his wife Laney — South Carolina born, like him — and Mary 13, William 11, John 9, Kervin 4 and Laney 2. Two of those children matter to this family: William, who died a Confederate pensioner in 1924 and whose estate was divided out among his nieces and nephews; and John, who became John T. Bell and fathered Thomas Jefferson Bell. By 1860 he was returning four thousand dollars in land and ten thousand in personal estate, and in the Alabama cotton country of that year a figure like that is largely a statement about people: he probably owned the handful of men and women set down under a John Bell of Barbour County in that year’s slave schedule. In July 1867 he registered as a qualified voter at Precinct No. 9. In August 1870, at sixty-three, he had Laney at sixty-four and two grown children still at home, Mary and Archibald — and his son John T., twenty-seven, was farming the very next place with a wife and four small children. By June 1880 he was seventy-two and a widower, and he told the enumerator that both his own parents had been born in South Carolina.

He farmed into old age. In 1876, at sixty-nine, he gave a crop lien in his own voice — “I John F Bell being indebted to J. C. Jones … thirty five dollars … to make a crop the present year on my plantation in Barbour County Alabama.” On 1 August 1890, at eighty-three, he divided the home place by deeds of gift — 203⅓ acres each to his son Curvin and his daughter Mary, each gift to take effect only after his death, “I however, holding and retaining the ownership and comfort of said above described lands during my natural life time.” The boundary runs past “the big gate leading to the house” on the public road from Clayton to Louisville. So there was no estate left to probate when he died on 21 November 1890: he had given the farm away and kept the comfort of it.

The following November his heirs petitioned to sell 604 acres containing “the John Bell homestead and out houses” for division — in equal thirds to William, to John T., and to the five children of the daughter who had married a Farrior. The boundaries of that tract name his neighbours, and they explain a good deal: the Daniel Norton land to the south, which is why two Bell wives later married a Norton, and the old William Beasley place, in whose little family burying ground he himself lies.

Nancy E. Jane was born in Mississippi in the last days of 1837 — 26 December is the date carried from her grave, though all her life she gave her age as if she had been born in 1838. Her people were probably Phillipses; the family has long held her father to be Robert Phillips and her mother Nancy White. She married Joseph Tolbert Swain in Lauderdale County on 6 February 1860, when she was twenty-two, and their first child was born that December.

She bore at least nine children. The two eldest, Joseph Lee and Alice Jane, were born in Mississippi; from 1866, after the family crossed into Sumter County, Alabama, the rest were born there — Martha Ann, Laura, Minnie Lou, William T., John M., and two who did not live, Jesse in 1878 and Hassiel in 1879. She kept house at Cuba, and in 1880 seven of the children were still under her roof. Two of her daughters, Alice Jane and Minnie Lou, married Phillips men in their turn.

She was widowed in January 1904 and lost her grown son William T. that May. Four of her children outlived their father by decades — her eldest, Joseph Lee, and John M. Swain, Mattie Shamburger and Laura McDonald, all three of the others still at York in 1940. She died on 2 June 1908, aged seventy, and lies beside her husband in Clay Memorial Cemetery at Cuba. Her stone does not give her own name. It reads only “Wife of J. T. Swain.”

Ben Simmons (1818 GA – 1894 Kewanee MS)

Ahnentafel #78 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Asbury Simmons — B. A. Simmons on his gravestone and Ben on most people's tongues — was born on 26 October 1818 in Georgia, in Troup County by his family's reckoning, and came as a young man with his people into Sumter County, Alabama. His father was very probably Robert Simmons of that county, who in the fifth item of a will written on 6 March 1845 set down: “I hereby appoint my Son Benjamin Asbury Simmons my executor to this my last will and testament.” The same will shows how that household looked after its women: the daughters' share was granted “to them and the heirs of their bodies; and not to be subject to the debts of their husbands.” The will was proved on 15 November 1855. Ben's brothers and sisters were Mariah Jane, who married a Bustin, William Miles, John Christopher, Robert H., an E. W. and Susan H., who married a Boyd and died young in 1852.

He married Adeline on 11 May 1847, when he was twenty-eight and she was seventeen. Their children's birthplaces trace a family moving back and forth along the state line: Alabama in the late 1840s, Mississippi in the early 1850s, Alabama again by the end of that decade. By 1870 he was at Cuba with Adeline and six children in the house, and he was still there in 1880, with a sixteen-year-old daughter among them whose name the clerk wrote down as “Dellon” — Rebecca Anna Dillon Simmons. Two of his daughters married the same Swain man, twenty-two months apart, so which of them carries Alec's descent is still an open question. Either way the parents are this couple, though the step down to the Swains is probable rather than certain.

He was a miller as well as a farmer. When he registered to vote at Cuba on 3 July 1875, entry 64 of the Sumter County book, he wrote his own name in a strong, practised hand, where most of the page made marks, and the registrar gave his living in three words: “Mill & Farm.” His was a slaveholding family before the war — in 1855 his brother Lewis was taxed for twenty-nine enslaved people, Robert H. for fifteen and William M. for two.

He ended his life just across the line at Kewanee, in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, and died there on 6 April 1894, aged seventy-five. His marble headstone in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, carved with two clasped hands, reads B. A. SIMMONS, HUSBAND OF ADALINE SIMMONS, BORN OCT. 26, 1818, DIED APRIL 6, 1894.
