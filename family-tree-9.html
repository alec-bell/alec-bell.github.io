Mathew Chalmers (~1821 Scotland – 1893 Orange NJ)

Ahnentafel #58 · his mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mathew Chalmers was born in Scotland about 1821 and crossed to America as a young man; with him and his wife the Scottish side of this family begins. He married Ellen King, another Scot, and by the middle 1850s they were settled at Orange, in Essex County, New Jersey, where all their children were born.

He made his living in trade and was out of it early. In 1870, at forty-nine, he was a retired dealer in stores with real estate worth $4,600 and $1,500 more in personal property — a comfortable house in the second ward of Orange holding his wife Ellen, forty-four, and four New Jersey-born daughters: Ellen B., thirteen, Margaret, seven, Catharine, four, and Sarah, one.

Catharine — Katharine — became a schoolteacher, went out to Detroit, and married the Paterson silk manufacturer Harry B. Frost there on 10 June 1896. Margaret married a Jordan; Sarah never married; both were still at East Orange in 1932, when Katharine died. Two of his grandchildren were christened with their grandmothers’ maiden surnames, Katherine King Frost and Harry Bell Frost, which is how this family marked descent.

He died in 1893, when Katharine was twenty-seven and teaching school, and he lies at Rosedale Cemetery in Montclair, four miles from Orange, under a flat granite stone cut MATTHEW CHALMERS, 1821–1893, with his wife Ellen’s beside it.

Moses B. Roush (1842 – 1925 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #60 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Moses B. Roush was born on 30 January 1842 in Meigs County, Ohio, in the riverside settlement the Roushes and the Neases had founded together and called the Nease Settlement. He was a son of Benjamin “Bennie” Roush and Sophia Altman, and grew up on their farm in Sutton Township — seven years old in 1850, eighteen in 1860, one of twelve children.

He went to the war late. He entered service on 23 July 1864, aged twenty-two, as a private in Company A — and afterwards Company B — of the 28th Ohio Volunteer Infantry, Ohio’s “2nd German Regiment,” raised at Camp Dennison from German-speaking volunteers and by 1864 taking whatever recruits the state had left. He served the last twelve months of the war, from the Atlanta summer through to three months after Appomattox, and was discharged on 6 July 1865.

On 1 September 1870 he married Sarah Duckworth, the daughter of a Lancashire collier, at Orange Township in Meigs County; her brother Adam married in the same township eight days later. They had Sarah Ann in 1871, Charles Benjamin in 1874, John G. in 1877 and Richard in 1880. In March 1880 he applied for an invalid pension on his war service; eleven months later, on 4 March 1881, Sarah died, leaving him four children under ten. He married Lydia E. in Mason County in August 1883 and took the family across the Ohio into Graham District, where he lived about twenty years and had five more children.

In May 1883 the Grand Army of the Republic mustered in Washington Post No. 13 at New Haven, Mason County, with fourteen charter members, and its Officer of the Guard was a Moses Roush — very probably him. In the first weeks of 1887 he was called before the United States Board of Examining Surgeons, which sat every Wednesday at Dr. Hoy’s office at Point Pleasant. The county paper ran the summons three weeks over, under the heading “ATTENTION! — The boys who wore the blue,” and listed him as “Moses Roush, Co. A. 28th Ohio Vols.”; he had to be re-examined before the quarter closed on 31 March or lose his place on the pension pay roll. In 1900 he and his son John were neighbours at Hartford village. He was still doing road work with a team for the county in his sixties, and the Graham District road accounts of 1907 and 1910 pay him for team and labour.

Lydia died in 1906. In 1908 he buried his son John and two granddaughters, and in May 1909 he stood surety on his widowed daughter-in-law’s $500 bond to be guardian of the four orphaned girls — the grandfather behind the orphans’ bond. He married a third time, to Margaret A. Zerkle, in 1911. From 1920 the government paid his invalid pension at an increased rate, and raised it again in 1923.

He died at six in the evening on 23 August 1925 at New Haven, aged eighty-three years six months and twenty-three days, of general arteriosclerosis with senility, after Dr. R. S. Bryant had attended him for three days. It was his son Charles who gave the county his parents’ names — “Bennie” Roush of Graham Station and Sophia Aultman of Portsmouth, Ohio. He was buried at Broad Run under a barrel-topped stone of the same pattern as his parents’, reading FATHER & MOTHER / MOSES B. ROUSH / BORN JAN. 30, 1842; no death date was ever cut on it, and it has since come off its base and lies tilted. His widow filed her pension claim eight days after he died, and the money that paid for the end of his life had been granted for a war that ended sixty years before.

Sarah Mary Duckworth (~1851 PA – 1881)

Ahnentafel #61 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Mary Duckworth was born in June 1851 in Pennsylvania, the fourth child and the first American child of James Duckworth, a Lancashire collier, and his wife Ann Collins. Her three elder brothers and sisters were born in England and her two younger brothers in Pennsylvania and Ohio, so hers is the birth that marks the family’s crossing: they were still in Lancashire when Adam came in March 1847, and in the Pennsylvania coalfield by the time she arrived.

By 1860 the Duckworths had moved on to the Ohio Valley coalfield at Syracuse, Meigs County, where the family name was as often written “Deckworth” as not, and where she was nine. She was still at home in the summer of 1870, aged nineteen, and on 1 September that year she married Moses B. Roush at Orange Township in the same county — a Union veteran nine years her senior. Her brother Adam married eight days later in the same township. It was a busy fortnight for the family.

They had four children: Sarah Ann in 1871, Charles Benjamin in 1874, John G. in 1877 and Richard, born at Syracuse in 1880 and named for her own brother. In June 1880 the little household was in Sutton Township, with her widowed mother Ann keeping house five doors away.

Sarah died on 4 March 1881, aged twenty-nine, leaving four children under ten and the youngest still a baby. Her mother was living at the end of the row when it happened. She is buried at Broad Run Cemetery in Mason County — which the neighbourhood also called the Nease Cemetery, and which is the same ground her husband, her son John and her grandchildren would come to — under a stone giving June 1851 to 4 March 1881.

Lewis J. Nease (1854 – 1917 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #62 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Lewis J. Nease was born on 20 November 1854 in Mason County, then still Virginia, the second child of George Nease and Nancy Roush, who had married six years before. He was six years old on the family’s farm in 1860, in a household the enumerator wrote down as “Neoce.”

On 12 April 1877, aged twenty-two, he married Martha J. Love, seventeen, who had been born across the river in Athens County, Ohio. Their eldest, Bertha, was born the following January, and Lewis reported the birth himself as a farmer of Mason County. At least nine more children followed over the next twenty years, down to births registered in 1891 and 1895. By 1900 he and Martha had been married twenty-three years and were living close by their married daughter Bertha.

Twenty years after his own wedding he stood at the same courthouse counter for his daughter’s: on 22 February 1897 it was he who gave the clerk Bertha’s age and birthplace when she married John G. Roush. He went by his initials all his life, and L. J. is the name on his gravestone.

By the end he was a labourer. He died on 17 February 1917 in the Waggener District of Mason County, of heart trouble, aged sixty-two years two months and twenty-eight days. He lies at Broad Run — the Nease burying ground, as the neighbourhood called it in print as early as 1899 — under a small obelisk carved with a gate or lattice, and the inscription reads L. J. NEASE, 1854 - 1917. Nobody at his death could give the county his own parents’ names; both columns were left unfilled.

John F. Bell (1807 SC – 1890 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #64 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Bell was born on 13 January 1807 in South Carolina, and came to Alabama a young man of about twenty-three, when his parents brought the family out of Darlington District about 1830 and settled in the Barbour County neighbourhood the family never left. He was in Alabama by 1836, because in that year he served as a private in Captain White’s company, Regiment of Alabama Volunteers, in the Creek War; he took his pay for it in land. He was settled in Barbour County before 1837, and every one of his children was born in Alabama — the eldest, Mary, about 1836 or 1837.

He began buying early. On 19 July 1838 John H. Miller sold him about 189 acres in Township 10, Range 26 for five hundred dollars, Ann Caroline Miller releasing her dower the same afternoon. In January 1841, from Alabama, he sold off a forty-acre Georgia gold-lottery lot in what had just become Forsyth County for thirty dollars, and signed the deed himself — John F Bell — in a generation where a great many men made their mark. In April 1853 President Franklin Pierce signed his bounty-land patent: warrant 4615, forty acres and eleven hundredths of an acre, for the Creek War service. He sold that same forty to William Bounds in 1858, and for a generation afterwards his neighbours’ deeds navigated by “the east corner of the Bell forty.”

His household can be followed across half a century. In 1840 there is a boy and a girl under five in the house. In 1850 he is forty-three and farming, with his wife Laney — South Carolina born, like him — and Mary 13, William 11, John 9, Kervin 4 and Laney 2. Two of those children matter to this family: William, who died a Confederate pensioner in 1924 and whose estate was divided out among his nieces and nephews; and John, who became John T. Bell and fathered Thomas Jefferson Bell. By 1860 he was returning four thousand dollars in land and ten thousand in personal estate, and in the Alabama cotton country of that year a figure like that is largely a statement about people: he probably owned the handful of men and women set down under a John Bell of Barbour County in that year’s slave schedule. In July 1867 he registered as a qualified voter at Precinct No. 9. In August 1870, at sixty-three, he had Laney at sixty-four and two grown children still at home, Mary and Archibald — and his son John T., twenty-seven, was farming the very next place with a wife and four small children. A few doors further along, his daughter Melissa, twenty-one, was keeping house for her young husband, Marion Farrior. By June 1880 he was seventy-two and a widower, and he told the enumerator that both his own parents had been born in South Carolina.

He farmed into old age. In 1876, at sixty-nine, he gave a crop lien in his own voice — “I John F Bell being indebted to J. C. Jones … thirty five dollars … to make a crop the present year on my plantation in Barbour County Alabama.” On 1 August 1890, at eighty-three, he divided the home place by deeds of gift — 203⅓ acres each to his son Curvin and his daughter Mary, each gift to take effect only after his death, “I however, holding and retaining the ownership and comfort of said above described lands during my natural life time.” The boundary runs past “the big gate leading to the house” on the public road from Clayton to Louisville. So there was no estate left to probate when he died on 21 November 1890: he had given the farm away and kept the comfort of it.

The following November his heirs petitioned to sell 604 acres containing “the John Bell homestead and out houses” for division — in equal thirds to William, to John T., and to the five children of the daughter who had married a Farrior. The boundaries of that tract name his neighbours, and they explain a good deal: the Daniel Norton land to the south, which is why two Bell wives later married a Norton, and the old William Beasley place, in whose little family burying ground he himself lies.

John Eakins Sr. (~1795 New Hanover NC – aft. 1880)

Ahnentafel #72 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Eakins was born about 1795 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, the eldest son of Joseph Eakins and the woman who was probably Elizabeth Pigford. He was about ten when his father died in the first months of 1805, leaving a widow, a houseful of small children, four hundred acres on Moores Creek, and an estate the county court had to superintend for the next fourteen years.

That estate paid for his upbringing. In February 1816 it was charged $3.83 for schooling Joseph and William Eakins and $10.50 for boarding them; his sisters Sarah and Deborah were put into calico and cambric and a pair of shoes. Part of the money came from hiring out the three people the estate held in slavery — Simon, Rachel and Cesar — who were still in the administrator Edward Pigford’s hands in 1817.

At the February term of 1819, aged twenty-four, he took over from Timothy Pigford as guardian of his own younger brothers and sisters, and on 22 March that year he rendered his first account to the court and signed it “John Eakins, Guard’n.” One of the securities named in that return was Stephen Coston.

He married Nancy Costin — of that same Coston family — probably about 1828 or 1829. In 1850 he was fifty-five, a farmer with $800 in real estate, Nancy was forty-three, and nine children were at home: Margaret, Ella, John, Andrew, Joseph, Susan, Presilla, Cornelia and Sarah. His younger brother Joseph lived in the next house; the two brothers’ tracts adjoined on the east side of Moores Creek, where their father had bought in 1790.

The war took two of the family. In May 1861 his son John J. B. Eakins and his nephew Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in Company E of the 18th North Carolina — the Moore’s Creek Rifle Guards, a company named for the water the Eakinses had farmed since before the Constitution was ratified. John J. B. was killed at Sharpsburg; Owen at Chancellorsville. On 29 January 1863, in New Hanover County, John Eakins Sr. swore before a justice of the peace that he was the father of the dead soldier and claimed the $100.23 owed on his account. Daniel McAlister, who had known the boy for twenty-five years, witnessed it. The government paid in November 1864.

He was still living after 1880. He has no marked grave in the Eakins family burying ground on the creek.
