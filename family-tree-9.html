Benjamin Nott (~1817 OH – 1891 Marietta)

Ahnentafel #52 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Nott — the name was written Knott about as often as Nott — was born in Ohio about 1815 to 1817 and farmed in Adams Township, near Lowell, in Washington County. His own parents are not known. An elder Benjamin Nott “of Adams” died insolvent in 1822, leaving a widow Polly; he was one of several Nott householders in that corner of Ohio, and if he was the father he was orphaned as a small boy on a broke farm on Calls Creek.

On the evening of Sunday 13 March 1836, in Morgan County, up the Muskingum, he married Margaret Kirk; their son John W. swore to her maiden name sixty-four years later. By 1850 they were farming in Adams Township, both thirty-three, with five children — Henrietta, Lucina, Frances, Pearly and William — and a Virginia-born woman of sixty-eight, Polly Morris, living with them. In 1860 the household stood at Lowell: Benjamin forty-three, a farmer; Margaret forty-three; P. J. fifteen; William F. thirteen; and Jos M eleven. That eleven-year-old is believed to be Joseph McGee Knott, who named Benjamin and Margaret as his parents and Lowell as his birthplace at the end of his own life; the ages do not quite line up, so the link is probable rather than certain.

In July 1861, at forty-six, he volunteered for Company A of the Thirty-sixth Ohio Infantry — enlisted 29 July 1861, a private, discharged 4 September 1864. By 1870 the family had moved to Marietta, where the household was Benjamin fifty-three, Margaret fifty-two, John W. twenty-three, Sarah twenty, a baby Henry, and three boarders.

His last years were hard. In December 1890 the probate court recorded “P. J. Nott, guardian of Benjamin Nott, an Insane person” filing an inventory — the guardian was his own child, the P. J. of the 1860 household. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891 of la grippe, the influenza that went round the world between 1889 and 1891, and the estate was settled that July: charged with nothing at all, credited with $10.91, and that sum found due the child who had cared for him. He lies in Oak Grove Cemetery at Marietta, lot 90, block 19, under an upright stone.

Margaret Kirk (1817 – 1899 Washington Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #53 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Margaret Kirk was born about 1817 — in Ohio by her own household’s reckoning in 1850 and 1860, in Virginia by the 1870 enumerator and her gravestone’s compiler. On the evening of Sunday 13 March 1836, in Morgan County, Ohio, up the Muskingum, a justice of the peace named A. Rich married her to Benjamin Nott. The Kirks of Meigs Creek in Morgan County were a real family, and one of them kept a cabin that sheltered Virginia migrants walking west in 1817, which may account for a daughter whose birthplace was once written as Virginia; which Kirk was her father is not known.

Their first child, Henrietta, was born the next year. By September 1850 they were farming in Adams Township, Washington County, near Lowell: Benjamin and Margaret both thirty-three, with Henrietta, Lucina, Frances, Pearly and a two-year-old boy, William, who later went by John W. Living with them was a woman of sixty-eight named Polly Morris, born in Virginia — a mother of one of them, very possibly, though the census does not say. In 1860 the household at Lowell held a boy of eleven entered as Jos M — Joseph McGee Knott, who named Benjamin and Margaret as his parents at the end of his life — and in 1870 the family was at Marietta, Margaret fifty-two.

Benjamin went to the war in the Thirty-sixth Ohio, came home an invalid pensioner, and in his last months was under his own child’s guardianship as insane. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891, and on 1 June Margaret applied for a widow’s pension, which was granted. Long afterwards her son John W., swearing to a marriage licence in 1900, gave his mother’s maiden name as “Margaret Keirk.”

A Mrs. Margaret Nott of Lowell, aged eighty-three, died on Sunday 15 January 1899, and her body was carried down the Muskingum by the steamer Sonoma for burial at Oak Grove Cemetery, Marietta, where Benjamin lies. The paper called her the widow of “the late Thomas Nott” — but Thomas H. Nott was her son, and the stone she shares with Benjamin reads MARGRET NOTT HIS WIFE 1817–1899, so it was almost certainly she.

George Frost (1822 Macclesfield – Paterson)

Ahnentafel #56 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

George Frost was born at Macclesfield in Cheshire in the spring of 1822 and baptised there that July, the son of George Frost, a silkman, and his wife Sarah, a Somerset woman from Bridgwater — a family “engaged largely in the silk manufacture.” He entered the trade himself in 1838, while still in his early boyhood, and his department from the first was throwing — the twisting of raw silk into yarn.

By March 1851, at twenty-eight, he was already a widower, a journeyman silk throwster living in his mother’s house in Oxford Road with three small children of the family, Eliza Ann, Mary Elizabeth and George Thomas Hatton. He was still a widower, with a son and a daughter, when on 10 June 1857, at thirty-five, he married Frances Wright Bell, twenty-five, in Prestbury parish church, after banns. Both gave their residence as Macclesfield; he was a silk throwster like his father, George Frost, and her father, Nathaniel Bell, a silk manufacturer, stood witness and signed the register. Their children included Mary; Frank, born at 6 Lord Street, Macclesfield, on 4 April 1858 and baptised at the Wesleyan chapel on Park Green; Harry Bell, baptised at Macclesfield in April 1860; Maurice and Charlie, baptised together in July 1863, by which time George had become a trimming manufacturer. Charlie lived only five months and was buried at Christ Church that December; a later son, born in America, was given his name, and was Charles, the youngest, in the Paterson census of 1880. The family came over in 1864 and settled at Paterson, and he commenced business in this country about 1870 with but four hands.

By June 1880 he was at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, fifty-eight years old, a silk manufacturer with rheumatism, and two of his sons were working in the mill. That year he took Frank and Harry B. into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, with a frontage of a hundred feet on Madison Street and, as one visitor put it, “an air of taste too rarely seen in factory buildings.” They threw yarn exclusively on commission, and were the first house in Paterson to install the celebrated Atwood machinery. By the middle eighties the firm was advertising from 36 to 52 Madison Street as makers of “Organzine, Tram, Floss, etc. On Commission,” and in September 1885 a fire in Straight Street singed the back of a new mill they were putting up.

The Albion Mill in 1887 was a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street, running from No. 34 to No. 52, with GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING lettered along the pavement. Reeling was done on the first floor, spinning on the first and second, winding and doubling on the third, winding on the fourth, and there was a carpenter’s repair shop in the basement. One engine of 350 horsepower drove the whole of it, an artesian well was sunk in the yard, and about three hundred hands worked there. His neighbours were the rest of the trade — Wm. Strange & Co. and Doherty & Wadsworth across the street, Jno. Dunlop’s silk twist mill and Naef Bros. & Co. round the corner, Benj. Eastwood making silk machinery a block away.

In February 1887, when the Paterson silk dyers struck and the whole trade seized up, George Frost & Sons told their four hundred hands that the Albion Mills would not reopen until the strike was settled. The news went out on the wire and was reprinted as far away as Topeka, Grand Island and Victoria, British Columbia. It is the one day his name travelled.

He died four months later, at Paterson, on 27 June 1887, aged sixty-seven, and was buried — all but certainly — at Cedar Lawn Cemetery, remembered there in the terms he would have chosen: “senior member of the firm, George Frost & Sons, silk manufacturers; native of Macclesfield, England.”
