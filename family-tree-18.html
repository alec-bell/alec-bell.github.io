John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #960 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there. The tradition carried down into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County — later Dunmore, then Shenandoah — and farmed it; the family says he kept a mill and a tannery as well, and more than thirty land transactions stand in his name or his sons’. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: the sons Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas, and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family has always said that nine sons went out of that house.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: he furnished supplies to the army between 1780 and 1783 and took a Shenandoah County claim certificate, number 2453, for the property impressed from him. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He was a Lutheran, and the family were tanners and builders of churches. He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. When the old burying ground’s stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868 his went with them, and it is kept upright there under glass with a Sons of the American Revolution patriot star beside it. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, two hundred years after his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

Alec’s descent reaches him three times over, because two of his grandchildren married each other. This is the first of the three paths, and it runs through his son George, the Revolutionary soldier who lies at Weldon Cemetery in Ohio — a son by the family’s own settled account rather than by the word of any surviving record.

Susannah Roush (d. ~1796 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #961 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah married John Adam Roush about 1739, soon after he landed at Philadelphia, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. She was the mother of the whole American Roush family — ten children, seven of them sons, and every one of the sons went into the Revolution. Her own maiden name is lost; Sehler, or Schlern, circulates in family trees, but no source for it has ever been produced.

Very little else of her life was ever written down. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” She stood as a baptismal sponsor at the Old Pine Church in 1770, and again on 5 January 1786 — nine months before her husband died — that second time as a grandmother. After his death the church’s list of communicants carries “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, and then never again.

She died at or about 1796, ten years after her husband, and was buried beside him at the Old Pine Church. “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked,” the family’s historian wrote, “but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When the old stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with them, because there was nothing to move. In 1936 the Roush and Allied Families Association cut her name into the bicentennial monument there — “1713 – HIS WIFE, SUSANNAH – 1796” — the only stone that carries it, and the only thing that gives her a birth year at all.

Alec descends from her three times over, her grandson John and her granddaughter Regina having married each other. This is the first of the three, through her son George.

Michael Zerckel (Shenandoah Co., Virginia)

Ahnentafel #962 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Michael Zerckel — the name was written Zirkle, Zerkel, Zerchel, Sircle, Circle and Zickel, and twice differently on his own family’s gravestones — came south with the Zirkle brothers out of Pennsylvania into the Shenandoah Valley. His parents were Ludwig Zirkle and Eve, and he was their eldest son: when he and his wife Catherine sold land in Dunmore County on 16 November 1776, the sale carried fifty-four acres “patented to my mother, Eve Sircle, now deceased … and descended to Michael as eldest son and heir of Eve.”

He settled near Forestville, on Holman’s Creek, in the country that became Shenandoah County — a neighbourhood where the Zirkles, the Neases and the Roushes lived within a few miles of each other and married one another for a century. His brother Andrew built the stone mill at Quicksburg that still stands. In 1776 Michael’s own name was on the roll of the Dunmore County militia under Captain Jacob Holman, the same captain Henry Roush had served under the year before.

He and Catharine had fourteen children, though whether she was mother to all fourteen is uncertain — her own stone would make her sixteen when the eldest was born. That eldest, Catharine, married George Roush. Mary, born 20 June 1770, married Henry Nease in June 1791, and the marriage papers carry her father’s consent signed “Michel Corckel.” His son Henry followed the Roushes and the Neases down the Ohio to the Graham District of Mason County and founded the Zirkle family there. His children’s marriages are a large part of why the Roush, Nease and Zirkle families are braided together in Alec’s tree in three separate places.

He died on 2 October 1811, aged seventy-six years, eight months and three days. A cracked slab in the Zerchel–Nehs burying ground, on the east bank of Holman’s Creek northeast of Forestville, is very probably his: cut in German with a Pennsylvania-German compass star at its head, HIR RUET MICHAEL ZICKEL IST GEBOREN DEN 29 JANER 1735 UND GESTORBEN DEN 2 OCTOBER 1811 UND SEIN ALTER WAR 76 JAR 8 MO 3 DA — born 29 January 1735, died 2 October 1811. The ground has long been unfenced. “The old graveyard used to have a good many tombstones in it,” a Forestville man wrote shortly before his own death, “but for many years it has been unfenced. Now cattle graze over the bodies of our forefathers, most of the tombstones have disappeared.”

Catharine was the wife of Michael Zerckel of Forestville in Shenandoah County, Virginia, and the mother, by the family’s account, of their fourteen children. The eldest, another Catharine, married George Roush; a second daughter, Mary, married Henry Nease in 1791; a son, Henry, took the family name down the Ohio to Mason County behind the Roushes. Her own family has never been traced.

She appears with her husband in a Dunmore County deed of 16 November 1776 — “Michael Sircle and Catherine, of Dunmore County, to Ludwick Fulmor” — the earliest surviving line that gives her a name of her own.

She was born on 12 July 1747 and died on 16 November 1830, aged eighty-three years, four months and four days — exact to the day — by the stone that stands beside Michael’s in the Zerchel–Nehs burying ground on Holman’s Creek. Timothy Tuttle was born about 1682, probably at New Haven, the son of Simon Tuttle, a cooper, and his wife Abigail. Late in the nineteenth century, standing empty, it was burned by an incendiary.

His children by Thankful were Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simon in 1721 and Moses in 1723. His father gave him nine and a half acres on 10 July 1711. He was one of the farmers on the west side of the river who petitioned in December 1715 for a parish of their own — the bounds they asked for ran “west from ye rever as high as Timothy tuttle & timothy Beach’s” — and when the new Cheshire society held its first meeting, on 16 September 1723, he was chosen moderator. He sat on the committee that managed the ordination; he was “Sergeant Tuttle” of the school-house committee in 1729 and society moderator again in 1732; and by 1 December 1741 he is “decon Timothy Tuttle” on the committee to petition for town privileges. He lived near the west end of the road to the south of Broad Swamp, four miles west of the river.

Thankful died in November 1728. He married again in June 1729, Mary, widow of Stephen Rowe, who died in January 1747/8; and last, on 28 June 1749, Sarah (Atwater) Humiston, widow of James Humiston. He had also taken a proprietor’s share in one of the new townships granted up in Hampshire County, Massachusetts, and in 1736 he signed the whole of that right over to his second son — “for divers good causes and considerations and perticularly for love, good will and fatherly affection that I have and [bear] to my son Gershom Tuttle.” It is the deed that sent the next generation north.

He died on 15 April 1756 and lies in Hillside Cemetery at Cheshire, in the parish he had helped to found, under a brownstone carved with a winged soul effigy: “Here Lies ye Body of Deacon Timothy Tuttle he died April 15th 1756 in ye 75th year of his Age.”
