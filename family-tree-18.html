John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #964 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there. The tradition carried down into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County — later Dunmore, then Shenandoah — and farmed it; the family says he kept a mill and a tannery as well, and more than thirty land transactions stand in his name or his sons’. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: the sons Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas, and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family has always said that nine sons went out of that house.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: he furnished supplies to the army between 1780 and 1783 and took a Shenandoah County claim certificate, number 2453, for the property impressed from him. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He was a Lutheran, and the family were tanners and builders of churches. He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. When the old burying ground’s stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868 his went with them, and it is kept upright there under glass with a Sons of the American Revolution patriot star beside it. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, two hundred years after his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

Alec’s descent reaches him three times over, because two of his grandchildren married each other. This is the second of the three paths, and it runs through his youngest son Jonas, who stood at Yorktown and watched Cornwallis’s army surrender — a son by the family’s own settled account rather than by the word of any surviving record.

Timothy Tuttle (~1682 New Haven – 1756 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #1536 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Timothy Tuttle was born about 1682, probably at New Haven, the son of Simon Tuttle, a cooper, and his wife Abigail. He was about three when the family removed to Wallingford, soon after his grandmother’s death. He grew up there, and as a young man he went out west of the river to look for land, chose a spot, and cleared it with his own hands — staying out through the week because it was too far to walk home each night, and finding his way back on Saturdays along a line of trees he had blazed through the woods. The place was about a mile east of what became the centre of Cheshire.

He put up a house there about 1706, the same season he married: on 2 November 1706 he took Thankful Doolittle to wife at Wallingford. The family called it the first frame house in Cheshire, and it was at the least the second; under its roof, as one neighbour wrote, were born the builder’s children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren and great-great-grandchildren. Late in the nineteenth century, standing empty, it was burned by an incendiary.

His children by Thankful were Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simon in 1721 and Moses in 1723. His father gave him nine and a half acres on 10 July 1711. Elizabeth was of Northampton, Massachusetts, and married William Allis of Hatfield in 1703 or 1704. She bore five children: Mary in 1705, Lois in 1707/8 and Eliphalet in 1710 at Hatfield, and then, after the family moved upriver to Sunderland, Zebediah in 1713 and Elizabeth in 1716.

In later life she and her husband moved again, across the river to Montague, and there she died on 1 May 1758. The town set it down in a single line: “Allis. May first 1758, Elizabeth Allis wife to Mr. William Allis, died in Montague.” Her husband outlived her by five years. Two of her daughters, Lois and Mary, married into the Tuttle and the Mitchell families, and their children then married one another, so she is an ancestor of this family along two lines at once.

Her maiden name was Davis, of Northampton — though that comes down on the family’s word rather than from anything written in her own girlhood, and the Montague book that records her death calls her only “Elizabeth Allis wife to Mr. William Allis.”

Michael Mitchell (Wallingford Conn. → Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #1540 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Michael Mitchell was a Wallingford, Connecticut man — a proprietor of the town, casting lots with the other townsmen in 1695–6 and still there in 1706. Where he came from before that nobody has ever been able to say. About 1694 he married Sarah, daughter of John Catlin of Deerfield.

He moved his family to Deerfield, the most exposed town on the Massachusetts frontier. On the morning of 29 February 1704, when the French and their allies came over the stockade, his whole family escaped unharmed — which in that town, on that morning, was the exception. His wife’s father and brother were burned in their own house a few hundred yards away.

His children were William, born 1697; Michael, born 8 March 1700/1, afterwards a soldier in Father Rasle’s war and later of Wethersfield, Connecticut; Joseph, born 1705, the weaver, through whom this family descends; and Samuel, born 7 March 1712, very probably the Samuel Mitchell granted land at Sunderland in 1737 on condition that he stayed five years. Sarah died on 30 December 1736.

His Wallingford years matter more than they look. The Tuttles were farming west of the river at Wallingford in exactly those years, and Gershom Tuttle was born there in 1714 — so when Michael’s granddaughter Tabitha married Gershom’s son at Deerfield in 1757, it was not a chance meeting a hundred miles from home. It was two Wallingford families who had both gone north, marrying each other.
