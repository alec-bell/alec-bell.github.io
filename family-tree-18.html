Emanuel Nease was born in Virginia about 1792 and farmed in Mason County, on the Virginia bank of the Ohio, with his wife Katharine. In 1850 he was fifty-eight and she fifty-three, living next door to the Roushes. On 4 October 1853 he signed his will in his own hand. Every one of his sons, it says, had been given a horse, four sheep and two hogs on reaching twenty-one, and he left the same to his youngest, Levi, so that he would not be short; everything else went to Katharine, and after her to all his children, “sons and daughters Share and Share alike without any differance whatsoever.” He was dead by July 1854. He is a candidate for the father of George Nease: George’s own death record names his father as Samuel, and no record yet settles which name is right.

Anthony Roush was born on 23 January 1792 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, near Solomon’s Church, and was baptised there on 18 March 1792 with Michael Levermore standing sponsor. He was the seventh child of Henry Roush and his wife Dorothy — a Henry who at twenty-three had served eighty-eight days as a private in Captain John Tipton’s company and been paid off at Romney in October 1775, and for whom his younger brother Jonas later stood substitute.

The family came down into the Ohio Valley, and Anthony with them. On 11 July 1812, when he was twenty, Henry Roush sold him seventy-five acres for three hundred dollars. About 1810 he had married Elizabeth Rickard, a sister of the Rickard brothers of that neighbourhood; after her he married Catherine Hoffman. He had a long family, and Nancy, born on 16 February 1832, was the eleventh child; she married George Nease in 1848, at sixteen.

He fell heir, in all likelihood, to a share of his father’s portion of the six-thousand-acre estate in Graham District, Mason County — land that had been fought over in the courts since a petition of 1806.

He lies in the Rickard cemetery, in the White Church community of Mason County, under a small stone with no epitaph cut on it.

William Rasor Bell (fl. 1784 – 1803/09 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #256 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

William Rasor Bell appears out of nowhere in 1784, already styled planter, in Claremont County of old Camden District, South Carolina. He is here as the father of Archy Bell of Sumter, and that is the one step on this branch that nothing asserts — his will names a son Archibald, but no paper says that the Archibald of the will is the Archy who died in 1839.

In June 1784 he helped appraise Isaac Higgs’s estate in Kershaw District. In April 1787 he bought Micajah Stokes’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle, for £105 15s — and the very next day began giving it all back to Stokes’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in that century usually means family. Ann was conveyed twice in two days between two households that were probably kin; she is the only person he is known ever to have held, and he held her for a day. His own household in 1790 was ten people and no one in slavery.

He took two surveys in 1785 — a hundred and fifty acres on Bare Branch in April, four hundred on Lynches Creek in June — and the grants on both issued to Samuel Boykin, who had paid the treasury fee, as was the local habit. His land lay on two watersheds at once: a later grant describes eighty acres “on Charleston Road between Black River and Lynches Creek, bounded N.W. by William Rasor Bell and Fred:k Bells Land,” and a 1787 grant to Frederick Bell recites its north-east line as “Land Surveyed for Wm Rasor Bell, Frederick Bell, Jonathan Newman and Lewis Mathews” — one line of one document carrying three surnames that stay beside this family for three generations.

He may have been a Patriot, on the strength of a single sheet of paper. Book G, stub number 30, in South Carolina’s Revolutionary indents: “Issued the 22d. April 1784 To Mr. William Bell for Eleven pounds Eight Shillings and Seven pence Sterling for two Horses impressed for Militia Use in October 1780.” It is the only William Bell indent in the state that no man has ever been attached to, and no lineage society claims it; whether the two horses were his, or one of at least six other South Carolina William Bells’, nobody can say.

On the day after Christmas 1803, “very sick & weak in body,” he made his will. It divides a plain farmer’s world among a wife and eleven children: the plantation and stock to Mary for her life, to be sold at her death or remarriage and the money divided among nine sons — “John Bell, Archibald Bell, Thomas Bell, William Bell, James Bell, Drury Bell, Jesse Bell, Benjamin Bell, & Robert Bell” — and feather beds to his two daughters, Mary and Elizabeth, Elizabeth also getting “One Cow & Calf which is now call’d hers,” the only line in the document about a child’s own particular possession. The witnesses were Lewis Bell, Mary Stuckey and a Douglas.

He was dead by 1809. Moses Gordon, the executor he had named, never served, and administration with the will annexed went on 15 April 1809 to Charles Williams of Georgetown District. Jonathan Newman — the neighbour Archy Bell had witnessed for in 1805 — appraised the estate.

His own parents are unidentified. The likeliest frame is the Raccoon Swamp Bell clan of Albemarle Parish, in Surry and Sussex counties, Virginia, where the patriarch’s 1746 will names five sons and no William; if he belongs there at all it is as an unnamed grandson. And the name Rasor — almost certainly his mother’s or grandmother’s family — has never been found anywhere.
