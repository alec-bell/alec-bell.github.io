Joseph Baldwin (Milford 1639 – d. 1684 Hadley)

Ahnentafel #6166 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Immigrant

Joseph Baldwin was among the first settlers of Milford, Connecticut, in 1639, one of three brothers who came over together and were given home lots there after a first stop at New Haven. He married Hannah, who joined the Milford church on 23 June 1644, when their first four children were all baptized at once; Mary, who married John Catlin, was one of the four. Hannah died in 1661, and about 1663 Joseph moved north to Hadley in Massachusetts, where he was made a freeman in 1666. He married twice more: Isabella, the widow of the elder John Catlin and afterwards of James Northam, who died in 1676; and Elizabeth, the widow of Luke Hitchcock and then of William Warriner.

On 20 December 1680 he made his will, and its first words say exactly where he was and how he felt: “In the Name of God Amen the twentieth day of december, One thousand Six hundred and eighty, I Joseph Baldwin Senr of Hadley in the Countie of Hampshire within the Colony of the Massachusetts: being together with old age and of life now Sick and weake of body but of good and perfect Memory, thanks and praise be to Almighty God for it.” He left his wife the use of his house and land at Hadley for her life, excepting the half of his lands he had already made over to his son Joseph; the other half, with the houses and the moveables, went to that son. Then he went through the daughters one by one — five pounds to his “Loveing Daughter Hanah Hull” and five pounds to her daughter Mary Hull when she should reach eighteen; forty shillings to “my Daughter Mary Cattlin”; a legacy to Elizabeth Warriner; something to the children of his daughter Sarah Bartlett, already dead; five pounds each to Martha Hawks and Hannah Hawks — and confirmed to his three sons Benjamin, Jonathan and David the gifts he had made them out of his Milford lands. He signed it before two witnesses, Chiliab Smith, who made his mark, and Samuel Partrigg.

Nearly four years later, on 30 October 1684, he had the will read over to him again and ratified it with some further provisos, appointing his grandson Joseph Baldwin junior his sole executor in place of the son, who had died. He lived only a few days more. The probate clerk headed the inventory “An Inventory of the Estate of Joseph Baldwin Senr who deceased November 3d, 1684, taken this Novembr 8th 1684 by Lt Smith, Chilliab Smith and S. Partrigg” — a day later than the 2 November that the printed genealogies give him. It came to £165 8s 7d, of which his house, barn and home lot at Hadley were valued at £50 and ten acres of land at £45, with debts of £10 14s 3d, leaving a net estate of £154 14s 4d. In the town he had helped to found at the other end of the colony, Milford set a granite boulder by the Wepawaug bridge among its founders' memorials: JOSEPH BALDWIN, OBIT 1690, HANNAH HIS WIFE. The date on it is wrong by six years, and his own probate file says so.

Hannah Baldwin (fl. 1644 Milford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #6167 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Hannah was the first wife of Joseph Baldwin, one of the first settlers of Milford, Connecticut. She joined the Milford church on 23 June 1644, and on that same day their first four children were baptized together — among them Mary, whose baptism is dated to that day and who grew up to marry John Catlin. Four sons and five daughters are credited to the marriage in all.

She died in 1661 at Milford, and her husband left the next year for Hadley in Massachusetts and married twice more. Nothing of her own origins is established: her Christian name comes from a single line in a compiled genealogy, and the surname Whitlock, and a wedding at High Wycombe in Buckinghamshire in November 1636, are claims made on her behalf by later family writers without a citation. Milford remembers her the way the colony usually did. On the founders' boulder by the Wepawaug bridge her husband's name is cut in full, and under it: HANNAH HIS WIFE.

Deacon William Peck (1601 London – 1694 Lyme Conn.)

Ahnentafel #12300 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Peck was born in 1601 and was a merchant in London. In 1637 he sailed for New England on the Hector with his wife Elizabeth and their young son Jeremiah, in the company of Theophilus Eaton, Edward Hopkins and the Reverend John Davenport; the ship reached Boston on 26 June. He went with that company to Quinnipiac, and in June 1639 he signed the Fundamental Agreement that founded New Haven. He was made a freeman there on 29 October 1640.

His home lot was on George Street and his shop fronted on Church Street. He was never a rich man — his estate shows it — but he was a trusted one. He was one of three men who inventoried the estate of Sergeant Thomas Jeffrie in 1661. In December 1662 he stood up at a New Haven town meeting to tell it that the town's elders were not getting enough to eat: there was not wheat and malt enough to go round. With Roger Alling he was appointed a trustee of the fund, £918 in all, that paid for the colony's collegiate grammar school. From 1659 until his death — thirty-five years — he was deacon of the First Church of New Haven, and that is the name he kept.

Elizabeth died in December 1683, and about a year later he married Sarah, the widow of William Holt; his will, drawn 9 March 1689, mentions the second wife and his four children, all of them Elizabeth's. He died in October 1694 while staying at the house of his son Joseph at Lyme, more than forty miles from home. His stone was moved with the rest from the Old Green to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821 and still stands against the north wall there. It settles, in its own worn letters, a date that the books had muddled: “Deacon William Peck, aged 93, deceased Octobr 14, 1694.”

Elizabeth Peck (England 1637 – d. 1683)

Ahnentafel #12301 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Elizabeth was the wife of William Peck, the London merchant who became deacon of the First Church at New Haven. They were married in London, and in 1637 she crossed to New England with him and their young son Jeremiah on the Hector, in the company that would found New Haven; the ship made Boston on 26 June. Their home lot in the new town was on George Street, and her husband's shop fronted on Church Street.

All four of William Peck's children were hers — Jeremiah, John, Joseph and Elizabeth — and they are the four named in his will. John, born about 1638, is the son in this line. She died on 5 December 1683, away from home: she was visiting her son at Lyme, forty miles down the coast, when she died. Eleven years later her husband died in the same place, at their son Joseph's house.

She lies in New Haven. Her grave was on the Old Green with the rest of the founding generation and was moved to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821, where a small rough stone against the north wall carries nothing but a date and two initials: 1683, and under it E P. Her maiden name was never written down by anyone who knew it.

John Moss (New Haven 1639 – 1707 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #12302 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Moss was at New Haven by 1639, in the first years of the colony, and is one of the three founding names — with Doolittle and Peck — that between them made Wallingford. He served the older town first: he was one of the commissioners New Haven chose to settle its disputed boundary with Branford, and he appears in the colony's records through the 1640s, 1650s and 1660s as a trainband corporal, representative and deputy. In 1670, when Wallingford was incorporated, he moved inland with the rest, and the town's historian, writing in 1870, called him flatly “the ancestor of all who bear the name in these parts.”

He and his wife had eleven children baptized at New Haven between 1640 and 1655. Among them were Mary, baptized 11 April 1647, who married John Peck in 1664 and is the one in this line; Abigail, who married Abraham Doolittle as his second wife; and a second John, born 1650, whose existence has confused genealogists ever since.

He died in 1707, and both the stone over him and every published account agree on the striking thing about him: he was said to be 103. Savage, who printed the figure in 1860, added “perhaps with slight exagg., yet thought to be the oldest that ever died in Connecticut” — and the figure traces back to a centennial sermon preached in 1770, sixty-three years after the funeral, so it should be carried with the same raised eyebrow. His stone stands in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, a heart cut into its face and inside the heart: “Mr John Moss, born 1604, died 1707, Æ 103.”

John Moss's wife

Ahnentafel #12303 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (13 generations back) · Known unknown — the person existed; the name does not survive

The wife of John Moss of New Haven and Wallingford is known only by what she did. Between 1640 and 1655 she and her husband brought eleven children to be baptized at New Haven, among them Abigail, who married Abraham Doolittle, and Mary, baptized 11 April 1647, who married John Peck in 1664 and carried this line forward. She was alive through the colony's first fifteen years and raised a family that founded a town.

Her name has not survived. Savage and the historian of Wallingford both wrote full biographical paragraphs about her husband and neither names a wife at any point — a silence that is informative rather than accidental, since both men were writing from the records. She is on the chart because the children prove her, and she is blank because the documents are.
