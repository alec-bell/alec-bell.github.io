William Tuttle (Ringstead → New Haven 1635 – 1673)

Ahnentafel #6144 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Tuttle came from Ringstead, a village on the Nene in Northamptonshire. The parish register there records John, son of William Tootell, baptized on 8 December 1631; Anne, his daughter, on 20 January 1632/3; and Thomas, in the first weeks of 1634/5. Who his own parents were is not settled. The likeliest answer is Symon Tootell of Ringstead, who signed as churchwarden in 1622 and was buried there on 15 June 1630: a William, son of Symon, was christened in the parish on 26 December 1607, but the surname in that entry is hard to read and Symon’s will has not yet been read.

In April 1635 he sailed from London on the ship Planter, which put into Boston about the first of July. The clerk wrote him down as “Husbandman Willm Tuttell 26,” with Elizabeth, 23, John, 3½, Ann, 2¼, and Thomas, 3 months: the same three children, in the same order. In his household were two girls, Cicely Clark and Mary Bill, and both surnames belong to Ringstead families. Also aboard was the household of Richard Tuttell of Ringstead, whom the genealogies make his brother, with an Isbell Tuttell, aged 70, whom they make their widowed mother; and John Tuttle, later of Ipswich, whether brother or cousin is disputed. The passenger list calls him a husbandman, a man who farmed his own ground. He had a merchant's interests too: a petition surviving in Boston shows him among the owners of the ketch Zebulon of Ipswich, asking the General Court for two guns to arm her for a voyage to the Indies. The court refused.

Two of his sons were baptized at Charlestown, Jonathan in 1637 and David in 1639, and by June of that year the family had moved to the new colony at Quinnipiac. On 4 June 1639 the planters met in Mr. Newman's barn and signed the church covenant that founded New Haven; William Tuttle's name stands on that list between Benjamin Ling and Ezekiel Cheever, near the head among the principal men. In the first division of land in 1640 he was rated at £100 of estate and seven persons in the family. In 1641 he held the home lot of Edward Hopkins, afterwards governor of Connecticut, and in 1656 he bought from Joshua Atwater a house, home lot and barn on the green. That became the Tuttle homestead, and the family held it for thirty years. Long after, the first building of Yale College was raised on the same ground.

He had land across the water at East Haven as well, enough of it that a stream there is still called Tuttle's Brook and the rise above it Tuttle's Hill. In 1651 he joined Jasper Crane and about fifty others in the New Haven colony's attempt to plant a settlement on the Delaware; the Dutch governor at Manhattan seized the party, held them prisoner and sent them home, and the petition of protest that followed — “the humble petition of Jasper Crane, William Tuttle and many others” — went to the commissioners of the United Colonies. The venture cost him money he never recovered, and after it he turned to farming and to town business.

At that business he was constant: fence viewer for Mr. Davenport's quarter in 1644, laying out allotments and settling boundaries; a juror again and again, his title of “Mr.” always carefully written, which in a New England town record is a rank and not a courtesy; sworn constable in March 1666-7; one of the commissioners who finally settled the line between Branford and New Haven in 1669 and between New Haven, Milford, Branford and Wallingford in 1672. When the meeting house was seated in 1646-7 — the seats graded by descent, public trust, piety and estate — Mr. Tuttle was voted into the first cross seat at the upper end, nearest the pulpit. He turns up in smaller things too. In 1646 he and Jeremy Watts were fined for falling asleep at the watch house. In 1664 a girl caught pilfering tried to drag his fourteen-year-old daughter Mercy into it; the charge collapsed, and before sentence William Tuttle asked the court, “with great affection,” to deal gently with the girl and place her in a pious family.

The town he helped found was drawn out, house by house, a lifetime after he died. A plan of New Haven ‘with all the buildings in 1748’, engraved in 1806 from General Wadsworth's survey, names every householder and his trade across the nine squares — and the Tuttle name is still scattered through it: a Widow Tuttle on the north road, a Noah Tuttle to the east, an Enos Tuttle, miller, by the harbour, and an Ab. Tuttle who was the sexton. The long hip-roofed building marked ‘Yale Colledge, Built 1717’ stands on the block beside the green where the family homestead had been.

He died suddenly in the first days of June 1673, leaving no will; his inventory was entered just after that of his neighbour Benjamin Ling, taken on the sixth. It came to £499 8s 6d: oxen, steers and cows; a house, barns and home lot at £110; nine acres of peas sown and plowed; rye, oats, flax and a hundred and seventy pounds of bacon; one silver spoon; and two matchlock guns with a flask and match, bandoleers, a fowling piece, a sword and a cutlass. The appraisers stopped at the foot to ask the court whether the food the family was living on — pork, bacon, candles, malt, rye, oats, corn and salt, sixteen pounds’ worth — ought to be counted at all. A deed he had agreed to was found unsigned. His widow Elizabeth took out the administration herself, and his Branford division lands were still being parcelled out to his children on 14 February 1705, thirty-two years after he died — his son Simon's deed of that year conveys “all my right in Branford division set to my father, Mr. William Tuttle, now (Feb. 14, 1705) laid out for his children.” The last remainder of the estate was not divided until 1709, thirty-six years after his death, when it was split into eleven parts and a grandson appealed the division to Hartford. He lies somewhere under the Old Green at New Haven; the exact spot is not known. Among his descendants is his great-grandson Jonathan Edwards, who studied and taught on the very ground where William Tuttle's house had stood.

Elizabeth Tuttle (~1609 England – 1684 New Haven)

Elizabeth Tuttle was born in England about 1609. She was about twenty-six when she crossed to New England in April 1635 on the ship Planter with her husband William and their first three children — John, Hannah and Thomas — from Ringstead, Northamptonshire, in a party that also held the household of Richard Tuttell of the same parish, whom the genealogies make her husband's brother; an Isbell Tuttell, aged 70, whom they make his widowed mother; and John Tuttle, later of Ipswich. They landed at Boston about the first of July, and a year later, on 24 July 1636, “Mrs. Elizabeth Tuttell” united with the church in Boston, where she brought two sons to be baptized: Jonathan in July 1637 and David in April 1639. Within weeks of that second baptism the family had gone to the new plantation at Quinnipiac, which became New Haven.

She bore twelve children and, in a century when that was rare, every one of them lived to grow up: John, Hannah, Thomas, Jonathan, David, Joseph, Sarah, Elizabeth, Simon — from whom this family descends — Benjamin, Mercy and Nathaniel. Her own standing in the town is legible in the dullest of records. When New Haven seated its meeting house in 1646-7, the seats graded by rank, Mrs. Tuttle was placed in the fifth seat in the middle among the wives of the leading men; in 1655-6 in the fourth long seat; in 1661-2 the clerk simply wrote “Sisters Miles, Peck, Lindon, Tuttle and Gibbard.”

Her husband died suddenly in June 1673, leaving no will. Elizabeth took out the administration herself — at a court in June 1674 she appeared to confirm a sale he had made, and is entered as “widow and relict and lawful administratrix,” signing with her mark. She stayed on at the homestead on the green, where she had lived since 1656, and kept it another eleven years; in all she lived there twenty-eight.

The widowhood was hard. In November 1676 her daughter Sarah, married to John Slauson of Stamford, died — struck down, it was afterwards said, by her own younger brother Benjamin after a quarrel. Sarah was dead on 17 November 1676; Benjamin, unmarried, made over his property to his brother-in-law Richard Edwards on 18 June 1677, and was hanged at New Haven that same month.

Elizabeth spent her last years with her youngest son, Nathaniel, and died on the last day of 1684. The will she left was presented to the New Haven court on 28 July 1685 and refused: “the other children objected and the court would not allow it.” Her inventory, taken on 3 February and coming to £294 7s 3d, was thought by a later reader to show “a ladylike refinement in apparel and household appointments.” The appraisers had to reckon with her grown children as they went: “A rug not entred their sister Hood had,” “Another rug Symon had,” and four swine “which Nathanll saith should not have been in.” The “sister Hood” was probably her daughter Anne, the wife of John Hurd of Stratford. Her gravestone, cut with the old-style date “Age 76: 31:10:1684 — Elisabeth Tutle” (month ten being December under the old calendar), was carried with the others from the Old Green to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821 and stands today in the row along the north wall. Part of its epitaph has crumbled away, but it was copied before it went: “The blest live as They are: the blest That live at Rest.”

Abraham Doolittle (~1620 – 1690 Wallingford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #6148 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Abraham Doolittle was born in England about 1620 — possibly at Kidderminster in Worcestershire, where an Abraham son of Edward Doolittle was christened on 20 August 1620, though another Abraham Doolittle was buried there as a grown man in 1706 — and was in New England by 1640. He was at Boston that year and at New Haven by 1644 — the monument later raised over him says 1642 — and from there he never left the colony's service: corporal, freeman, marshal of New Haven Colony from 1662 until the colony was swallowed by Connecticut, townsman, sergeant of the first train band, deputy to the General Court for New Haven and later for Wallingford, town treasurer. In the earliest records the name is written “Dowlittle.” On 10 November 1669 he sat as a juror at New Haven on the same panel as William Tuttle and Roger Alling — his own brother-in-law — in a court presided over by the deputy governor.

That same year he was chosen to superintend the affairs of the new settlement inland, and he became one of the founders of Wallingford. He and John Peck were on the ground there in 1668, two years before the town was incorporated. He was one of four trustees who managed the plantation until the incorporation in 1670, one of the committee that gathered its first church, and deputy for it in 1668 and 1672. During King Philip's War his house was one of those fortified, and he served on the town's vigilance committee in 1675.

He married twice. His first wife was Joan Alling, the daughter of a Kempston blacksmith, the mother of his first four children, Abraham, Elizabeth, Mary and John, born 1650–55 — John, born 14 June 1655, is the son from whom this family descends. The genealogists say she died about 1661; no record of her death has been found, but she was gone by July 1663. On 2 July 1663 he married Abigail Moss, daughter of his fellow Wallingford founder John Moss. In all there were thirteen children, and when he died on 11 August 1690, aged seventy, seven sons and three daughters survived him; his will, signed that May, names his wife, the seven sons and the three daughters. He wrote it, he said, “that love & concord may be preserved, & contention & discord prevented after my decease among my surviving relations”, and spent three pages parcelling Wharton’s Brook and the west rocks acre by acre among ten children — including ten acres of Boston saltmarsh that his childless brother John of Rumney Marsh had left him in 1681. He lies in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, where the town replaced his worn original stone in 1918 with a tall granite monument that sets out his whole public career — and gives his first wife a line of her own. John is counted a child of that first marriage, though no record of the marriage itself survives.

But her father’s will of January 1657 left ten pounds to “her children”, and Abraham’s children then living were exactly these four, carried to the font together at the First Church on 24 April 1657. A fifth, Abigail, was baptized in May 1659 and died young; the family genealogists add a daughter Sarah. In January 1662, when the town assigned seats in the meeting house, “Sister Dowlittle” sat in the seventh long seat for women — very probably Joan. She died before July 1663, when Abraham married again; when she died, and where she lies, are not recorded.

The town of Wallingford remembered her all the same. When it raised a new monument over Abraham Doolittle in 1918 it cut a line for her near the top: “First wife Joane Alling is buried in New Haven.”

John Peck was born about 1638, the son of Deacon William Peck of New Haven, the London merchant who had come over on the Hector. He grew up in the colony his father helped found, and became a freeman there in 1669. On 3 November 1664 he married Mary Moss, daughter of John Moss, in New Haven.

In 1668, two years before Wallingford was incorporated, he and Abraham Doolittle were already on the ground there, and John Peck settled in the new inland town and stayed. The first of his ten children were born at New Haven and the last six at Wallingford; the eldest, Mary, born 4 March 1666, married John Doolittle, so that the two men who had broken the ground together became grandfathers to the same children.

John Peck died at Wallingford in 1724, about eighty-six years old; Mary outlived him by a year, dying 16 November 1725 aged seventy-eight. They lie in the Center Street Cemetery, in a plot that is now the ninth stop on the town's walking tour, but no stone for either of them survives — none was standing when the burying ground was surveyed stone by stone in 1934, and the two markers standing in the plot today belong to a later John Peck and his wife.
