John Wesley Truett (1833 – 1914)

Ahnentafel #88 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Wesley Truett was born on 20 October 1833, the son of the Reverend William Hampton Truett and Alcey Wiggins.

On 2 November 1854, at twenty-one, he married Emily Caroline Ward in Henry County. She was seventeen, so her father had to sign: five days earlier W. H. Ward had written out his consent for the clerk to copy into the marriage book — “license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” The father-in-law wrote a firm, practised hand. The bridegroom could not write at all.

By the war the family had moved three hundred miles west to Clarke County. He enlisted on 15 October 1862 at Coffeeville, his own village, signing on “for the War” with Colonel Alexander McKinstry — Private, Company D, 32nd Alabama Infantry, under a Captain Cox. He was twenty-eight. Four weeks later he was in the Confederate post hospital at Dalton, Georgia, with chronic rheumatism, and he never really came back: sent on to Tullahoma in February 1863, drawing clothing at Dalton that April and signing the receipt with an X, furloughed for thirty days at the end of May. Every muster roll in his file says the same word, absent. The file stops in the summer of 1863 with no discharge, no capture and no parole, and fifty-seven years later his widow, asked on a state form when he had quit the service, left the line blank. The family did not know either.

He farmed at Chilton. The 1880 census takes the whole house in one page: John, 46; Emlie C.; the children, William Curtis among them; and his mother Alecey, the preacher's widow, at sixty-seven. In 1907 the state counted him among its surviving old soldiers at Coffeeville, aged seventy-three.

On 9 July 1909 he went in person before the county pension board. What he owned, sworn on the schedule: thirty-six acres at $150, a “Small Frame Box House one Room” at $25, and one cow and calf at $10. The other eleven categories — horses, hogs, guns, watches, furniture, tools — all say None. The board wrote to the State Auditor at Montgomery: “He has some head trouble, is unable to do but little work, has no income except what little work he can do himself, no one to look to for support.” He signed John his X mark Truett, as he had at Dalton forty-six years before.

Elizabeth Roberts on 27 December 1905. Another Truett lies in the same cemetery as his son under a Confederate government marker that reads G. W. Truett, Corporal, Company D, 32nd Alabama Infantry — his own company.

His own pension application, filled in for him at Grove Hill in July 1909, says he enlisted in October 1862 at Murfreesboro in Tennessee — not at Coffeeville — and that he was honourably discharged in May 1865; the compiled service record, which stops in the summer of 1863, says neither thing. Asked on the printed form what made him unable to make a living, the board wrote three words: catarrh of the head. He had no children he could look to for support, the form says, though six of them were living. Two neighbours swore to his service, Elijah Brown and J. H. Geiger senior, and both of them signed with a mark, as he did. The county board put him in Class 4 and the state board at Montgomery agreed; he drew pension number 27126 in Clarke County until he died.

He died on 17 April 1914, aged eighty, and lies at Oak Grove Cemetery, Chilton, under a rounded marble headstone with clasped hands carved at the top and the words GONE BUT NOT FORGOTTEN. Caroline outlived him by eight years and lies three hundred miles away in Florida. A studio photograph of the two of them survives, with their son George standing behind.

Emily Caroline Ward (1836 Henry AL – 1922 Levy FL)

Ahnentafel #89 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emily Caroline Ward was born on 25 December 1836 at Abbeville in Henry County, Alabama — a date she swore to herself, under oath, at seventy-nine. She was the second daughter of William H. Ward, a South Carolina-born farmer with four hundred dollars of land at Rowville. Her mother is uncertain: Syntha, the wife beside him in 1850, married him only in December 1844, eight years after Caroline was born, and was very probably her stepmother. In 1850 she was twelve, one of eight children in the house.

She married at seventeen. Because she was under twenty-one her father had to consent, and the clerk copied his words into the Henry County marriage book above the licence: “I give my free and unqualified consent that license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” The licence issued on 28 October 1854 and they married five days later. Neither bride nor groom could write.

They moved west to Clarke County. Children came — Sarah Ann in 1858, William Curtis in 1867, George in 1872, John Wesley in 1875, Elisha Dozier in 1877, Carrie in 1878 — and in 1880 she is “Emlie C.” at Chilton, keeping house for her husband, four children and her mother-in-law Alcey. When her father's Henry County estate was broken up in 1870 she was so far away that the administrators left her off the petition altogether; a neighbour, Abijah Wiggins, put her back in six weeks later, swearing that among the heirs of full age was “the wife of JOHN TRUETT.”

She was widowed on 17 April 1914 and applied for her Confederate widow's pension three months later. In April 1915, at Coffeeville, she swore she had been born at Abbeville on the 25th day of December 1836, and signed with her mark. Then, in 1917, at eighty, she left Alabama for good: her daughter Sarah and Sarah's husband William Green Geiger moved the household to Hardeetown in Levy County, Florida, and the old woman went with them. On 13 December 1920 she put her X on an Alabama pension form with the word ALABAMA struck out and “Florida” written in, giving her address as Hardeetown and her income as “NONE.”

A second likeness of her survives besides the family group: a head-and-shoulders portrait of a dark-haired woman with her hair parted in the middle, kept in a cousin's scrapbook and copied so many times that the face has gone to black and white patches. Her gravestone at Hardeetown gives her birth as Christmas Day 1829 -- seven years earlier than she herself swore to in 1915.

She died at Chiefland at six in the evening on 30 November 1922, of what the doctor called paralysis of the bowels after a fortnight, with old age contributing — eighty-five years old. The informant was simply “Son,” who gave her father's name as John Ward and answered the next three questions with a question mark. She is buried at Hardeetown Methodist Cemetery in plot S5, the only Truett in the ground, with her daughter Sarah on one side and Sarah's mother-in-law on the other: three old women in a row. Her stone reads CAROLINE W. TRUETT — the W for Ward, her maiden name cut into her own marker — and a birth year, 1829, that is seven years wrong and came from the son standing at the registrar's desk two days after she died.

Clark Strong Tuttle (1811 Jefferson Co. NY – 1876/80)

Ahnentafel #96 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Clark Strong Tuttle was born on 27 April 1811 in Jefferson County, New York — the date is in the family Bible; the 1883 Tuttle genealogy says the 28th. His father, Colonel Gershom Tuttle, kept a farm and a famous tavern outside Watertown; his mother was Pamelia Strong Clark, and he carried both of her surnames, which is unusual enough that a United States land officer wrote him down in full in 1837 as Clark Strong Tuttle. He used the name himself.

In November 1817 a federal clerk at the Vincennes land office entered his father as “Gershom Tuttle, Jeff'n Co N.Y.”; three weeks later the same ledger calls him “Gershom Tuttle, Fort Harrison.” The family had crossed to Indiana, and Clark grew up on the Wabash. His father died in September 1823, when Clark was twelve, leaving an estate that proved insolvent and took nine years to close. In 1830, aged nineteen, he was counted not under any Tuttle roof — there was none in the county — but in the household of Henry Markle, his sister Amanda's husband.

He came of age in April 1832 and married Harriet M. Sweany two weeks later, on 11 May. Their children, in the Bible's hand, were Gersham, Aula, Hannah, Chester, Josephine, Frank and a second Aula; four of the first five died as infants or children and lie together under “C.S. & H. TUTTLE” stones at Otter Creek Union Cemetery. The second Aula was named for the sister who had died four years before she was born. Harriet died on 3 June 1851, aged thirty-seven; five weeks later, on 10 July, Clark married Nancy A. Harbert, and they had Edward, Emma, Dennis, Richard B., Harriet B. and Dollie A.

He was a miller, and a rich one. The 1850 census gives him $9,500 in real estate; in 1860 it is $14,000 in land and $9,215 in personal property, with three hired hands and a servant living in and every school-age child in the house at school. He took United States patents on four parcels in 1839 and 1841. He became a county commissioner in October 1858, and he was postmaster at Ellsworth — which was the post office at his own Tuttle's Mills. The 1874 county atlas prints the mill on its own plate: a four-storey frame mill under a smoking stack with box cars on the siding, captioned C. S. TUTTLE & CO., MILLERS & DEALERS IN GRAIN, FLOUR, MEAL &c., ELSWORTH, VIGO CO., IND., with his name, his son Frank's and a partner's beneath it. The same atlas letters the township plat “C. S. Tuttle · Mill · ELSWORTH STA.” The mill was the town.

Not everything went well. In October 1869 a postal detective charged his son Frank, who ran the office under him, with robbing the mail, and the Terre Haute paper reported that “Mr. Clark Tuttle, the father of young Frank, is terribly distressed at the result of this unfortunate affair.” The following summer he sold out: the 117-acre mill tract on 12 July 1870 for $6,000, the partnership of C. S. Tuttle & Co. dissolved on the 26th, and the home parcels gone by 24 October. By 1878 a correspondent could write that the mill “has been standing idle for four years.”

He was still alive and public-spirited in June 1876, when the Centennial Fourth of July committee listed him among its vice-presidents as “Clark Tuttle, Otter Creek.” He was dead by 5 May 1880, when the paper called his daughter “daughter of the late C. S. Tuttle.” No death notice survives — the Terre Haute dailies of those years are digitised nowhere. His widow Nancy was buried from Union Church in Otter Creek in April 1883. No stone marks either of them.

Nancy A. Harbert / Herbert (b. ~1826 Ohio)

Ahnentafel #97 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Nancy A. Harbert — the Vigo County clerk wrote it Herbert — was born about 1826 or 1827 in Ohio. Nothing is known of her parents or of how she came to Indiana.

On 10 July 1851 she married Clark Strong Tuttle, the miller of Otter Creek Township, before Harvey W. Allen, justice of the peace. His first wife Harriet had died five weeks earlier, leaving him with young children and a mill to run; four of Harriet's children were already buried in the Otter Creek ground. Nancy had six children of her own by him — Edward, Emma, Dennis, Richard B., Harriet B. and Dollie A. — and the line of this family runs through her.

The census of 1860 shows the house she kept: her husband a miller worth $14,000 in land, three hired hands and a servant living in, and every school-age child at school. She was thirty-three and born in Ohio, and that single line is the first recorded fact of any kind about her.

She outlived her husband by several years. She died on 25 April 1883 and was buried the next day from Union Church in Otter Creek Township, the church attached to the cemetery where her stepchildren lie. The paper gave her no name of her own: Mrs. Clark S. Tuttle — the widow of the late Clark S. Tuttle, the miller. No stone survives for her.

Francis “Frank” Fuerstenberger (~1826 Bavaria – 1894)

Ahnentafel #98 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Francis Fuerstenberger — Frank, to Terre Haute — was born in Bavaria in 1826 or 1827. He is the immigrant on this line, and nothing of his German town, his crossing or his naturalisation has been found; his name is mangled in every index that touches it, which is why the family was invisible for so long. The censuses have him as Fersenburger and Fuerstebuger; the marriage clerks as Ferstenberger and Furstenberger.

On 26 February 1852, in Clark County, Illinois, he married Sophia, a young widow. Their first child was born at Marshall, in that county, the following November. About 1854 they came to Terre Haute, and from that year to the end of his life he lived in one house: 521 South Third Street. He was a shoemaker, and by 1870 he owned $2,000 of real estate; on the same census page, a few doors away, lived another Bavarian in the leather trade — the immigrant quarter of a river city in miniature.

Eight children were born to them. In October 1881 his daughter Emma married Richard B. Tuttle, a young miller; three years later, in December 1884, Richard died of consumption, and the funeral was held at ten in the morning from Frank's own house on South Third Street. The widow and the baby stayed.

Sophia died about 1890. Frank died in the autumn of 1894, aged sixty-eight; the Saturday Evening Mail gave him a paragraph on its front page as “an old and well known citizen” and noted the thing he was known for — forty years in one city and all of it in one house. He lies at Highland Lawn Cemetery, Section 3, Lot 162, under a shared obelisk with Sophia, the inscription recording his age to the day: sixty-eight years and twenty-four days.

The earliest time his name appears in a Terre Haute newspaper is as a man who had not collected his post. On 15 October 1864 the Terre Haute Star printed the post office's list of uncalled letters, and in the gentlemen's column, between Foulke and Fort, stands "Fuerstenberger F."

The Clark County, Illinois record book holds both halves of his marriage. A licence was granted to Francis Fiestenburgen and Sophia Flint, 'now both of this County', in February 1852, signed by J. Stockwell, Clerk; and the minister's certificate beneath it reads that on the 26th day of February 1852 he joined them in the state of matrimony 'according to the usual custom and laws of the State of Illinois'. It was returned, registered and filed on 1 March 1852.

On the Wednesday before 1 March 1890 he and Sophia kept their fortieth wedding anniversary at the South Third Street house, "very delightfully," afternoon and evening. She was dead within four months.

The censuses of 1870 and 1880 both find him in Terre Haute, and long after he was gone, a family lawsuit over an inheritance in 1934 still named the heirs of five of his eight children.

Sophia Snyder (~1828 PA – 1890 Terre Haute)

Ahnentafel #99 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sophia was born in 1828 in Pennsylvania.

She was married before she met Francis Fuerstenberger. The Clark County, Illinois index records the bride of 26 February 1852 as Sophia Flint — so she had very probably been married before. That is almost certainly why a generation of searching under “Snyder” found nothing.

She and Francis had eight children between 1852 and 1870: John Fredrick, James W., Anna, Clara Rebecca, Emma, Daniel, George and Eliza. The family came to Terre Haute about 1854 and lived at 521 South Third Street, where Francis made shoes and where, in December 1884, her daughter Emma's young husband was buried out of the front room.

She married as SOPHIA FLINT. The Clark County, Illinois book names her that way twice on one page -- once in the licence granted to her and Francis Fiestenburgen, 'now both of this County', and again in the minister's certificate of 26 February 1852. Her being a widow Flint rather than a Snyder at the altar is why the Clark County free layer went on returning nothing for 'Snyder'.

She died on 15 June 1890 at 520 South Third Street, Terre Haute, aged sixty-two, of pleuro-pneumonia, and was buried at Highland Lawn — four months after she and Francis had kept their fortieth wedding anniversary in the same house.

She died on 14 June of pleuro-pneumonia, in that house, aged sixty-two years, three months and two days, and was interred the following day at Highland Lawn. The year is in dispute: the project's own records and Find a Grave's header say 1889, while the City of Terre Haute's old cemetery database, quoted on her memorial, says 1890 — and her husband's 1894 death notice, which said his wife had died “four years ago,” points the same way. She lies with Francis in Section 3, Lot 162, under a stone that records her age but not her birth.

John Fletcher (b. Pennsylvania or Indiana)

Ahnentafel #102 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Fletcher is known from one document. When his daughter Mary Elizabeth died at East Ellen, Vigo County, Indiana, on 16 April 1934, the certificate named her father as John Fletcher and her mother as Sarah Wallace. Nothing else so far found says the two of them belonged to each other.

Where he was born is in dispute, and the dispute is his daughter's own. In 1880, enumerated as a servant at Clinton, she gave her father's birthplace as Pennsylvania; in 1900, keeping her own house in Terre Haute, she gave it as Indiana. Both answers are hers and twenty years apart, and no Fletcher record has yet been found to decide between them.

He was probably dead before 1882, when Indiana's county death indexes begin — which would explain why no death record for him exists. His daughter was living in another family's house as a domestic at twenty-three, a year before her wedding, which is what happens when the parental household has gone. No marriage of a Fletcher to a Wallace appears anywhere in the Vermillion, Parke or Vigo county registers that survive, and one reading of that silence is that they married in another state before coming to Indiana.

Sarah (Wallace) Fletcher (b. Indiana)

Ahnentafel #103 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Sarah Wallace was born in Indiana — that much her daughter reported twice, in 1880 and again in 1900, without varying. She married John Fletcher, and their daughter Mary Elizabeth was born on 21 September 1858.

She is named, with her husband, on one document only: Mary Elizabeth's Indiana death certificate of 1934, which gives the mother as Sarah Wallace. No marriage record for the couple has been found in any surviving register of Vermillion, Parke or Vigo County, and no household holding a John and Sarah Fletcher with a daughter Mary of the right age appears in the Indiana census indexes for 1860 or 1870.

Vermillion County had a cluster of pioneer Wallaces — Robert Wallace, a Virginian who reached Newport in 1828 and died there in 1881 aged ninety-one, and a run of Wallace daughters marrying out of the same township in the 1830s. No Sarah appears among them, and nothing connects her to that family but the surname and the county. It is a lead and no more.
