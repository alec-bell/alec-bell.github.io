Ben Simmons (1818 GA – 1894 Kewanee MS)

Ahnentafel #78 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Asbury Simmons — B. A. Simmons on his gravestone and Ben on most people's tongues — was born on 26 October 1818 in Georgia, in Troup County by his family's reckoning, and came as a young man with his people into Sumter County, Alabama. His father was very probably Robert Simmons of that county, who in the fifth item of a will written on 6 March 1845 set down: “I hereby appoint my Son Benjamin Asbury Simmons my executor to this my last will and testament.” The same will shows how that household looked after its women: the daughters' share was granted “to them and the heirs of their bodies; and not to be subject to the debts of their husbands.” The will was proved on 15 November 1855. Ben's brothers and sisters were Mariah Jane, who married a Bustin, William Miles, John Christopher, Robert H., an E. W. and Susan H., who married a Boyd and died young in 1852.

He married Adeline on 11 May 1847, when he was twenty-eight and she was seventeen. Their children's birthplaces trace a family moving back and forth along the state line: Alabama in the late 1840s, Mississippi in the early 1850s, Alabama again by the end of that decade. By 1870 he was at Cuba with Adeline and six children in the house, and he was still there in 1880, with a sixteen-year-old daughter among them whose name the clerk wrote down as “Dellon” — Rebecca Anna Dillon Simmons. Two of his daughters married the same Swain man, twenty-two months apart, so which of them carries Alec's descent is still an open question. Either way the parents are this couple, though the step down to the Swains is probable rather than certain.

He was a miller as well as a farmer. When he registered to vote at Cuba on 3 July 1875, entry 64 of the Sumter County book, he wrote his own name in a strong, practised hand, where most of the page made marks, and the registrar gave his living in three words: “Mill & Farm.” His was a slaveholding family before the war — in 1855 his brother Lewis was taxed for twenty-nine enslaved people, Robert H. for fifteen and William M. for two.

He ended his life just across the line at Kewanee, in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, and died there on 6 April 1894, aged seventy-five. His marble headstone in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, carved with two clasped hands, reads B. A. SIMMONS, HUSBAND OF ADALINE SIMMONS, BORN OCT. 26, 1818, DIED APRIL 6, 1894.

Adeline Simmons (1829 AL – 1900 Kewanee MS)

Adeline was born on 7 October 1829, probably in Walton County, Florida, though in later life she was put down as Alabama-born as often as Florida-born. Her maiden name was most likely Peavy, and her father Levi Daniel Peavy. She married Ben Simmons on 11 May 1847, at seventeen, and bore nine or ten children over the next twenty years, the last about 1867 when she was thirty-seven. At least two did not live out infancy.

She kept house at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama, through the war and after it, with a few years in Mississippi in the early 1850s that show up only in where her middle children were born. In 1870 she was “Adline,” forty-one, with six children at home; in 1880 she still had a sixteen-year-old daughter under the roof, written down as “Dellon.” It is through one of her daughters that Alec's line runs, and which one is still unsettled, so her place in it is probable rather than certain.

A sepia photograph of her survives from about 1875: a woman seated in a dark dress with a lace collar and a tasselled ribbon at her throat, hands still, looking straight out.

Her husband died in April 1894. She outlived him by six years and died at Kewanee, in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, at seventy. Her tall marble headstone at Salem Baptist, beside his, was cut with a weeping-willow panel: IN MEMORY OF ADELINE, WIFE OF B. A. SIMMONS, BORN OCT. 7, 1829, DIED SEPT. 25, 1900, AGED 70 YRS. 11 MOS. 16 DAYS.

Henry H. Stephens (1822 NC – 1879 Sumter Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #80 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Henry H. Stephens was born on 8 August 1822 in North Carolina, in Orange County by the family’s account, the son of Moore Stephens and Rebecca Moize. He was a small child when the family left for Alabama — they were in Greene County by 1824 — and he grew up on the Alabama side of the Tombigbee country, moving on with his father into Sumter County.

On 4 June 1846, at twenty-three, he married Sarah Ann Jones, born in North Carolina about 1833, whose family lived a few doors from his own. In the summer of 1850 the whole arrangement sat on one page of the census: in the “Intercourse” division of Sumter County, enumerated on 7 June, Henry was twenty-seven and farming at dwelling 1184 with Sarah, seventeen, and a son Calvin, aged one, and a boarder in the house, Redden Marshall, twenty-three and North Carolina-born. Two dwellings along lived Daniel Jones, Sarah’s father; six houses away, at dwelling 1190, lived Henry’s own father Moore. A son, Caswell Massey Stephens, was born on 8 January 1852.

Sarah died young, and nothing records her death. On 21 October 1854 Henry married again, to Elizabeth Jones — almost certainly his first wife’s younger sister — before Jesse B. Thorne, Justice of the Peace. He was a Minister of the Gospel as well as a farmer, and the Sumter County marriage registers have him performing weddings through the 1860s and seventies, his own son Caswell’s among them in October 1871.

By 1860 the family had followed Moore Stephens west into Newton County, Mississippi, where Henry was listed with Elizabeth, twenty-one, and the children Caroline, Caswell, James and William. He was forty when the war reached him, with a second wife and small children at home and no exemption — he was liable to Confederate conscription from September 1862. He served, and the company he most likely marched in was the Newton County company of the 5th Regiment, Mississippi Infantry (State Troops) — the Minute Men — where an H. H. Stephens appears as a private in 1863, with a W. Stephens and a Z. Stephens beside him on the roll and a cluster of Joneses: Daniel, Samuel, William, Riley, H. D., A. and W., the surname he had married into twice. The 5th mustered from 31 July to 1 October 1862 and again from 17 July to 21 September 1863, and it was at Vicksburg: the regiment’s papers afterwards count the men paroled there on 5 August 1863, with the killed, the dead of wounds and the dead of disease through the siege beside them. His widow, swearing to his service forty years later, remembered it differently — 2nd Lieutenant in Company I of the 4th Mississippi Infantry Volunteers — but that company was the Benela Sharpshooters of Calhoun County, two hundred miles from anywhere this family lived, and no Stephens of his initials is on its roll.

He came home. By 1870 the family was back at Cuba, in Township 17 of Sumter County, Alabama — the third leg of one continuous migration. On 31 December 1869 he stood in the Probate Court at a special term before Judge J. A. Abrahams and was appointed guardian of three of his own sons, Caswell M., Calvin D. and John M., each over fourteen and each described as “a minor heir of Daniel Jones deceased,” posting bonds of $800, $800 and $1,000; the property was real enough that he was filing annual settlements two years later. On 22 June 1875 he registered to vote at Thornville, Precinct No. 3 of Sumter County, entry 25, writing “H. H. Stephens,” farmer, in his own hand. Two lines down the same page a D. C. Stephens registered the same day, and immediately above him A. J. Shelton, “Farmer & Blk Smith.”

He died on 20 October 1879 at Gaston, in Sumter County. His gravestone reckons his life to the day — born 8 August 1822, died 20 October 1879, aged 57 years, 2 months and 12 days — and carries a Masonic square and compasses inside a carved wreath. The carver spelled the family name STEAVENS.

What the war and his death left behind shows in one document, sworn a quarter of a century later. On 4 July 1904, in Sumter County, Elizabeth Stephens went before the county pension examiner R. H. Hale and swore that she was “the widow of H. H. Stevens, who served as a 2[nd] Lieutenant in company I of the 4th Regiment Miss Infantry Volunteers,” that her husband “died on the 29 day of Oct, 1879,” and “that he did not desert the service of the State of Alabama, nor of the Confederate States.” Two men of the neighbourhood swore the same day that they had known him personally and that “the services set forth in the application were rendered by said H. H. Stephens” — W. H. Stephens, very probably one of his own family, and T. J. Tucker. The Sumter County board passed it, and on 17 August 1904 the State Board of Examiners at Montgomery placed her in class 4 under the pension act of 10 February 1899. With it she filed a Schedule of Property, a printed form with a line for every kind of thing a Black Belt household could own — acres of land, town property, horses and mules, cattle, hogs, sheep and goats, pistols and guns, watches and clocks and jewelry, household and kitchen furniture, bicycles and vehicles, goods and merchandise, mechanical and farming tools. She wrote a nought on every line, and a nought at the total, and the application says she had “no children upon whom she can depend for support.”

Sarah Ann Jones (b. ~1833 North Carolina)

Ahnentafel #81 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Ann Jones was born about 1833 in North Carolina, almost certainly the eldest daughter of Daniel Jones. Her younger brothers and sisters were all Alabama-born from about 1837, which puts the family’s move south in the middle thirties, with Sarah born just before it.

On 4 June 1846, at about thirteen — fourteen or fifteen if her recorded age runs light — she married Henry H. Stephens in Sumter County, Alabama. She had her first child, Calvin, at about sixteen. In 1850 she was “Sarah Stephens, 17,” keeping house at dwelling 1184 in the Intercourse division of Sumter County with her husband, their one-year-old son and a boarder. Her father’s house stood two doors away and her husband’s father’s six houses off; the whole connection fitted into one short row. Caswell Massey Stephens was born to her on 8 January 1852, and a son John M. probably followed.

She died somewhere between then and October 1854, when Henry remarried — the new wife, Elizabeth Jones, in all likelihood her own younger sister. Nothing records her death, her age at it, or where she lies. What survives instead is an inheritance: in December 1869 her three sons were bonded in the Sumter County probate court as “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” a share that could only reach grandsons through a parent already dead. That is the nearest thing she has to an epitaph.

John Wesley Truett (1833 – 1914)

Ahnentafel #88 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Wesley Truett was born on 20 October 1833, the son of the Reverend William Hampton Truett and Alcey Wiggins.

On 2 November 1854, at twenty-one, he married Emily Caroline Ward in Henry County, Alabama. She was seventeen, so her father had to consent, and five days before the wedding W. H. Ward wrote it out for the clerk to copy into the marriage book: “license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” His father-in-law wrote a firm, practised hand. The bridegroom could not write at all.

They moved three hundred miles west to Clarke County, and the children came — Sarah Ann in 1858, William Curtis in 1867, George in 1872, John Wesley in 1875, Elisha Dozier in 1877, Carrie in 1878 — and he farmed at Chilton.

He enlisted on 15 October 1862 at Coffeeville, his own village, signing on “for the War” with Colonel Alexander McKinstry: Private, Company D, 32nd Alabama Infantry, under Captain Cox — George W. Cox, one of the two captains the county furnished to that regiment. He was twenty-eight. Four weeks later he was in the Confederate post hospital at Dalton, Georgia, with chronic rheumatism, and he never really came back. He was sent on to Tullahoma in February 1863, drew clothing at Dalton that April and signed the receipt with an X, and was furloughed for thirty days at the end of May. Every muster roll says the same word, absent. Then the rolls stop, in the summer of 1863, with no discharge, no capture and no parole; fifty-seven years later his widow, asked on a state form when he had quit the service, left the line blank. The family did not know either.

In 1880 the house at Chilton held him at forty-six, “Emlie C.”, the children — William Curtis among them — and his mother Alecey, the preacher’s widow, at sixty-seven. In 1907 the state counted him among its surviving old soldiers at Coffeeville, aged seventy-three.

On 9 July 1909, at Grove Hill, he went in person before the county pension board. What he owned, sworn on the schedule: thirty-six acres at $150, a “Small Frame Box House one Room” at $25, and one cow and calf at $10. The other eleven categories — horses, hogs, guns, watches, furniture, tools — all say None. Asked what made him unable to make a living, the board wrote three words: catarrh of the head. It wrote to the State Auditor at Montgomery that “he has some head trouble, is unable to do but little work, has no income except what little work he can do himself, no one to look to for support,” and the form says he had no children upon whom he could depend, though six of them were living. Two neighbours swore to his service, Elijah Brown and J. H. Geiger senior, and both signed with a mark, as he did — John his X mark Truett, the same mark he had made at Dalton forty-six years before. The application also has him enlisting at Murfreesboro in Tennessee and honourably discharged in May 1865, neither of which matches the rolls of his own company; whoever filled it in for him was working from an old man’s memory. The county board put him in Class 4, the state board at Montgomery agreed, and he drew pension number 27126 in Clarke County until he died.

Another Truett lies at New Prospect under a Confederate government marker reading G. W. Truett, Corporal, Company D, 32nd Alabama Infantry — his own company — and how the two of them were kin has never been worked out.

He died on 17 April 1914, aged eighty, and lies at Oak Grove Cemetery, Chilton, under a rounded marble headstone with clasped hands carved at the top and the words GONE BUT NOT FORGOTTEN. Caroline outlived him by eight years and lies three hundred miles away in Florida. A studio photograph of the two of them survives, with their son George standing behind.
