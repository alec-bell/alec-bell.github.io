Susannah Roush (d. ~1796 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #1001 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah married John Adam Roush about 1739, soon after he landed at Philadelphia, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. She was the mother of the whole American Roush family — ten children, seven of them sons, and every one of the sons went into the Revolution. Her own maiden name is lost; Sehler, or Schlern, circulates in family trees, but no source for it has ever been produced.

Very little else of her life was ever written down. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” She stood as a baptismal sponsor at the Old Pine Church in 1770, and again on 5 January 1786 — nine months before her husband died — that second time as a grandmother. After his death the church’s list of communicants carries “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, and then never again.

She died at or about 1796, ten years after her husband, and was buried beside him at the Old Pine Church. “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked,” the family’s historian wrote, “but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When the old stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with them, because there was nothing to move. In 1936 the Roush and Allied Families Association cut her name into the bicentennial monument there — “1713 – HIS WIFE, SUSANNAH – 1796” — the only stone that carries it, and the only thing that gives her a birth year at all.

Alec descends from her three times over, her grandson John and her granddaughter Regina having married each other. This is the third of the three, through her son Henry Sr., the Church Builder.

Timothy Tuttle (~1682 New Haven – 1756 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #1536 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Timothy Tuttle was born about 1682, probably at New Haven, by the family genealogy of 1883 the son of Simon Tuttle, a cooper, and his wife Abigail, a descent very probably right but not yet proved from a record. He was about three when the family removed to Wallingford, soon after his grandmother’s death. He grew up there, and as a young man he went out west of the river to look for land, chose a spot, and cleared it with his own hands — staying out through the week because it was too far to walk home each night, and finding his way back on Saturdays along a line of trees he had blazed through the woods. The place was about a mile east of what became the centre of Cheshire.

He put up a house there about 1706, the same season he married: on 2 November 1706 he took Thankful Doolittle to wife at Wallingford. The family called it the first frame house in Cheshire, and it was at the least the second; under its roof, as one neighbour wrote, were born the builder’s children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren and great-great-grandchildren. Late in the nineteenth century, standing empty, it was burned by an incendiary.

His children by Thankful were Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simeon in 1721 and Moses in 1723. That genealogy records a gift of nine and a half acres from Simon on 10 July 1711. He was one of the farmers on the west side of the river who petitioned in December 1715 for a parish of their own — the bounds they asked for ran “west from ye rever as high as Timothy tuttle & timothy Beach’s” — and when the new Cheshire society held its first meeting, on 16 September 1723, he was chosen moderator. He sat on the committee that managed the ordination; he was “Sergeant Tuttle” of the school-house committee in 1729 and society moderator again in 1732; and by 1 December 1741 he is “decon Timothy Tuttle” on the committee to petition for town privileges. He lived near the west end of the road to the south of Broad Swamp, four miles west of the river.

Thankful died in November 1728. He married again in June 1729, Mary, widow of Stephen Rowe, who died in January 1747/8; and last, on 28 June 1749, Sarah (Atwater) Humiston, widow of James Humiston. He had also taken a proprietor’s share in one of the new townships granted up in Hampshire County, Massachusetts, and in 1736 he signed the whole of that right over to his second son — “for divers good causes and considerations and perticularly for love, good will and fatherly affection that I have and [bear] to my son Gershom Tuttle.” It is the deed that sent the next generation north.

He made his will on 1 December 1753, “being in ye 71st Year of my age.” Sarah was to have the bay mare, whichever half of the dwelling house she chose, gardens, the young orchard and her firewood from the farm, “for such term of time only as she remains to be my Widow.” His eldest son Ephraim took the house and barn, “he not disturbing his Mother in her use of the same.” To Gershom he left six acres at the Swamp Pasture and thirty pounds old tenor; to Timothy ten pounds and to Simeon twenty; to Moses land and the Great Swamp. The moveable estate went to his daughters Mary, Abigail and Mehitabel and to the children of his dead daughter Rachel. And he set aside twenty pounds old tenor to go into the hands of the minister and deacons, the interest “to be for ye poor of sd Parish as they judge best.”

He died on 15 April 1756 and lies in Hillside Cemetery at Cheshire, in the parish he had helped to found, under a brownstone carved with a winged soul effigy: “Here Lies ye Body of Deacon Timothy Tuttle he died April 15th 1756 in ye 75th year of his Age.”

Thankful Doolittle (~1688 Wallingford – 1728)

Ahnentafel #1537 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Thankful Doolittle was born about 1688, probably at Wallingford, Connecticut — the family’s genealogists say in the spring, a daughter of John Doolittle and Mary Peck — and a granddaughter, on her father’s side, of Abraham Doolittle, one of the men who founded the town. She married young, at about eighteen: on 2 November 1706 Justice Hall married her to Timothy Tuttle, who built his house out west of the river that same season.

The town book enters ten children of Timothy and Thankful across twenty years: Rachel (her birth is written 1706, months before the wedding, most likely a clerk’s slip), Ebenezer in 1708, Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Abigail in 1719, Simon in 1721, Moses in 1723 and a last daughter, Thankfull, in November 1726. In March 1725 the new church at Cheshire received her into membership, entered on the roll not under her own name but as “wife of Timothy Tuttle”; her husband was not admitted until January 1738/9, ten years after she was dead.

If the family’s genealogists have her parents right — and the two oldest Wallingford genealogies leave her out of John Doolittle’s family, giving a Susannah on the birthday later writers gave her — then her brother was the Reverend Benjamin Doolittle, who took his degree at Yale in 1716 and was settled from 1718 as minister at Northfield, out on the Massachusetts frontier. When her son Gershom went north as a young man to take up his father’s land above Northfield, it was his uncle’s house he lodged in. Her family is the reason the Tuttles knew that country at all.

She died on 23 November 1728, aged forty, when her youngest, the daughter who carried her name, had just turned two. Where she is buried nobody knows. Her husband married again thirteen months later. Wallingford’s books, as they were copied in the 1920s, record her marriage and her death, but not her birth: the town’s page of Doolittle children has every other Thankful and not her, and her wedding entry names no parents. So the link to the Doolittles rests on the family’s genealogists, and it stays probable rather than settled.

William Allis (1680/84 Hatfield – 1763 Montague)

Ahnentafel #1538 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (10 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

William Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, a grandson of the first William Allis of Braintree and Hatfield. Which of that man’s sons was his father is not settled. Hatfield had two cousins named William Allis of nearly the same age. One was born on 19 October 1680 to Samuel Allis, the carpenter and builder of Hatfield street, and his wife Alice. The other was born on 16 May 1684 to Captain John Allis and his wife Mary. Both were alive in 1712, when Captain John’s children signed a family agreement that names their own brother William and, separately, “Cousin William Allis”. In 1729 a “William Allis of Sunderland” sold land together with Captain John’s children, which now points to Captain John’s son, though no record yet settles it. If he was Samuel’s son, he was ten when his father died, and twenty-three on the morning of 29 February 1704 when his mother, remarried and living at Deerfield, was killed in the French and Indian raid, along with his brother Samuel. If he was Captain John’s son, Alice was his aunt.

He had married Elizabeth Davis of Northampton in 1703 or 1704. Their children were Mary, born 18 February 1705, and Lois, born 13 January 1707/8, both at Hatfield; Eliphalet in 1710, also at Hatfield; then Zebediah in 1713 and Elizabeth in 1716, both at Sunderland — so the household moved upriver between the end of 1710 and the autumn of 1713. At Sunderland he appraised a neighbour’s estate in 1739 and deeded land to his son Zebadiah. On 18 August 1748 he made over to Zebadiah the homestead itself: a house lot of twenty-two and a half acres on the Sawmill River, “including a House and a barn orcharding and fence”, with his son Eliphalet’s land on one side and his son-in-law Joseph Mitchell’s on the other. He added all his land in Sunderland not already conveyed, and his clothing, horse furniture and household goods “found after my Decease”. He was still able to ride in and acknowledge the deed before a justice in March 1755. Later the whole family moved again, across the river to Montague, where he was “Mr. William Allis”, a title of standing in a New England town. His wife died there on 1 May 1758. Montague could be a hard place to live: in January 1759 his son Eliphalet and his wife Mary buried four of their children in nine days — Mary on the 7th, Jonathan on the 10th, Samuel on the 12th, David on the 15th.

William Allis died at Montague on 20 February 1763, aged eighty-two. He stands in this family’s pedigree twice over: his daughter Lois married Gershom Tuttle in 1737, his daughter Mary married Joseph Mitchell in 1726, and in 1757 Mary’s daughter married Lois’s son, so two of his children’s lines run down to the same descendants.

Michael Mitchell (Wallingford Conn. → Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #1540 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Michael Mitchell was a Wallingford, Connecticut man — a proprietor of the town, casting lots with the other townsmen in 1695–6 and still there in 1706. Where he came from before that nobody has ever been able to say. About 1694 he married Sarah, daughter of John Catlin of Deerfield.

He moved his family to Deerfield, the most exposed town on the Massachusetts frontier. On the morning of 29 February 1704, when the French and their allies came over the stockade, his whole family escaped unharmed — which in that town, on that morning, was the exception. His wife’s father and brother were burned in their own house a few hundred yards away.

His children were William, born 1697; Michael, born 8 March 1700/1, afterwards a soldier in Father Rasle’s war and later of Wethersfield, Connecticut; Joseph, born 1705, the weaver, through whom this family descends; and Samuel, born 7 March 1712, very probably the Samuel Mitchell granted land at Sunderland in 1737 on condition that he stayed five years. Sarah died on 30 December 1736.

His Wallingford years matter more than they look. The Tuttles were farming west of the river at Wallingford in exactly those years, and Gershom Tuttle was born there in 1714 — so when Michael’s granddaughter Tabitha married Gershom’s son at Deerfield in 1757, it was not a chance meeting a hundred miles from home. It was two Wallingford families who had both gone north, marrying each other.

Sarah Catlin (m. ~1694 – d. 1736)

Ahnentafel #1541 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Catlin was a daughter of John Catlin — a Wethersfield man who taught school at Newark in New Jersey, served as that town’s attorney and five years its selectman, and then brought his family to Deerfield about 1683, where he was dignified with the title of “Mr.” — and of his wife Mary Baldwin. About 1694 she married Michael Mitchell, and her eldest child was born in 1697.

That marriage is probably why she lived. On 29 February 1704, when the raid came, she was under her own husband’s roof, and the Mitchell household came through untouched. Her father’s did not. His buildings were all burned and he and his son Jonathan were consumed with them; another son was killed in the Meadow Fight later the same morning; a son and three daughters were carried off toward Canada. Two of those daughters were her sisters — Elizabeth, wife of James Corse, and Mary, wife of Thomas Wells — and both were killed on the march. In a town where a hundred and twelve people were marched away in a single morning, it was said of her father that “no family suffered more than his.”

She bore at least four sons — William, Michael, Joseph and Samuel — and died on 30 December 1736, about two months before her granddaughter Tabitha was born. She lies in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, in the same ground as the common grave of the dead of 1704, her father and her brother among them; time and weather have taken the place of her own grave.
