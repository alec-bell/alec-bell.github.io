William Allis (fl. 1640 Braintree – 1678 Hatfield)

Ahnentafel #6152 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Immigrant

William Allis was born in England, probably between 1613 and 1616; the family tradition puts his birthplace in Essex or London and brings him over with Winthrop's fleet in 1630, but nothing outside the family's own telling confirms it. He is certain from 13 May 1640, when he was made a freeman of Braintree, Massachusetts, and the Braintree town book then carries him through the 1650s as a selectman, as “Deacon Allis”, and as a commissioner, with Thomas Meekins holding land next to his.

In 1641 he married a woman named Mary, whose surname has not survived. Their children were born at Braintree: John in 1642, Samuel on 24 February 1647 — the son through whom this family descends — two boys named Josiah, two named William, Hannah, and Mary. About 1661 he moved the family west to Hatfield on the Connecticut River.

There his title stops being a courtesy and becomes a command. When King Philip's War set the valley towns to garrisoning themselves, the commands were parcelled out: thirty-nine men under Pynchon at Springfield, twenty-nine at Westfield, twenty-six at Northampton, thirty at Hadley — and thirty-six at Hatfield, under Lieut. Wm. Allis, the largest force in the valley after Springfield's. Six more soldiers were ordered to him, to be employed through the winter getting out timber for the fortifications at Hatfield and Deerfield. In the same war, on 19 May 1676, his son William, aged twenty, was killed in the Falls Fight at Turners Falls.

His wife Mary died on 10 August 1677. On 25 June 1678 he married Mary, daughter of John Bronson and widow of John Graves of Haddam — and died ten weeks later, in September 1678. A Deerfield land redistribution of 1680 notes it in brackets, as a thing everyone in the valley knew: “[Lieut. Wm. Allis had died September, 1678.]”

Mary married William Allis in 1641 and spent the next twenty years at Braintree, Massachusetts, bearing a family of eight. The town register records the births without ever giving her a surname: “Mary Allis the dauter of wilom Allis and mary his wife was born the (25)(8)(53)”; “William Allis the sonne of William Allis and Mary his wife was borne the (10)(11)1655”; and, on the same page, the death of a baby — “Josia Allis the sone of William Allis and Mary his wife dyed the (15)(8)1651”. William Davis (~1617 – 1683 Roxbury Mass.)

William Davis was born about 1617 and lived at Roxbury, Massachusetts, where he was made a freeman in 1673. His children come into view in a single entry in the Roxbury church book in 1649, when the congregation received the children of a newly admitted member: “John Davis, Samuel Davis, Joseph Davis — Children of William Davis whose wife was admitted & so brought her children to p'take of this blessed ordinance.” Samuel, born 21 February 1645, is the son through whom this family descends, and the same church book marks his going: “Samuel Davis dismissed to North-Hampton”, when he went west up the Connecticut valley.

He married twice. His first wife was Elizabeth, who died or was buried on 4 May 1658. He married second, that October, Alice Thorp, and the Roxbury clerk wrote her end in one unsparing line: “Alice wife of William Davis who dyed in child birth undelivered.” He was probably a brother of Tobias Davis of the same town — a household that has to be kept carefully apart from his, because it held a Samuel too.

He made his will on 6 December 1683 and died three days later, on the 9th, aged about sixty-six. That the Samuel who settled at Northampton is the same boy brought to the ordinance at Roxbury in 1649 is probable rather than certain.

Joseph Baldwin (Milford 1639 – d. 1684 Hadley)

Ahnentafel #6166 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Immigrant

Joseph Baldwin was among the first settlers of Milford, Connecticut, in 1639, one of three brothers who came over together and were given home lots there after a first stop at New Haven. He married Hannah, who joined the Milford church on 23 June 1644, when their first four children were all baptized at once; Mary, who married John Catlin, was one of the four. Hannah died in 1661, and about 1663 Joseph moved north to Hadley in Massachusetts, where he was made a freeman in 1666. He married twice more: Isabella, the widow of the elder John Catlin and afterwards of James Northam, who died in 1676; and Elizabeth, the widow of Luke Hitchcock and then of William Warriner.

On 20 December 1680 he made his will, and its first words say exactly where he was and how he felt: “In the Name of God Amen the twentieth day of december, One thousand Six hundred and eighty, I Joseph Baldwin Senr of Hadley in the Countie of Hampshire within the Colony of the Massachusetts: being together with old age and of life now Sick and weake of body but of good and perfect Memory, thanks and praise be to Almighty God for it.” He left his wife the use of his house and land at Hadley for her life, excepting the half of his lands he had already made over to his son Joseph; the other half, with the houses and the moveables, went to that son. Then he went through the daughters one by one — five pounds to his “Loveing Daughter Hanah Hull” and five pounds to her daughter Mary Hull when she should reach eighteen; forty shillings to “my Daughter Mary Cattlin”; a legacy to Elizabeth Warriner of Springfield; something to the children of his daughter Sarah Bartlett, already dead; five pounds each to Martha Hawks and Hannah Hawks — and confirmed to his three sons Benjamin, Jonathan and David the gifts he had made them out of his Milford lands. He signed it before two witnesses, Chiliab Smith, who made his mark, and Samuel Partrigg.

Nearly four years later, on 30 October 1684, he had the will read over to him again and ratified it, appointing his grandson Joseph Baldwin junior his sole executor in place of the son, who had died before him. He lived only four days more, and died on 3 November 1684. The inventory of his estate was taken on the 8th by Lieutenant Smith, Chiliab Smith and Samuel Partrigg: £165 8s 7d in all, of which his house, barn and home lot at Hadley stood at £50 and ten acres of land at £45, with debts of £10 14s 3d, leaving a net estate of £154 14s 4d.

In the town he had helped to found at the other end of the colony, Milford set a granite boulder by the Wepawaug bridge among its founders' memorials: JOSEPH BALDWIN, OBIT 1690, HANNAH HIS WIFE. The date cut on it is wrong by six years.

William Peck was born in 1601 and was a merchant in London. In 1637 he sailed for New England on the Hector with his wife Elizabeth and their young son Jeremiah, in the company of Theophilus Eaton, Edward Hopkins and the Reverend John Davenport; the ship reached Boston on 26 June. He went with that company to Quinnipiac, and in June 1639 he signed the Fundamental Agreement that founded New Haven. He was made a freeman there on 29 October 1640.

His home lot was on George Street and his shop stood on the south-easterly side of Church Street, fronting it. He was never a rich man, but he was a trusted one. He was one of three men who inventoried the estate of Sergeant Thomas Jeffrie in 1661. In December 1662 he stood up at a New Haven town meeting to tell it that the town's elders were not getting enough to eat: there was not wheat and malt enough to go round. With Roger Alling he was appointed a trustee of the fund, £918 in all, that paid for the colony's collegiate grammar school. From 1659 until his death — thirty-five years — he was deacon of the First Church of New Haven, and that is the name he kept.

Elizabeth died in December 1683, and about a year later he married Sarah, the widow of William Holt; his will, drawn 9 March 1689, mentions the second wife and his four children, all of them Elizabeth's. Elizabeth Peck (England 1637 – d. 1683)

Ahnentafel #12301 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Elizabeth was the wife of William Peck, the London merchant who became deacon of the First Church at New Haven. They were married in London, and in 1637 she crossed to New England with him and their young son Jeremiah on the Hector, in the company that would found New Haven; the ship made Boston on 26 June. Their home lot in the new town was on George Street, and her husband's shop fronted on Church Street.

All four of William Peck's children were hers — Jeremiah, John, Joseph and Elizabeth — and they are the four named in his will. John, born about 1638, is the son in this line. She died on 5 December 1683, away from home: she was visiting her son at Lyme, forty miles down the coast, when she died. Eleven years later her husband died in the same place, at their son Joseph's house.

She lies in New Haven. Her grave was on the Old Green with the rest of the founding generation and was moved to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821, where a small rough stone against the north wall, eight feet from her husband's, carries nothing but a date and two initials: 1683, and under it E P. Her maiden name was never written down by anyone who knew it.

John Moss (New Haven 1639 – 1707 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #12302 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Moss was at New Haven by 1639, in the first years of the colony, and he is one of the three names — with Doolittle and Peck — that between them made Wallingford. He served the older town first. He was one of the commissioners New Haven chose to settle its disputed boundary with Branford: they met on 5 October 1669 and, after full debate, set the bounds and ended the case for ever. Through the 1640s, 1650s and 1660s the colony's records carry him as a trainband corporal, a representative and a deputy. In 1670, when Wallingford was incorporated, he moved inland with the rest, and the town's historian, writing in 1870, called him flatly “the ancestor of all who bear the name in these parts.”

He and his wife had eleven children baptized at New Haven between 1640 and 1655. Among them were Mary, baptized 11 April 1647, who married John Peck in 1664 and is the one in this line; Abigail, who married Abraham Doolittle as his second wife; and a second John, born 1650, who carried his father's name and has been taken for him ever since.

He died in 1707, and the striking thing said of him was said everywhere: that he was 103 years old, and the oldest man who had ever died in Connecticut. The claim was local and old — the stonecutter carved it, and it was repeated from a Wallingford pulpit at the town's centennial in 1770 — and whether he truly reached it is another matter. His stone stands in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, a heart cut into its face, and inside the heart: “Mr John Moss, born 1604, died 1707, Æ 103.”
