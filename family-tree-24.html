Elizabeth Peck (England 1637 – d. 1683)

Ahnentafel #12301 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Elizabeth was the wife of William Peck, the London merchant who became deacon of the First Church at New Haven. They were married in London, and in 1637 she crossed to New England with him and their young son Jeremiah on the Hector, in the company that would found New Haven; the ship made Boston on 26 June. Their home lot in the new town was on George Street, and her husband's shop fronted on Church Street.

All four of William Peck's children were hers — Jeremiah, John, Joseph and Elizabeth — and they are the four named in his will. John, born about 1638, is the son in this line. She died on 5 December 1683, away from home: she was visiting her son at Lyme, forty miles down the coast, when she died. Eleven years later her husband died in the same place, at their son Joseph's house.

She lies in New Haven. Her grave was on the Old Green with the rest of the founding generation and was moved to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821, where a small rough stone against the north wall, eight feet from her husband's, carries nothing but a date and two initials: 1683, and under it E P. Her maiden name was never written down by anyone who knew it.

John Moss (New Haven 1639 – 1707 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #12302 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Moss was at New Haven by 1639, in the first years of the colony, and he is one of the three names — with Doolittle and Peck — that between them made Wallingford. He served the older town first. He was one of the commissioners New Haven chose to settle its disputed boundary with Branford: they met on 5 October 1669 and, after full debate, set the bounds and ended the case for ever. Through the 1640s, 1650s and 1660s the colony's records carry him as a trainband corporal, a representative and a deputy. In 1670, when Wallingford was incorporated, he moved inland with the rest, and the town's historian, writing in 1870, called him flatly “the ancestor of all who bear the name in these parts.”

He and his wife had eleven children baptized at New Haven between 1640 and 1655. Among them were Mary, baptized 11 April 1647, who married John Peck in 1664 and is the one in this line; Abigail, who married Abraham Doolittle as his second wife; and a second John, born 1650, who carried his father's name and has been taken for him ever since.

He died in 1707, and the striking thing said of him was said everywhere: that he was 103 years old, and the oldest man who had ever died in Connecticut. The claim was local and old — the stonecutter carved it, and it was repeated from a Wallingford pulpit at the town's centennial in 1770 — and whether he truly reached it is another matter. His stone stands in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, a heart cut into its face, and inside the heart: “Mr John Moss, born 1604, died 1707, Æ 103.”

Richard Tootill (d. by 1590 Woodford, Northants)

Ahnentafel #24576 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (14 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Most of what is told of Richard Tootill comes from a genealogy of 1952; an index of Northamptonshire wills confirms that a Richard Tootill of Woodford left one, but the will itself has not yet been read. It places him at Woodford, a Northamptonshire village a few miles up the Nene from Ringstead, born about 1530. His neighbours trusted him with their affairs: he witnessed the will of Elizabeth Lyncoln in 1556 and oversaw that of Richard Beare in 1579. His own will was proved at Peterborough on 11 March 1589/90, that is 1590 by the modern calendar, with his widow Elizabeth and his son Anthony as executors, and it is said to name seven children: Symon, Anthony, Elizabeth, Ellen, Mary, Frances and Thomas. If that Symon is the man who went to Ringstead, Richard is the Tuttles’ ancestor.

John Wells (bur. 1618 Ringstead, Northants)

Ahnentafel #24578 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (14 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

John Wells of Ringstead was a freeholder there in 1605, listed as “John Weales” beside “Symon Tootwell,” and his will of 1618 survives, though it has not yet been read. By the account of a genealogy of 1952, he was the father of Isabel, who married Symon Tootell, and when he made his will in 1618 he remembered her, his son-in-law, and all four of his Tuttell grandsons. If that is right, the will is the earliest paper to name William Tuttle, then about ten years old.
