Archibald Bell (~1770/75 – bef. 1839 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #128 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Archibald Bell — Archy to his neighbours, who spelled it that way in the deeds they swore to — was born about 1770 to 1775, probably a son of William Rasor Bell, a planter of Salem in Sumter District, South Carolina, who died leaving nine sons, two daughters and a widow named Mary. Whether the John Bell he names first among his older children is the John Bell who went out to Alabama about 1830, and who is Alec’s ancestor, has never been settled, so his place on this chart is a likelihood and not a fact.

He farmed on both banks of Lynches Creek, the water that divides Sumter District from Darlington, and his own paperwork crossed the line with him: he is “of the same place” in Darlington in 1807, “of the district of Sumpter” in 1810, and “of the State and District aforesaid” in Darlington again in 1817. He bought land in Darlington in 1806 and sold a hundred and twenty-seven acres of it to John B. Divine on 22 February 1817, between Bay Branch and Newmans Swamp. His wife Martha released her dower on his land twice, in October 1809 and again in February 1817, both times before the same justice of the peace, John Huggins, whose family would later marry into his. He was witnessing and swearing to deeds across the creek in Sumter from 1810, and in 1812 James Hutson senior conveyed land to “Archd Bell of the district of Sumpter.” In 1815 he mortgaged a 345-acre tract on Lynches Creek to Zachariah Cantey — the same ground a surveyor had laid out for William Rasor Bell thirty years before.

Martha died in the 1820s, and he married Margaret, a Dixon widow with grown sons of her own. That is why one of his children is called Ezekiel Dixon Bell, and why John H. Dixon, his stepson, was the executor he trusted with everything. They had five more children, with names that read like a roll-call of his neighbours: Benjamin Sumter, Margaret Louisa, Ezekiel Dixon, Hugh McCallum, Columbus Campbell. He was sued in 1830 for a witness fee. He was still buying land in 1836, at sixty, from Robert Severance, who had been his next-door neighbour since at least 1820.

He made his will on 4 October 1839, weak in body but of sound mind, and it was filed on the 22nd. He left Margaret the 275-acre plantation for her life or her widowhood, and after her to the five children of the second marriage; the residue he divided among six more — “John Bell, Celey Amoson, Harriet Bell, Martha Bell, William Wesley Bell, and Jane Bell.” He left a set of blacksmith’s tools and thirteen geese. And he directed that the rest of his property be sold to pay his debts, “including my negroe boy Limus” — a boy of about fifteen whom he had already pledged to Henry Scarborough nine months earlier. When the appraisers came on 26 October they valued Limus at seven hundred dollars against an estate of eleven hundred and eighteen. Sixty-three per cent of everything Archy Bell owned was one enslaved child, and after the sale the boy passes out of sight.

His widow remarried a Severance, which tripped the widowhood clause in the will and forced the partition of 1846–47 — and the “Margaret Severance” who bought the plantation in May 1847 was the widow buying her own place back.

Martha Bell (fl. 1809–1817 Darlington SC)

Ahnentafel #129 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Martha was the wife of Archibald “Archy” Bell of Lynches Creek, in Darlington and Sumter districts of South Carolina, and she is probably the mother of his older children — the six he named in his will of 1839, among them the John Bell who went to Alabama — though nothing says so, and her whole place on this chart hangs on her husband’s.

She appears twice, briefly, and both times because the law required a man’s wife to be asked. In October 1809, and again in February 1817, Archy sold land in Darlington District, and each time she came before a justice of the peace — the same man both times, John Huggins — who examined her privately and separately from her husband, as the statute said, to be sure a wife was not signing away her dower under her husband’s eye. She declared that she did it freely and voluntarily, without compulsion, dread or fear of any person whomsoever. In 1809 she made her mark.

She was still living in September 1820, when she gave up dower once more on the Sumter side of the creek. By October 1829 Archy’s wife was Margaret, a Dixon widow, so Martha died somewhere in that decade. Her maiden name, her age and her burial place are all unknown, and the two renunciations are the whole of what the world kept of her.

Hobbs Bradley (fl. 1806–1830 Kershaw SC → Ala.)

Ahnentafel #136 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Hobbs Bradley was living in Kershaw District, South Carolina, by 1806, when he bought land on Grannies Quarter Creek. The Kershaw people believed the family had come from the Eastern Shore of Maryland; his grandson’s family remembered him as an Irishman who had come by way of Delaware. In 1825 his uncle Solomon Bradley died in Kershaw claiming no relation in the world but Hobbs, and a neighbour swore to the court that Hobbs had children named John, Abram, Salathiel and Ezekiel, and two daughters. The court heard that Solomon, an old man who had left his brother John behind in Maryland at sixteen, had told Hobbs: “I do not mean to make you my heir but your son John.” Neighbours swore the two men were as alike as any two men they had ever seen; others said there was no kinship at all. When Hobbs went to see his uncle he sent for his own mother to keep house — “an old crooked creature”, called the widow Bradley, who flew into a passion because he did not send a horse for her. By then Hobbs had already left Kershaw “for the state of Alabama”, and in 1825 he and his wife Sarah sold the land they had left behind. He won a quarter of Solomon’s estate and was still collecting on the decree in 1830. The sons scattered west into Alabama, Mississippi and Arkansas; one of them, Robert, settled near Clayton in Barbour County and named his first son Hobbs. In June 1827 he was living in Autauga County, Alabama, near a married daughter, the wife of John Peeples, and he gave their son, his grandson John Peeples Jr., a two-year-old brown bay mare colt, taking care that it could never be seized for his son-in-law’s debts. He signed with his mark. The family later said Hobbs died in Edgefield District, but the records show him alive in Alabama in the late 1820s; where and when he died is not known.

Joseph Eakins (d. 1805 New Hanover Co. NC)

Joseph Eakins came into New Hanover County, North Carolina about 1788 or 1790, a man of some twenty-four years with money in his pocket and no kin of his name anywhere in the county. Where he came from, nobody knows; it is the oldest unanswered question on this side of the family. On 11 June 1790 James Fleming, a Wilmington merchant, conveyed to “Joseph Eakins of the same place, Gentleman,” for fifteen pounds currency, two tracts: a hundred acres beginning at a black oak below the old track path, and three hundred acres on the east side of Moores Creek.

He worked that land for fifteen years. In December 1793 he sold a parcel to Jacob Moore, calling himself “planter” this time rather than gentleman. In 1796 he went to the state land office and took out a patent of his own — grant No. 482, two hundred and ten acres on the east side of the Widow Moore's Branch, beginning at a pine on the east side of the New Road in the meadow, signed at Raleigh by Samuel Ashe, Governor, Captain General and Commander in Chief. By then the surveyors' boundary calls were running past “a pine, Parker's corner, now Eakins.”

He married, almost certainly, Elizabeth Pigford, and they had six children: John, born about 1795, Sarah, Joseph, William, Deborah and Andrew.

He died in the first months of 1805, leaving children too young to act for themselves. At the March term the county court sent three men out to set aside his widow's year's provision, and they wrote down what they left in the yard to keep the household alive for twelve months: the corn in hand with the bacon, four cows and calves, one four-year-old steer, and the mark or right of hogs. On 11 April the rest went under the hammer — cattle, hogs, sheep, plantation tools, household furniture — and made $1,657.57. Edward and Jacob Pigford administered the estate, Timothy Pigford took the children, and Solomon Pigford stood security on its notes. He also left three people he had held in slavery, Simon, Rachel and Cesar, whose hired-out labour went on paying the estate's bills for years afterwards, including the schooling of his own sons.

The family never moved off that ground. His son Joseph bought the adjoining 138 acres in 1834, the deed running from “Joseph Eakins's own corner” along “Eakins's line”; and the Eakins Family Cemetery still stands on the Moores Creek land, in what is now Pender County. That son, born in 1797 and eight years old when his father died, lived to ninety-three; his is the oldest grave in it, and the newest is from 2019.

Moore Stephens (b. ~1796 NC – Sumter Co. AL 1850)

Ahnentafel #160 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Moore Stephens was born about 1796 in North Carolina, one of at least ten children of a Stephens household in Orange County whose father’s name has not survived. On 3 September 1817 he and his brother Charles bound themselves to the Governor in five hundred pounds that there was no lawful cause to obstruct his marriage to Rebecca Moize, with Joseph A. Woods witnessing. Both men signed the bond with a mark: Moore could not write his own name.

Within a few years the whole family picked up and went to Alabama — they were in Greene County by 1824 — and Moore worked his way west again into Sumter County. On 12 October 1846 the Greene County Orphans’ Court divided the estate of Mary Thorn among ten heirs at law in equal shares, and he was one of them, due $107.50 and receipting $95. The nine others were his brothers and sisters: William, Lewis, Charles and James Stephens, Nancy Barnett, Mary Barnett, Lucinda Ball, Elizabeth Beavers and Esther Hitt. He bought nothing at the sale, because by then he no longer lived in the county; the clerk’s cost bill files him under “Heirs of Sumter Co., Ala.” Two-thirds of what he inherited was the price of a human being — the sale raised $1,075.86 and $700 of it was paid for a woman named Jane, so about $70 of Moore’s share was her price.

In 1850 his house at dwelling 1190 in Sumter County held Moore, fifty-four, Rebecca, fifty, and the children Benjamin W. 21, James M. 18, John F. 16, Mary A. R. 13, Charles L. 12, Easter C. 10 and William K. 6 — and two more, Rebecca J. and Richard Hitt, both ten, the orphans of his dead sister Esther, being raised alongside his own. He had named a daughter Easter for that sister. His son Henry farmed six houses up the road. He himself held one enslaved person that year, a Black girl of about seven, born around 1843, whose name was never written down.

He was a man the county put its trust in. In 1853 and 1854 he stood surety three times alongside his neighbour Daniel Jones — twice on the administration of Thomas Fowler’s estate, for $1,200 and $2,800, and once on a $5,000 guardianship of Willis Thomas’s small children.

By 1860 he had moved on again: “Moore Stevens, 66, born North Carolina,” in Newton County, Mississippi, with Rebeca 55 and five children still at home. Henry brought his family to the same county. When Moore died is not known. The Newton County courthouse burned in September 1876, “supposed to have been of incendiary origin,” and the records that would have dated his death burned with it.
