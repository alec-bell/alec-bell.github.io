Simon Tuttle (1647 – 1719 Wallingford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #3072 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Simon Tuttle was baptised at New Haven on 28 March 1647, the ninth child of William Tuttle, the immigrant, and his wife Elizabeth. He was bred a cooper. When Wallingford was laid out in 1670 he put his name on the proprietors’ list and was assigned lot No. 13 with eight acres of out-land, but he did not take the allotment up; he stayed on at New Haven, where he was still drawing his share in the third division in 1680 — the list credits him with “two heads,” which is a clerk’s way of saying a wife and no children yet. He had married Abigail about 1679 or 1680. He was of New Haven through 1683 and “of Wallingford” from 1685, moving, it seems, after his mother died in December 1684.

At Wallingford he did well. He drew lot No. 52 with a hundred and four acres in 1686 and lot No. 14 in 1690, was rated at £79 on the grand list of 1701, and from 1708–9 served the town as gauger and packer, the officer who inspects goods sold by weight and measure. His deeds run across three decades and all of them call his father by name: in 1677–8 he sold a cordwainer land “my father, Mr. William Tuttle, bought of John Punderson”; in 1686 six acres “formerly belonging to my father, Mr. William Tuttle”; in 1705 to his cousin Joshua all his right in the Branford division “set to my father, Mr. William Tuttle.” In 1673 he and five of his brothers had signed a right-of-way agreement whose original paper still survived two centuries later, with his signature on it.

He gave land to his sons in his lifetime, as his son Timothy would in turn: nine and a half acres to Timothy on 10 July 1711, “I bought of Roger Tyler,” and a fourteen-acre house lot to Daniel on 23 June 1715. Simon Tuttle died on 16 April 1719 at Wallingford. His widow Abigail outlived him by three years and died on 11 August 1722.

Abigail Tuttle (m. ~1679 – d. 1722)

Ahnentafel #3073 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Abigail married Simon Tuttle, the New Haven cooper, probably in 1679 or 1680 — the date is read off a land record rather than a marriage entry, because in 1680 the New Haven proprietors credited Simon with “two heads” in the third division, meaning a wife but as yet no children. She moved with him to Wallingford about 1685 and raised his family there; her son Timothy, the deacon, was born about 1682.

She outlived her husband. Simon died on 16 April 1719; Abigail died on 11 August 1722.

Her own family is not known. An old tradition made her a daughter of Richard Beach, and the Beach surname does fit the neighbourhood — the Tuttles’ neighbour in the 1715 school-district line was a Timothy Beach. But the 1883 Tuttle genealogy itself prints the doubt: Joseph P. Beach of Cheshire, the family’s own authority, “thinks that she could not have been a Beach. Richard’s dau. Abigail is not mentioned in settlement of her father’s estate. The point remains in uncertainty.” Fifty years later Jacobus wrote her into Families of Ancient New Haven as “Abigail — [not Beach]”. Her identity as Simon’s wife and Timothy’s mother is not in question; her surname is simply lost.

John Doolittle (1655 New Haven – aft. 1717 Conn.)

Ahnentafel #3074 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Doolittle was born at New Haven on 14 June 1655, a son of Abraham Doolittle — who had come to New Haven from Boston in 1644, took the freeman’s oath there, served as the county’s chief executive officer, and was one of the founders of Wallingford — and his wife. John married Mary Peck on 13 February 1682/3, and settled at Wallingford, the town his father had helped lay out.

Their children included Esther, born 24 January 1683, who also married a Tuttle; Samuel in 1685; Thankful, born 5 April 1688, who married Deacon Timothy Tuttle in 1706; and Benjamin, born 1695, who took his degree at Yale in 1716, married Lydia Todd the next year, and was settled as the minister of Northfield, up on the Massachusetts frontier, from 1718 until he died suddenly in January 1748. It was to that uncle’s house that Thankful’s son Gershom went when he first travelled north as a young man — which is how a Wallingford family came to have a foothold a hundred miles up the Connecticut River.

Mary died on 1 September 1710. John married again on 29 January 1717, to Grace Blakeslee, and that is the last record of him alive; he was then sixty-one. The year of his death is not established. A John Doolittle who died at Wallingford in 1746–7, aged thirty-five, was a son of the same name, not this man, and the rank “Sergeant” that is often attached to him belonged to his father Abraham.

Mary Peck was born at New Haven on 4 March 1665/6, a daughter of John Peck and his wife Mary Moss. The Pecks, the Mosses and the Doolittles were the three families who founded Wallingford between 1668 and 1670 and whose children married across all three, and on 13 February 1682/3 Mary married John Doolittle, Abraham Doolittle’s son.

Her children included Esther, born January 1683; Samuel, 1685; Thankful, born 5 April 1688, who married Deacon Timothy Tuttle; and Benjamin, born 1695, afterwards minister at Northfield. Mary died at Wallingford on 1 September 1710, at forty-four. Her husband remarried seven years later.

One warning belongs with her name. Her son-in-law Timothy Tuttle, left a widower in 1728, married in 1729 a second Mary Peck — a different woman entirely, daughter of Joseph and Sarah (Alling) Peck and widow of Stephen Rowe. This Mary Peck had been dead nineteen years by then. Any account that has Timothy Tuttle marrying his own mother-in-law has run the two together.

Samuel Allis (1647 Braintree – 1691 Hatfield)

Ahnentafel #3076 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Samuel Allis was born at Braintree, Massachusetts, on 24 February 1647. The town register enters him under the year 1646, because the year then began in March and February was reckoned the twelfth month: “Samuel, sonne of William Alyes & Mary his wife was Borne the 24th. of ye 12 month.” In the Old Style of the time the date is written 24 February 1646/7. His father, William Allis, was a Braintree freeman of 1640 who removed about 1661 up the Connecticut River to the new town of Hatfield.

Samuel followed him there and took his own home lot as a young man of twenty-two. On 4 February 1669 the town granted him a house lot sixteen rods wide next to Samuel Marsh’s, with land in the Plain above the great meadow and ten acres in the swamp by the Mill river — “provided he build a sufficient dwelling house upon his lot by or before the last of May in the year 1671, otherwise to forfeit his land.” Daniel Belden had the lot on the other side. He built, and he stayed: a carpenter and builder, living on the east side of Hatfield street.

About 1675–6 he married a woman named Alice, whose surname no record has ever given. Seven children followed: Mehitable on 2 July 1677; Samuel on 20 February 1679; William on 19 October 1680, from whom this family descends; Mary on 6 July 1682; Thomas on 12 March 1684; Sarah about 1685; and Rebecca on 29 November 1687.

Samuel Allis died at Hatfield on 9 March 1691, aged forty-four, leaving Alice with seven children all under fifteen. She remarried in 1696 and was killed in the raid on Deerfield of 29 February 1704; their son Samuel was killed in the same attack, and their daughter Mary was carried to Canada and survived to come home.

Mr. John Catlin (~1643 – killed 1704 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #3082 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Catlin was born about 1643, the son of a Catlin of Wethersfield, Connecticut, who died when the boy was an infant. He was of Wethersfield in 1662 — the year he married Mary Baldwin — and of Branford by 1665, and from there he went with the New Haven colony party that founded Newark, New Jersey. He is the first man on this chart to live in New Jersey, and he was not a farmer there. In 1676 he kept Newark's first school, in his own house, as the custom then was; the townsmen's vote engaged him to “do his faithful, honest and true endeavour to teach the children or servants of these as have subscribed … english and also arithmetick as much as they are capable to learn and he capable to teach them.” In 1678 he was the town's attorney — not a lawyer, but an “honest brother” charged to see that all town orders were executed and to punish any breach — and he was a selectman from 1676 to 1681.

About 1683 he moved north again, by way of Hartford, to the frontier town of Deerfield in Massachusetts, where he was one of the early permanent settlers. In 1685 he bought the Frary part of home lot 32, four acres on Deerfield street, and lot 33 from John Parsons in the same year; lot 36 was his as well by 1704. In the town records he is given the title “Mr.”, which in a New England town book is a rank and not a courtesy, and he took a prominent part in the town's affairs.

On the night of 28 February 1704 there were 291 people under Deerfield's roofs. Before the next morning was out, all but a hundred and twenty-six of them were dead or being marched through the snow towards Canada, three hundred miles away. The historian of Deerfield wrote that no family suffered more than John Catlin's. He and his son Jonathan were killed defending their house, which was then taken and burned with all his buildings; another son was killed later that morning in the Meadow Fight; a third son, John, and three of his daughters were carried off. Two of those daughters, Elizabeth, the wife of James Corse, and Mary, died on the march. The 1704 casualty list enters him simply as “John Catlin, (?) 60”.

The house that gave the raid its surviving relic still stood in Deerfield until 1848 and was photographed before it came down: Capt. John Sheldon's, built about 1698, which held out while half the village burned. Its front door, hacked through, is kept in Memorial Hall in the village to this day.

His line survived for one accidental reason: his daughter Sarah had married Michael Mitchell about 1694 and was in a household of her own that night. The captured son came home from Canada, and by about 1715 had built a new house on his father's ashes; he was a housewright, and was always known in Deerfield by tradition as “Master Catlin.” The forty-eight who died in the raid lie together in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield under a brownstone block raised over the common grave.

Mary Baldwin (bap. 1644 – aft. 1704)

Ahnentafel #3083 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Baldwin was baptized at Milford, Connecticut, on 23 June 1644 — the same day her mother Hannah joined the Milford church and the first four Baldwin children were all baptized together. Her father was Joseph Baldwin, one of the first settlers of the town. On 23 September 1662 she married John Catlin, and in a tangle that is rare even in a small colony she married the son of her own stepmother: her father's second wife, Isabella, was the widow of the elder John Catlin.

She went with her husband from Branford to Newark in New Jersey, where he kept the town's first school, and about 1683 to Deerfield on the Massachusetts frontier. They had ten children. Her father remembered her in his will of 20 December 1680 — “I doe give to my Daughter Mary Cattlin … fortie shillings” — a line in his own instrument, nearly a quarter of a century after she had left his house.

On the morning of 29 February 1704 her husband and one son were killed defending their house at Deerfield and it was burned; another son was killed in the Meadow Fight; a son and three daughters were carried off towards Canada, and two of the daughters died on the march. Mary was taken with the other prisoners to the Sheldon house, the largest in the town, which the attackers had kept as a holding place for captives. A wounded French officer was brought in and laid on the floor, calling for water, and Mary Catlin fed him with water. Someone asked her how she could do that for her enemy. She answered, “If thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him water to drink.” The Frenchman was carried away, the captives were marched off, and Mrs. Catlin was left behind. Afterwards a little boy who had been hidden in the house came out, and she told him to run and hide. The boy said, “Mrs. Catlin, why don't you go and hide?” She said, “I am a captive; it is not my duty to hide, but you have not been taken, and it is your duty to hide.”

That account came down in the family and was written out a century later by a descendant, Lucy D. Shearer; the historian of Deerfield printed it because he judged it an authentic tradition, and he accepted its explanation in five words of his own — this was the place “where Mrs. Catlin relieved the wounded French lieutenant, and secured her own freedom by her practical Christianity.” The house she stood in outlasted her by a century and a half; the “Old Indian House” stood until 1849, and its scarred and battered door is in Memorial Hall at Deerfield still. Mary did not long survive that morning: a descendant's account, citing the Deerfield vital records, gives her death as 4 April 1704, five weeks after the raid, and says she died of grief. She lies in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield.
