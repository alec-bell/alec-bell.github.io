Tabitha Mitchell (b. 1736/7 – m. 1757 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #385 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Tabitha Mitchell was born on 9 February 1736/7, the daughter of Joseph Mitchell and Mary Allis. The family was at Sunderland, in the Connecticut valley of Massachusetts, where her brother Joseph had been born in 1726/7, and afterwards at Deerfield.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield, she married Gershom Tuttle of New Cambridge in Connecticut — her own first cousin. Her mother Mary Allis and his mother Lois Allis were sisters: Lois had married the elder Gershom Tuttle in 1737, and twenty years later Lois’s son married Mary’s daughter. The wedding was at Deerfield because the bride was at home there.

She went with him through Connecticut, through the Revolution, north to New Hampshire and on into New York. Her father’s will, made on 14 April 1792 and proved in November 1794, gives “to my Daughter Tabitha Wife of Gersham Tuttle Twelve Pounds,” beside her sisters Mary Belding and Hannah Bigelow and the children of “my late Son Joseph” — so she was still living in the spring of 1792, in her middle fifties.

Her name very nearly did not survive at all. On the Daughters of the American Revolution’s own file for her husband she is “X Mitchel” — a surname and nothing else.

Pamelia Clark's father (Farmington, Connecticut)

Ahnentafel #386 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Known unknown — the person existed; the name does not survive

Pamelia Clark’s father was a Farmington, Connecticut, man of the Revolutionary generation. His daughter was born there on 4 November 1770, and she married Gershom Tuttle at Farmington on 8 July 1788. His own name has not come down.

He is visible only through his children and grandchildren. One of his sons was an officer of the United States Army and died while in charge of the arsenal at Troy, New York. A grandson was General Newman S. Clarke, born in Connecticut, commissioned an ensign in 1812, brevetted brigadier-general in 1847, and commanding the Department of the Pacific when he died at San Francisco in 1860.

His daughter carried “Strong” as a middle name, and it went down four more generations after her — Clark Strong Tuttle, Dennis Clark, Richard Clark. Where it came from, nobody knows.

George Roush (1761 Frederick VA – 1845 Meigs OH)

Ahnentafel #480 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

George Roush was born in July 1761 in Frederick County, Virginia — the part of it that became Shenandoah County eleven years later — one of the younger sons of the German immigrant John Adam Roush and his wife Susannah. He grew up on Mill Creek in a house where the Bible was printed in German, and he was eighteen when the Revolution reached him.

He enlisted in the autumn of 1779 and served two months under his own brother, Captain John Roush, “both of Shenandoah County Virginia.” He was drafted again in the spring of 1780 under Captain Joseph Pugh, and a third time in 1781 for three months — that tour, his brother Jonas later swore, as a substitute for another brother, Jacob. The family always told that he was at Yorktown and saw Cornwallis hand over his sword; his brother Jonas certainly was. He applied for a pension from Ohio in 1832 and it was allowed as claim S18579, and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as Patriot Ancestor A098975.

About 1781, in Shenandoah County, he married Catharine Zerkel, the eldest daughter of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville. Their children, born over more than twenty years, included Michael, Magdalena, John — who in 1808 married his cousin Regina, Jonas Roush’s daughter — Daniel, Lewis, Catherine, Samuel, Susannah, Jacob, Mary and a second Jonas.

He went down the Ohio ahead of his family about 1798, taking up land in Mason County with his brother Captain John, in whose name the purchase was made; his son Jacob was still born and baptised back in Shenandoah County in 1799. In 1807 he crossed to Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, bought a level tract from Edward W. Tupper west of the hamlet of Dorcas, near Racine, and lived on it for the rest of his life. He was a mechanic by trade — “a very good one, we are told,” the family said — and a farmer, and when Meigs County organised itself in 1819 he sat among its first jurors. He had been a citizen of three counties at the moment each was created.

Catharine died in Virginia, after a journey home she had pined for; her burial place was never written down. On 13 August 1815, at Gallipolis, George married a widow, Catherine “Kitty” Wolfe, and they had one daughter, Hannah.

One Sunday morning a schoolmaster boarding in the Roush house — one of the Henkels, the Lutheran printing family of New Market, the family believed — asked to sketch him. After “considerable reluctance” George gave in, put on the coat of his uniform, and sat. The small sketch stayed in the family at Racine for a century, kept by Elizabeth Wilcoxin and then by James L. Wilcoxin, and was carried out to Meigs County pioneer meetings to be looked at. Of the nine Roush brothers who went to that war, he is the only one whose face is known.

In his last illness he talked about charging the British, and told his comrades to get ready, they were coming over the hill. He died on 31 May 1845 and lies in Weldon Cemetery, on the third terrace above the Ohio at Racine, Meigs County. His small marble stone is still legible: “GEORGE ROUSH … DIED May 31, 1845 AGED 84 years.” On 19 August 1933, before about a thousand descendants, the Sons of the American Revolution dedicated a grave marker there; the flat marker lying at the foot of his headstone today reads “GEORGE ROUSH / CONTINENTAL LINE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / JUL 1761 – MAY 1845,” and it gets his war wrong — all three of his terms were Virginia militia, not the Continental Line. His German Bible, said to have come from his father out of Germany, was still in the family in 1928, in the hands of a great-granddaughter at Racine.

Catharine Zerkel (Shenandoah Co., Virginia)

Ahnentafel #481 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Catharine Zerkel was born on 14 August 1763 in Shenandoah County, Virginia — so the family’s own reckoning has it — the eldest of the fourteen children of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville, with Henry Brock and his wife Magdalene standing as her baptismal sponsors. She was a married woman at seventeen, sponsoring a baptism herself in October 1780, and she married George Roush in Shenandoah County about 1781.

Her first child was born in January 1783, and there were a dozen more over the next twenty years, among them John, born 22 November 1785, who married his cousin Regina Roush. She went west with her husband — to Mason County about 1798, then across the Ohio into Meigs County in 1807.

The one story the family kept about her is a homesick one. In declining health she pined for the Virginia home, and at last George set out with her, more than three hundred miles over the mountains on horseback and on foot. They came up the Shenandoah and stood the horses in the door yard of the old plantation. “The joy was too great, she was overwhelmed and wept as a child.” She did not live long after, and was buried somewhere in Virginia; nobody wrote down where. A later Zirkle family historian guessed the old Nease–Zirkle graveyard on Holman’s Creek, where her father and mother lie, but he was guessing. Her husband married again in August 1815, so she was dead by then; the year itself has never been recovered.

Jonas Roush (1763 Frederick VA – 1850 Mason VA)

Ahnentafel #482 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Jonas Roush was born in September 1763 at Holman’s Fort in Frederick County, Virginia — soon to be Shenandoah County — the youngest of the sons of John Adam and Susannah Roush. He was seventeen when his turn came in the Revolution, and he told the story himself, on oath, before the court at Mason County, Virginia, on 6 November 1832.

He “entered the service as a substitute for his Brother Henry Roush who was drafted for a tour of three months in the summer of 1781,” served under Captain All in Colonel Bird’s regiment at Falmouth near Fredericksburg, and went out again on a second three-month tour. That tour took him to the siege of Yorktown, and he was there on 19 October 1781 when Cornwallis’s army surrendered. He fell ill near Fredericksburg while escorting prisoners. His pension was allowed at twenty dollars a year from 4 March 1831 as claim S4785, and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as Patriot Ancestor A098985.

He married Barbara Fox, of a Shenandoah County family settled near Forestville. Their daughter Regina, born 30 November 1788, married her cousin John Roush in 1808 — the marriage that doubles back through Alec’s tree, so that Jonas and his brother George both stand as his ancestors. Their other children were Abraham, John, Adam and Philip.

Like his brothers he came down the Ohio, and he was living in Mason County, Virginia when he applied for his pension. That same year, 1832, his brother Daniel made him executor of a will leaving the residue of the estate “equally divided among the children of my brothers Jonas, George, Henry, Philip and Jacob.” Barbara died in the mid-1830s, and in 1837 he gave up his own household, moved his pension across to Meigs County, Ohio, and spent his last years in his daughter Regina’s house in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township. Whether he died on the Ohio bank or back on the Virginia one has never been settled; the two places are opposite sides of the same water.

He died on 14 December 1850, aged eighty-seven, and lies in Gilmore Cemetery at Minersville, Meigs County, Ohio, the burying ground of that settlement. John and Regina were laid beside him in graves that were never marked. His own stone is still there, worn but standing, and two later Revolutionary War markers have been set by it: an upright government headstone, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT / VA TROOPS / REV WAR / 1763 1850,” and a flat bronze, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT VA REGIMENT / REVOLUTIONARY WAR.”

Henry Roush Sr. (1752 – 1831 Plants Cemetery)

Ahnentafel #500 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Henry Roush was born in 1752 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, one of the oldest of the children of John Adam and Susannah Roush.

He was twenty-three when the war began, and he was in it from the first season: a private in Captain John Tipton’s company, eighty-eight days’ service, paid off at Romney on a receipt dated 25 October 1775 for £6 12s. In the summer of 1781 he was drafted again and his younger brother Jonas went in his place — Jonas swore to it half a century later, applying for his own pension. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry Henry as Patriot Ancestor A098977.

He married Dorothea about 1777 and they raised ten children in Shenandoah County near Solomon’s Church — Michael, Christina, Henry Jr., Elizabeth, Susannah, Anthony, Dorothy, Eva and Balser among them. Anthony, born 23 January 1792, is the son through whom this family descends. Henry gave the two and a half acres the church and its graveyard stand on, and in 1793 he put his hand to the Solomonburg deed, signing “HENRICH ROUSH” in German on the same page where his wife made her mark. It is why the descendants who later marked his grave called him the Church Builder.

He followed the Roush migration down the Ohio — to Mason County in 1798, and on to Letart Falls in Meigs County, Ohio, about 1800 — and he settled his children on land as he went: a deed to Henry Jr. in October 1805, seventy-five acres to Anthony for three hundred dollars in July 1812, and in June 1817 land to his son Balser and to his daughters Susanna Linscott and Catharine “for natural love and affection.”

He died on 26 October 1831, aged seventy-nine, and lies in Plants Cemetery above the river at Plants, Meigs County. The little stone set over him then — “Henry Roush died October 26, 1831, aged 79 years” — has been joined by a granite block carrying a plaque from his descendants: “HENRY ROUSH / 1752-1831 / ‘THE CHURCH BUILDER’ / ONE OF NINE BROTHERS / WHO FOUGHT IN THE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / ERECTED BY HIS DESCENDANTS.” A bronze tablet at its foot reads “HENRY ROUSH / PVT CAPT TIPTON’S CO / VA STATE TRP / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / 1752 † 1831,” naming the very company he had served in fifty-six years before he died. The markers were dedicated by the Sons of the American Revolution at the Roush family’s annual meeting on 19 August 1933, the same afternoon as his brother George’s, a few miles down the Ohio.
