Pamelia Strong Clark (1770 Farmington – 1851)

Ahnentafel #193 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Pamelia Strong Clark was born at Farmington, Connecticut, on 4 November 1770. Clark was her surname; Strong was a middle name, and it descended four more generations — Clark Strong Tuttle, Dennis Clark, Richard Clark — long after anyone remembered where it had come from. Her brother was an officer of the United States Army and died while in charge of the federal arsenal at Troy, New York. On 8 July 1788, aged seventeen, she married Gershom Tuttle of Bristol.

She spent the next twenty-eight years moving west with him: Wilmington in Vermont, Paris in Oneida County, and about 1800 the new Black River country of Jefferson County, New York, where her husband farmed 275 acres, built a mill and kept the tavern whose ball-room served the neighbourhood for a church. Her son Chester was born at Sackets Harbor in 1796, Cynthia at Watertown in 1803, Amanda in 1810, Clark Strong in April 1811, Asa in 1813. In 1816 the family went on to Vigo County, Indiana.

Her husband died on 5 September 1823, leaving a will that gave her everything for life, with power to provide “for any of our children for whom she may think necessary to provide.” Three weeks later she gave bond as executrix, with Abraham and Henry Markle as her securities, and the inventory of the whole estate came to a little over six hundred dollars. It was insolvent, and her co-executor Nathaniel Huntington was out of the sheriff’s reach by 1829 and dead by 1831.

What followed is the hardest stretch of any life on this chart. The probate court cited her in January 1829, again in November 1829, and summoned her once more on 2 November 1830 to show cause “why she has not made a final settlement of the estate of Gursham Tuttle deceased, of which she is the executrix.” In March 1831 she petitioned, through a solicitor, to be let out of it, in words that are still painful to read: the estate’s business was “long since closed,” but “the papers of said Estate were left in the possession of Nathaniel Huntington Esq. who is dead, and they are now in so confused and mutilated a situation that no accurate statement of the condition of the Estate could be made from them.” On 29 December 1831 the court issued a writ of attachment for her body for contempt. The sheriff executed it on 2 February 1832 and charged eighty-three and a half cents in fees. Bail was set at ten dollars.

She outlived her husband by twenty-eight years, and in old age she followed one of her children ninety miles south into the hills of Brown County. She died on 22 April 1851, in her eighty-first year, and her stone at Southview Cemetery in Nashville reads: In Memory of PAMELIA TUTTLE DIED April 22 1851 Aged 80 Y’s, 5 M’s, 18 D’s — an age that counts back to the day she was born, 4 November 1770.

Joseph Lamb (~1782 NC – 1855 Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #216 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lamb was born in North Carolina about 1782. He married Lydia Adamson there in November 1806, her brother Samuel standing bondsman — the clerk wrote her name “Lidya Addison” — and they were members of the Society of Friends. Nine children were born to them, of whom seven grew up: Ezekiel, Esther, Smith, Elias, Mournen, Joseph and Ithamar. The family was living in Randolph County, about sixteen miles north of Asheboro, in 1814, when his son Elias was born there.

In April 1829 he took the family out of North Carolina for Indiana — the ordinary Quaker departure from the slaveholding South — and settled in Clay Township, Wayne County, on land adjoining Perry Township that a widow, Eve Shoemaker, had first taken up in 1821. He farmed there for the rest of his life, and his sons and his wife’s brother settled around him: when the Clay Township school section was sold in 1832 the buyers included Samuel Adamson, and his son Ithamar was still living on that land forty years later.

Lydia died first — in 1844, his son Elias remembered, though the year is also given as 1837. Joseph died on 29 July 1855, aged seventy-three, and lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery in Wayne County under a small marble stone, weathered but still readable: JOSEPH LAMB DIED 29th Day of the July of year 1855.

John Bish lived in Fairfield County, Ohio, about eighteen miles east of Columbus, where his daughter Susannah was born in November 1818. He may be the John Bish who married an Anne Campbell in Rockingham County, Virginia, in November 1810. He and his wife Ann Elizabeth raised ten children to maturity.

In 1836 he brought the family to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township. The following March his daughter Susannah was married to Elias Lamb at his own house — fifty years later the account of the couple’s golden wedding still gave the place as “the residence of John Bish, the bride’s father, in Clay township.” Besides Susannah his children included William, Henry, George W., John junior, Elizabeth Anne and Mary Ann, the last two marrying into the Petty and Sykes families.

The Bishes lived in Wayne County many years. Later they removed to Miami County, Indiana, where his wife died; afterwards he came back to Wayne County and died on the old homestead, on 20 August 1866. He lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery, Row 9 of the north section — the same burying ground as Joseph Lamb — under a tall marble marker now so badly spalled that the name is only a shadow, though “AUG. 20, 1866” and “AGED” can still be made out.

John Roush (1785 – 1861 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #240 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Roush was born on 22 November 1785 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, a son of George Roush and Catharine Zerkel, and was baptised into the Lutheran faith on 5 January 1786, with another John Roush and his wife Susannah standing as witnesses.

On 18 May 1808 Francis Watkins, a justice of the peace, married him to his own cousin Regina Roush, the daughter of Jonas Roush — a Roush marrying a Roush, both of them grandchildren of the same Shenandoah Valley family, and the name still written Rausch in that neighbourhood. Eleven children were born to them, and all of them, by the family’s own account, were baptised in the Lutheran church at New Haven.

Benjamin, the fifth, was born at Graham Station on the Virginia side in August 1816; Hannah was born in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, in May 1820. Between those two births John and Regina crossed the river for good, into the few square miles where the Roush and Nease families who later intermarried were founding neighbours — the same ground where their great-great-grandson John G. Roush would be born more than fifty years on. He was known in the family as “Blackbird John.”

He could not write.
