Mary Thorn / Thorne (d. 1844 Greene Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #321 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Mary Thorn died in Greene County, Alabama, shortly before 23 January 1844. She was the widow of James Thorn, who had made his will on 12 June 1837 and whose estate was proved there in October 1839; his will names his own children but no wife at all, which is one reason she is probably his second wife and not the mother of his family.

Her own next of kin were Stephenses. The court that opened her estate on 23 January 1844 cited Moore, James, Lewis and William Stephens, Esther Hitt, Lucinda Ball and Elizabeth Bevers as her nearest relations; James Stephens was appointed administrator; and when the estate was finally divided on 12 October 1846 it went in ten equal shares to that same set of brothers and married sisters, with the share of Esther Hitt — by then dead — going to her “Representatives,” which is how a court treats a predeceased child. The most economical explanation is that Stephens was her first married name and Thorn her second, making those ten her children. No document has ever used the word, so this is a strong reconstruction and not a settled fact; she may equally have been their sister or aunt.

Her inventory, taken on 27 April 1844, catalogues her entire world down to an eighteen-cent umbrella and a woman’s saddle — and then one line worth more than all the rest together: “1 Negro woman Jane — 475.00.” Jane had been hired out for seventeen months and was sold at the estate sale on 29 April 1844 to the administrator, James Stephens, for $700. The whole sale raised $1,075.86, so Jane was sixty-five per cent of everything Mary Thorn owned, and roughly $70 of Moore Stephens’s $107 share was her price. The clerk summarised the packet on its outside wrapper in two lines, and the first line is her name.

Mary had also been lending money to the men around her: her papers include promissory notes on Charles Stephens for $15.13 and $32.40 and on Lewis Stephens for $100. Her land was sold at the ordinary sale to Andrew Norwood for $127.09.

Two men have been proposed as the father of the Reverend William Hampton Truett, and the tree offers no way to choose between them: Elisha Truitt, given as 1786–1864, and Noel Truett, given as 1769–1850. Nothing documented ties either of them to William Hampton.

The case for Elisha is circumstantial but it keeps accumulating. John Wesley Truett named a son Elisha Dozier Truett in 1877, and families of that time named children for grandfathers with great regularity. Truitt, 1814–1864, is buried in Oak Grove Cemetery at Chilton in Clarke County — the same small graveyard as John Wesley Truett — and the compiled trees make him William Hampton's brother, with a third brother Curtis Forby. An Elijah Truett served as a private in a Clarke County company in 1861. Against all that, “Noel Truett” has almost no independent footprint at all; the one place the name does turn up in this family is as the supposed father of Suzanna Truitt, the woman who married Daniel Wiggins in 1800 — a different person in a different generation, which may be how the name got attached here in the first place.

If the reconstruction holds, this is the man who brought the family from Darlington District, South Carolina, into Alabama. It is not settled, and it will not be settled by trees. It needs a South Carolina or early Alabama land or probate record — a will or a distribution naming a son William Hampton.

Gershom Tuttle II (1738 Farmington Conn. – 1818)

Ahnentafel #384 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Revolutionary patriot

Gershom Tuttle was born on 22 August 1738 at Farmington, Connecticut — in the parish of New Cambridge, which later became the town of Bristol. His father was Ensign Gershom Tuttle and his mother was Lois Allis.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield in Massachusetts, he married Tabitha Mitchell. The Deerfield clerk styled him “of New Cambridge” and gave the bride no parish, which is what a clerk does for a local bride — and she was local in more than one sense: her mother Mary Allis and his mother Lois Allis were sisters. He married his own first cousin.

His father died in 1777, leaving a will made that May which gave the widow Lois a third for life and divided the rest among “my Three Sons Gershom, Oliver and Samuel Tuttle,” and appointed “my Son Gershom Tuttle whole and sole Executor.” The executor's account of “Gershom Tuttel Executor” was allowed in January 1779.

He is a patriot of the Revolution, and the service is logistics rather than battle — which in Connecticut in 1780 mattered as much. He was a conductor of teams, which meant he ran wagon trains of provisions out of Connecticut into New York State: some three thousand barrels, about five hundred tons, averaging a hundred and fifteen miles a haul. On 23 May 1780 the Connecticut General Assembly paid him £285.13.8 in wages and £60.13.11 for forage in advance, and still owed him £200.2.8. He is listed first of the nine conductors. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him as ancestor A131791, rank teamster, “Conductor of teams transporting supplies for the army.”

After the war he moved north. At Claremont, New Hampshire — where his father had died — he signed a town recommendation of Ambrose Cossitt as justice of the peace on 17 December 1789, with his brother Oliver and his son Solomon, and in 1790 he headed the only Gershom Tuttle household in the census of the whole country. About 1790 he went on to Clinton in Oneida County, New York, and afterwards joined the settlement at Watertown in Jefferson County. He was alive to see his son become a colonel of militia there.

He died on 5 January 1818, in his eightieth year. Where, and where he lies, is not documented by any record; the family genealogy gives no place and the DAR's “Watertown” carries no source. No Gershom Tuttle appears in Jefferson County in the 1820 census, four county histories record no death, and neither Rutland's nor Watertown's cemetery transcriptions hold him.

The Tuttle family genealogy of 1883 makes him the first deacon of Watertown’s first church, organised in 1803. The church’s own historian, who read its minutes, dates the Tuttle deaconship to 1808 and gives it to his son the colonel, so the honour may belong to the son.

Tabitha Mitchell (b. 1736/7 – m. 1757 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #385 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Tabitha Mitchell was born on 9 February 1736/7, the daughter of Joseph Mitchell and Mary Allis. The family was at Sunderland, in the Connecticut valley of Massachusetts, where her brother Joseph had been born in 1726/7, and afterwards at Deerfield.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield, she married Gershom Tuttle of New Cambridge in Connecticut — and the match was not the mystery it looked like for years. Her mother Mary Allis and the groom's mother Lois Allis were sisters: Lois had married the elder Gershom Tuttle in 1737, and twenty years later Lois's son married Mary's daughter. Tabitha and her husband were first cousins, and the Deerfield venue needs no explaining — the bride was at home.

She went with him through Connecticut, the Revolution, New Hampshire and on into New York. George Roush (1761 Frederick VA – 1845 Meigs OH)

Ahnentafel #480 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

George Roush was born in July 1761 in Frederick County, Virginia — the part of it that became Shenandoah County eleven years later — one of the younger sons of the German immigrant John Adam Roush and his wife Susannah. He grew up on Mill Creek in a house where the Bible was printed in German, and he was eighteen when the Revolution reached him.

He enlisted in the autumn of 1779 and served two months under his own brother, Captain John Roush, “both of Shenandoah County Virginia.” He was drafted again in the spring of 1780 under Captain Joseph Pugh, and a third time in 1781 for three months — that tour, his brother Jonas later swore, as a substitute for another brother, Jacob. Family tradition, collected while his grandchildren were still alive, held that he was at Yorktown and saw Cornwallis hand over his sword; his brother Jonas certainly was. He applied for his pension in 1832 from Ohio and it was allowed as claim S18579. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him as Patriot Ancestor A098975.

About 1781, in Shenandoah County, he married Catharine Zerkel, the eldest daughter of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville. Their children, born over more than twenty years, included Michael, Magdalena, John — who in 1808 married his cousin Regina, Jonas Roush’s daughter — Daniel, Lewis, Catherine, Samuel, Susannah, Jacob, Mary and a second Jonas.

He went down the Ohio ahead of his family about 1798, taking up land in Mason County with his brother Captain John, in whose name the purchase was made; his son Jacob was still born and baptised back in Shenandoah County in 1799. In 1807 he crossed to Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, buying a level tract from Edward W. Tupper west of the hamlet of Dorcas, near Racine, and he lived on it for the rest of his life. He was a mechanic by trade — a good one, the family said — and a farmer, and when Meigs County organised itself in 1819 his name is among its first jurors. He had been a citizen of three counties at the moment each was created.

Catharine died in Virginia, after a journey home she had pined for; her burial place was never found. On 13 August 1815 George married a widow, Catherine “Kitty” Wolfe, at Gallipolis, and they had one daughter, Hannah. In his last illness, the family remembered, he talked about charging the British and told his comrades to get ready, they were coming over the hill.

Gwathmey's Historical Register of Virginians in the Revolution lists him at page 679 as 'Rousk, George, 73, Meigs Co., O., mpl.' — the age and the county put the entry on our man, applying from Ohio in his seventies.

The portrait of him printed in the family's 1928 history is not the original likeness. The book explains that a schoolmaster boarding in the Roush house — the compiler believed he was one of the Henkels, the Lutheran printing family of New Market — asked one Sunday morning to sketch him, told him to put on the coat of his uniform, and after "considerable reluctance" was allowed to make a small sketch from life. That sketch was still in the family at Racine, Ohio in 1928 and had been shown at Meigs County pioneer meetings; the plate in the book was drawn again from it, "with great difficulty." The 1928 compilers only recovered the sketch through his descendants at Racine: it had been kept by Elizabeth Wilcoxin, then by James L. Wilcoxin, and it was Mrs. Anna Eliza (Salser) Hoops who helped get it back in time for the book.

He died on 31 May 1845 and lies in Weldon Cemetery on the third terrace above the Ohio at Racine, Meigs County. His small marble stone is still legible: “GEORGE ROUSH … DIED May 31, 1845 AGED 84 years.” On 19 August 1933, before about a thousand descendants, the Sons of the American Revolution dedicated a grave marker there; a flat marker lettered “GEORGE ROUSH / CONTINENTAL LINE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / JUL 1761 – MAY 1845” lies at the foot of the headstone today. His German Bible, said to have come from his father out of Germany, was still in the family in 1928, in the hands of a great-granddaughter at Racine; and of the nine brothers who went to that war he is the only one whose face is known.
