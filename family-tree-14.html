Lydia (Adamson) Lamb (d. 1844 Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #217 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Lydia Adamson married Joseph Lamb in North Carolina in November 1806; the marriage bond was made out for “Lidya Addison,” and her brother Samuel stood bondsman. He later married Joseph’s sister Naomi, so two Adamsons married two Lambs. They were Quakers. Nine children were born to them, of whom seven lived to grow up, among them Elias, through whom this line descends.

In April 1829 she came north with her husband and children to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township; her brother Samuel was one of the buyers when the township school section was sold in 1832. She did not live to be old in Indiana. Her son Elias gave her death as 1844; it is elsewhere put at 11 November 1837, at Economy in the same county.

Almost nothing of her own survives. The county’s history of its first settlers names her husband and all seven of their grown children without once naming her, and she is the only one of the four Lambs and Bishes of her generation on this chart with no stone — none at Sugar Grove beside Joseph, and none anywhere else that can be found.

John Bish (Fairfield OH – 1866 Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #218 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Bish lived in Fairfield County, Ohio, about eighteen miles east of Columbus, where his daughter Susannah was born in November 1818. He may be the John Bish who married an Anne Campbell in Rockingham County, Virginia, in November 1810. He and his wife Ann Elizabeth raised ten children to maturity.

In 1836 he brought the family to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township. The following March his daughter Susannah was married to Elias Lamb at his own house — fifty years later the account of the couple’s golden wedding still gave the place as “the residence of John Bish, the bride’s father, in Clay township.” Besides Susannah his children included William, Henry, George W., John junior, Elizabeth Anne and Mary Ann, the last two marrying into the Petty and Sykes families.

The Bishes lived in Wayne County many years. Later they removed to Miami County, Indiana, where his wife died; afterwards he came back to Wayne County and died on the old homestead, on 20 August 1866. He lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery, Row 9 of the north section — the same burying ground as Joseph Lamb — under a tall marble marker now so badly spalled that the name is only a shadow, though “AUG. 20, 1866” and “AGED” can still be made out.

John Roush (1785 – 1861 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #240 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Roush was born on 22 November 1785 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, a son of George Roush and Catharine Zerkel, and was baptised into the Lutheran faith on 5 January 1786, with another John Roush and his wife Susannah standing as witnesses.

On 18 May 1808 Francis Watkins, a justice of the peace, married him to his own cousin Regina Roush, the daughter of Jonas Roush — a Roush marrying a Roush, both of them grandchildren of the same Shenandoah Valley family, and the name still written Rausch in that neighbourhood. Eleven children were born to them, and all of them, by the family’s own account, were baptised in the Lutheran church at New Haven.

Benjamin, the fifth, was born at Graham Station on the Virginia side in August 1816; Hannah was born in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, in May 1820. Between those two births John and Regina crossed the river for good, into the few square miles where the Roush and Nease families who later intermarried were founding neighbours — the same ground where their great-great-grandson John G. Roush would be born more than fifty years on. He was known in the family as “Blackbird John.”

He could not write. Every deed he made he signed “John his + mark Roush.” In the twenty months between July 1848 and March 1850 he settled land on four Roush men — Abel, James, John Junior and Benjamin. On 9 August 1849 he cut the east strip of Section 25, Town 3, Range 12 in two on a single day, before one justice and the same two witnesses: seventy acres running north from the south-east corner to John Roush Junior, and sixty-nine acres and twenty poles running south from the north-east corner to Benjamin, each for five hundred dollars. He was a widower by then; no wife joined in any of those deeds to release her dower.

He was sixty-six in 1850, living four doors up the road from Benjamin in Sutton Township. He died on 21 January 1861. Regina’s father Jonas had spent his last years in her house and lies in the same ground. For most of a century John’s and Regina’s graves went unmarked, though the neighbours could still point out the two of them, just north of Jonas’s. Today a granite stone at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville carries the name ROUSH, and underneath, JOHN 1785 – 1861 and REGINA 1788 – 18. Whoever ordered it still could not supply her death year, and the cutter left the last two figures off.

Regina Roush (b. 1788)

Ahnentafel #241 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Regina Roush was born on 30 November 1788, the second child of Jonas Roush — a private of the Virginia line who was at the siege of Yorktown and saw Cornwallis surrender — and his wife Barbara Fox.

On 18 May 1808, at nineteen, she married her cousin John Roush before Francis Watkins, a justice of the peace. A Roush had married a Roush: both of them grandchildren of the same Shenandoah Valley family. Eleven children were born to them. Benjamin, the fifth, arrived at Graham Station on the Virginia bank in August 1816; Hannah, in May 1820, was born after the move across the Ohio into the Nease Settlement in Sutton Township, Meigs County.

Her father came to live with her. In June 1837 Jonas, by then an old pensioner of the Revolution, removed to Meigs County to be with a daughter, and he spent his last years in her house in the Nease Settlement, near where he now lies buried. He died there on 14 December 1850.

When she died is not known, and it was already beyond recovery within a lifetime of it. She had very probably gone before the summer of 1848: her husband was a widower by the time he began deeding his land away to his sons and kinsmen, with no wife to join in and release her dower.

She lies at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville, beside her husband and near her father. The granite over the two of them reads JOHN 1785 – 1861 and REGINA 1788 – 18, and there the inscription stops.

David Altman (~1788 Pennsylvania – 1850/70)

Ahnentafel #242 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

David Altman was born about 1788 in Pennsylvania and farmed in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio — the Roushes’ own township, and the Duckworths’ county. He was on that ground by 1840, when the name was as likely to be written Attman as Altman.

In 1850 he was sixty-two, with a thousand dollars of land, keeping house with Margaret, fifty-six, also Pennsylvania-born, and four Ohio-born children at home: Jacob, twenty-three, and Solomon, nineteen, both farming with him, Mary, fourteen, and Samuel, twelve. A John Altman of about thirty-one, born in Ohio, kept a second Altman household in the same township, with a wife and four sons.

He was very probably the father of Sophia Altman, who was born in Ohio on 1 June 1823 and married Benjamin Roush in Meigs County in 1839 — she falls exactly into the gap between John, born about 1819, and Jacob, born about 1827, and she had married out of the house eleven years before his family was last counted — though nothing yet says so outright.

He was dead, or gone from Meigs County, by 1870.

William Rasor Bell (fl. 1784 – 1803/09 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #256 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

William Rasor Bell appears out of nowhere in 1784, already styled planter, in Claremont County of old Camden District, South Carolina. In June of that year he helped appraise Isaac Higgs’s estate in Kershaw District; in April 1787 he bought Micajah Stokes’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle, for £105 15s — and the very next day began giving it all back to Stokes’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in the language of that century usually means family. Ann was conveyed twice in two days between two households that were probably kin; she is the only person he is documented ever to have held, and he held her for a day. His own 1790 household was ten people and no one in slavery.

He had two surveys in 1785 — a hundred and fifty acres on Bare Branch in April, four hundred on Lynches Creek in June — and the grants on both issued to Samuel Boykin, who had paid the treasury fee, as was the local habit. His land lay on both watersheds at once: a later grant describes eighty acres “on Charleston Road between Black River and Lynches Creek, bounded N.W. by William Rasor Bell and Fred:k Bells Land.” A 1787 grant to Frederick Bell recites its north-east line as “Land Surveyed for Wm Rasor Bell, Frederick Bell, Jonathan Newman and Lewis Mathews” — one line of one document carrying three surnames that stay beside this family for three generations.

There is a single sheet of paper that might make him a Patriot. Book G, stub number 30, in South Carolina’s Revolutionary indents: “Issued the 22d. April 1784 To Mr. William Bell for Eleven pounds Eight Shillings and Seven pence Sterling for two Horses impressed for Militia Use in October 1780.” It is the only William Bell indent in the state that no man has ever been attached to, and no lineage society claims it. Nothing yet ties its claimant to him rather than to one of at least six other South Carolina William Bells, and the original stub, read at Columbia in 2026, carries no district, no auditor and no assignee to help.

On the day after Christmas 1803, “very sick & weak in body,” he made his will. It divides a plain farmer’s world among a wife and eleven children: the plantation and stock to Mary for her life, to be sold at her death or remarriage and divided among nine sons — “John Bell, Archibald Bell, Thomas Bell, William Bell, James Bell, Drury Bell, Jesse Bell, Benjamin Bell, & Robert Bell” — and feather beds to two daughters, Mary and Elizabeth, Elizabeth also getting “One Cow & Calf which is now call’d hers,” the only line in the document about a child’s own particular possession. The witnesses were Lewis Bell, Mary Stuckey and a Douglas.

He was dead by 1809. His executor Moses Gordon never served, and administration with the will annexed went on 15 April 1809 to Charles Williams of Georgetown District. Jonathan Newman — the man Archy Bell had witnessed for in 1805 — appraised the estate. The sale of the land that would have named all nine sons in one instrument has never been found, and the fiduciary volumes that would carry it are unfilmed paper in Columbia.

His own parents are unidentified. The leading frame is the Raccoon Swamp Bell clan of Albemarle Parish in Surry and Sussex counties, Virginia, where the patriarch’s 1746 will names five sons and no William; if he belongs there at all it is as an unnamed grandson. And the name Rasor — almost certainly his mother’s or grandmother’s family — has never been found anywhere.
