Thomas Jefferson Bell (1875/78 – 1921 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #16 · his father’s father’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Thomas Jefferson Bell — “Tom J.” to the stonecutter, “Tom Bell” to the probate clerk — farmed near Louisville in Barbour County, Alabama. He was one of the eleven children of John T. Bell and Sallie Cox, and he married Alice May Bradley on 13 August 1895, when she was eighteen. They had nine children. Seven were still living when the 1920 census found the whole household together at Louisville — Thomas J. 44, Alice 42, Henry 18, Ray 16, William 12, Ethel 10 and Juanita 2 — and two were already buried in the churchyard at Bethlehem Baptist, the congregation his people had belonged to since it was organised in 1824.

He died on 30 August 1921, at about forty-three, and was laid beside those two children. The Clayton Record called him a son of Mr. and Mrs. John Bell Sr., pioneer citizens, and said he was buried at Bethlehem “where two of his children are buried.” The granite his family set over him afterwards gives different dates — SEPT. 18, 1874 and AUG. 27, 1920, each a year adrift of the newspaper — and the two have never been reconciled.

He left no estate. The county’s probate minutes for the year after his death record no administration in his name, and the clerk’s index to seventy years of administrators’ records contains no Thomas J. Bell at all. There was simply nothing to divide — and his own father had died six years earlier worth fifty thousand dollars in lands, cotton, insurance and money.

So when his children finally received something, it came through that estate instead. In April 1925 a Barbour County court divided one deceased heir’s share seven ways among Tom’s children, naming each of them — Ellie, Ray, Ruth, William, Ethel, little Juanita, and “Henry Bell, Birmingham.” That decree is the court’s own answer to the question of who his children were, and it is the document that finally settled this family’s longest-standing puzzle.

The church he and Alice lie beside, Bethlehem Baptist on the Clayton–Louisville road, had been gathered by thirteen people in April 1824, six years before the Bells came into the county.

The index to his Alabama death record gives his age at death as 45 years, 11 months and 18 days. Set against the Clayton Record's date of 30 August 1921, that puts his birth at about 12 September 1875 -- which agrees with the 1920 census's 'Thomas J, 44' and with the month, though not the day or the year, cut on his headstone.

Alice outlived him by thirty-five years, married again, and was brought home to lie beside him. Their names are cut side by side on one block of grey granite at Bethlehem, under the single word BELL.

Alice May Bradley (1876 – 1956 Montgomery Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #17 · his father’s father’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice May Bradley was born on 8 October 1876 in Barbour County, Alabama, the daughter of Henry Bradley and Elvira Loveless. She lost both parents young. Her father and mother lie together at Clayton City Cemetery, under two draped marble stones inside one iron rail, and the cemetery record reads their worn figures as 27 February 1887 for Henry Bradley, when Alice was ten, and 14 June 1891 for Elvira. That October a county court appointed a guardian for Alice, then fourteen, and her younger sister Katie, twelve, recording that the minors had no father living. The petitioner was a great-aunt, L. M. Bell, and among the seven signers of the four-thousand-dollar bond were two Bradley relations and John T. Bell — the man who would be her father-in-law four years later. Two years after that, a Eufaula paper printed the probate court’s own settlement of an earlier guardianship and listed her by name among “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased.”

She married Thomas Jefferson Bell on 13 August 1895, at eighteen, and had nine children. Her sister Katie married Tom’s younger brother George, so the two orphaned Bradley girls went into the same farming family at Louisville. Widowed in 1921, Alice married Mack K. Norton — and there is a quiet symmetry in it, because Mack’s first wife had been Katie, who died in 1924. Two Bradley sisters each married a Bell, each was widowed, and the same man married them in turn.

By 1935 she was living with Mack at 118 East North Street in Dothan, and it was that address her eldest son wrote on his draft card in 1942, when the form asked who would always know where he was. The 1940 census records her, on the supplementary line, as married more than once, eighteen at her first marriage, nine children born. Mack died in 1954; his obituary called her children his step-sons and step-daughters and named them as Bells, which is how this family was finally proved from the outside.

Her sister Katie married George Bell, a brother of Alice's own husband: two Bradley sisters married two sons of John T. Bell.

She outlived her first husband by thirty-five years and died at Montgomery on 29 December 1956, aged eighty. They brought her home to Bethlehem, in Barbour County, and buried her beside Tom under the name Bell — not Norton. Her dates are cut on the right-hand half of his stone: ALICE, OCT. 8, 1876, DEC. 29, 1956.

Andrew Jackson Eaken (1881 Lauderdale Co. MS – 1922)

Ahnentafel #18 · his father’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Andrew Jackson Eaken was born on 1 May 1881 in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, one of the youngest children of Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins and Sarah Hudson Payne. His people had come to Mississippi out of New Hanover County, North Carolina, and he reckoned both his parents North Carolina born.

About 1907 he married Maggie Dillon Swain, of York and Cuba in Sumter County, Alabama — the next county across the state line. In 1910 the two of them were living at Kosters, in Mobile, with their one-year-old daughter Beatrice and Maggie’s young brother Langford Swain lodging in the house. In 1917 he registered for the draft at Princeton, in Jackson County at the far north-east corner of Alabama. He was a man who crossed the state repeatedly, and the clerks spelled him as they heard him: by 1920 the family name was being written “Eakans.”

He was living at 2716 Avenue F South in Birmingham when he died on the night of 20 March 1922, aged forty. The body was sent down to Cuba the next morning, and the funeral and burial took place there, at Clay Memorial Cemetery in his wife’s home village, among her people. He left Maggie a widow at thirty-five. Their children included Alec’s great-grandmother Margaret Beatrice; Andrew Jackson “Jack” Eaken, who became a colonel; Joseph William “Jo Will” Eaken, who rose to be a vice-president of American Airlines; and Melvin Swain Eaken.

The surname moved under him. “Our name was originally spelled Eakins,” his granddaughter wrote in 2009. “My father’s grandfather stopped dotting the ‘i’ and it became an ‘e’. My father dropped the ‘s’, as did his brothers.” Andrew Jackson was the Eaken in the middle of that sentence.

Maggie Dillon Swain (1886 – 1985 Jefferson Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #19 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Maggie Dillon Swain was born on 27 September 1886 in Sumter County, Alabama, a daughter of Joseph L. Swain and the woman everyone called Dillon Simmons. Her middle name was her mother’s given name, and it puzzled the family for three generations. She grew up at York, where in 1900 the house held Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford.

She married Andrew Jackson Eaken about 1907. Their daughter Margaret Beatrice was born in 1908, and in 1910 the three of them were at Mobile with her brother Langford boarding in the house. Andrew died on 20 March 1922, and she took him home to Cuba to bury him, twenty miles from where she had been born. She was thirty-five.

On 6 November 1925 she married Thomas J. Bloebaum at Birmingham, and the marriage took her north — to Milton, in DuPage County, Illinois, by 1930, and to Wheaton in the same county in 1940 and 1950. That is the “Chicago area” her descendants remembered without being able to name it. The Bell family came north to join her, and in January 1944 her grandson Thomas Jackson Bell was killed in a car crash at sixteen; she had been going to give him a car for his birthday, and he took the keys a few days early.

Illinois held her for thirty-five years, but she kept coming home. In May 1951 she came down from Wheaton to visit her mother, Mrs. Dillon Swain, and her sisters, Mrs. Geo. McElroy and Mrs. M. C. Cobb. Her mother died in November 1952, and on 6 February 1953 Maggie and her two sisters were granted letters testamentary on the will in the Sumter County probate court, signing the notice to creditors together — Maggie S. Bloebaum, Pinkie S. McElroy, Merle S. Cobb. Her second husband, then eighty, died at Wheaton on 18 July 1962 and was brought down to Clay Memorial at Cuba for burial, so she buried both her husbands in her own village, forty years apart.

She came back to Birmingham in the end, to the house her family still pictures, with a set of rosewood twin beds with beautiful inlay that later passed out of the family. She died there on Sunday 10 March 1985, aged ninety-eight, after fifteen years back in Alabama, outliving her own daughter by two years. The notices called her Margaret Swain Eaker Bloebaum and listed her sons, Col. Andrew J. and Joseph W. Eaker, and her two surviving sisters, Mrs. G. R. McElroy of Chattanooga — the Pinkie of the old York household — and Mrs. Cliffton Cobb of Meridian, who was Merle. She was buried at Clay Memorial Cemetery in Cuba, where her first husband had lain since 1922 and her second since 1962. In family memory she is “Ms Blevon”: Bloebaum, softened by sixty years.

Ahnentafel #20 · his father’s mother’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Willis Stephens was born on 12 March 1876 in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of Caswell Massey Stephens and his first wife, known to her own gravestone only as A. M. and remembered in the family as Ava Marilla Shelton. His mother died when he was fourteen, and his father married again the following spring, near Cuba.

On 4 July 1906 he married Mary Price Williams in Sumter County; she was nineteen and had been born across the state line at State Line, in Greene County, Mississippi. They had four children — Lois Mildred, born 1908, who married Thomas Turnipseed; Rufus D.; Jess Steve Willard, born at York in 1917; and Clara Mae, born 1922, who married Edgar Herndon. In 1920 the family was at Election Precinct 6, York.

Like his father he went by “J. W.” He farmed and kept a dairy, lived his whole life in Sumter County, and was a member of the Baptist church for fifty years. In time he moved the family to Livingston, the county seat — a town of two livery stables still doing business in the middle of it, the court house and jail on Marshall Street, the Everitt Hotel on Spring, the public school, the Methodist church and the public library. He was living there when his father died in February 1935; the Livingston paper offered its sympathy to him by name “in the loss of his father.”

On the last Sunday of July 1939 he and his wife gave the Stephens-Williams reunion at their Livingston house, and the company that day ran from his brother H. R. at Halsell and his sister’s Knight household to his wife’s Williams brothers coming in from Pascagoula, Baton Rouge and Jackson.

He died at home on the night of Sunday 13 April 1947, aged seventy-one, after a long illness — though the stone was later cut with the 14th. The funeral was held in the house at half past ten on the Tuesday, Rev. Audrey Granade assisted by Rev. Henry Niles. He lies in Myrtlewood Cemetery at Livingston under a double stone with his wife, who followed him in 1957: his half headed FATHER, hers MOTHER, a stone urn between them and the family name cut across the base.

Thomas William Truette was born in September 1893 in Clarke County, Alabama, the son of William Curtis Truett and Amanda Webb, and grew up answering to the family’s own pet name for him, “Tamie.” In June 1900 he was six years old at Clarksville, fifth of six children in a farmhouse full of them: Tela, thirteen; Jane, twelve; Bigy, ten; Lillie, eight; himself; and Bessie, who was one.
