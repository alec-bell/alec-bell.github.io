Helen May Knott (~1905 Indiana – 1977 Evanston IL)

Ahnentafel #13 · his mother’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Helen May Knott was born on 12 June 1905 in Indiana, the younger daughter of Joseph McGee Knott, a heater in a Terre Haute steel mill, and Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford Knott. Her sister Mildred was seven years older; a half-brother, Percy Ford, was a grown man by the time Helen could walk. She grew up with all three of them under one roof in Terre Haute — in 1910 Helen was four, Mildred eleven and Percy twenty-four.

Three photographs survive of her childhood about 1911 to 1913: one on the lawn beside her big sister, which their mother sent as a postcard inscribed “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen”; and two of Helen with her friend Grace, both girls in wide straw hats. Grace was almost certainly Grace Estella Schomer, born six months after her in December 1905, the daughter of the man who ran Terre Haute’s dancing school — a child performer the local paper once called “the miniature Pavlowa.” She lived to be ninety-seven.

In the autumn of 1920 she started at Garfield High School, Terre Haute, where the yearbook listed the new freshmen as the “Cradle Roll” of the Class of 1924, under a cartoon of a howling baby in a high chair; she had left by the next year. On 2 April 1923 Helen married Gifford Herman Tuttle in Rush County. Two sons followed, Robert Dale in 1925 and Richard Clark — Alec’s grandfather — in the last days of July 1929, when the family were living at 2266 Warren Street and the Terre Haute paper announced him in a single line: “to G. H. and Helen Tuttle, 2266 Warren street, a son, Richard Clark.” They raised the boys in a rented house in Terre Haute through the Depression, with Helen’s widowed mother living under the same roof.

Her father died in January 1932 at her sister’s house in Cedar Rapids, and lay in Highland Lawn Cemetery across the city under a flat granite marker cut simply Joseph Knott 1851 1932; her mother joined him in the same grave in 1945, under a matching stone.

About 1944 or 1945 the family moved north to Evanston, Illinois, and into St. Luke’s Episcopal Church, which they belonged to for thirty years. A hand-tinted studio portrait shows Helen in her late thirties, dark hair set in rolled waves; another photograph catches her with Gifford in the early 1970s, near their fiftieth anniversary. Gifford died in 1974, and Helen lived on as a widow in Wilmette.

She died on Sunday evening, 24 April 1977, at Evanston Hospital, aged seventy-one. The Chicago Tribune carried her two mornings later under her own maiden name — “Helen Knott Tuttle of Wilmette” — naming her two sons, her seven grandchildren and her sister Mildred Cooper of Cedar Rapids, and setting the visitation and a requiem eucharist for that evening in the Lady Chapel of St. Luke’s. The notice ends with two words that explain why no grave of hers has ever been found: “Interment private.” Terre Haute had not forgotten her either: the same morning her old home-town paper ran her among its DEATHS, word having reached a sister-in-law still living in the city. Where she was buried is not known. Mildred followed her six months later.

Alan C. Frost (1903 Paterson – 1982 Clyde NY)

Ahnentafel #14 · his mother’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alan Chalmers Frost was born on 10 February 1903 at Paterson, New Jersey, the third child of Harry B. Frost, an English-born silk manufacturer, and Katharine Chalmers of Orange. His middle name was his mother’s maiden name. In a photograph of about 1903 or 1904 he is an infant in a long white gown and bonnet, propped on a stool between his sister Katherine, six or seven, and his brother Harry, three or four.

In 1905, aged two, he was living in a Paterson household that kept a live-in servant. By 1910 the family had moved out to Wyckoff, in Franklin Township, Bergen County, and that is where he grew up; in 1915 he was twelve and at school.

By the time his father died in December 1930 he was living at Wilmington, Delaware, and he was still there in January 1932, when he came up to spend a Sunday and Monday with his widowed mother on Wyckoff Avenue. She died three weeks later. By that summer he was working at Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, and came home to Wyckoff Avenue for the Fourth of July and again for a two-week vacation.

Some time in the early 1930s — when and where is not known — he married Velma Gladys Roush of Mason County, West Virginia, and their daughter Judith was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. He was with S. S. Kresge by the mid-1930s, at Sandusky, Ohio, and in October 1937 he came to Mansfield, Ohio, as assistant manager of the company’s Dollar store; in January 1939 he took over as manager. In July 1940 “the A. C. Frosts of Linden road, and their daughter Judy” were vacationing with relatives in New Jersey, and in February 1942 he had two men jailed for thirty days for stealing ten pairs of men’s socks from the store. He registered for the draft on 16 February 1942 from 1506 West Fourth Street, Marion, working for the S. S. Kresge Co.: five foot ten, a hundred and forty-five pounds, light complexion, brown hair, blue eyes, and the person who would always know where to find him was Velma Gladys Frost. He spent his whole working life in that chain, running the Marion store for many years at seventy hours a week, until Jupiter took over the location about 1962 and the Frosts moved to Flint, Michigan.

Velma died at Flint on 3 October 1964; they were living then at 316 West Dartmouth. He outlived her by eighteen years and died in May 1982, aged seventy-nine, at Clyde, a village in Wayne County, New York. What took him to the Finger Lakes was never explained in this family, but his elder brother Harry had married a Clyde girl, Esther Baird, in the village church there in December 1933 and lies in its Maple Grove cemetery — which is very likely the answer. Where Alan himself is buried is not known.

Velma Gladys Roush (1907 Mason Co. WV – 1964 Flint MI)

Ahnentafel #15 · his mother’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Velma Gladys Roush was born on 21 June 1907 in Mason County, West Virginia, the daughter of John G. Roush, a coal miner, and Bertha Ellen Nease.

She was fifteen months old on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, when her father tried to fill a coal-miner’s lamp from an oil can that had been standing by a hot cook stove, and the house at Broad Run went up. Her eldest sister, who had been ironing beside the table, died within hours; a second sister died five days later; the house and everything in it burned, including the family’s clothes. Velma was the infant asleep in the room, and a six-year-old sister carried her out through the smoke to their grandparents’ house, unseen and unaided. Her father died of his burns on 21 October. She can never have remembered any of it, and within six weeks the two people best able to tell her were dead.

Her widowed mother kept the four surviving girls together. In May 1909, before Velma was two, Bertha went to the Mason County court and qualified as guardian of “Iva Roush, Florence Roush, Velm Roush and Della Roush, infant children of John G. Roush, deceased,” on a $500 bond, with the girls’ grandfather Moses Roush standing surety. In 1910 the household at Wagner, Mason County, was Bertha at its head with Iva, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia; in 1920 Velma was still in the county, about thirteen. She ran away from home at sixteen. She was five feet tall, and somebody wrote in her school yearbook, “She’s small but oh my!”

On 6 September 1931 she married Alfred Hesterberg at Greenup, Kentucky, signing as Velma Roush. He divorced her at Akron in October 1933, when she was living at Charleston, West Virginia. Her daughter was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 she was at Mansfield, Ohio, married to Alan C. Frost, and by 1950 at Marion, Indiana, where he managed the S. S. Kresge dollar store on the square and worked seventy hours a week.

In Marion the family lived at 914 South D Street, and she went with her husband to the staff parties: a Valentine dinner for the employees at the Marion Hotel in February 1947, and in May 1949 a dinner for both Marion stores’ staff to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the Kresge chain, where she won a prize. In March 1947 she and her daughter drove to Akron to see her sister and went on to Mason, West Virginia, where her mother was ill. She took a few days with relatives at Cleveland in 1951. On a Monday morning in May 1953 she cut one of the ribbons at the opening of a new Marion store, standing in for the town’s retail trade beside the mayor’s wife and the wives of the president and vice-president of the Chamber of Commerce. At Christmas 1953 she and Alan put up a neighbourhood tree with the Drummonds at the corner of Ninth and D Streets, and the neighbours held a party round it; the Marion paper printed a photograph of it lit up at night. She spent a whole month in Florida in the spring of 1954. In August 1955 she gave a lawn shower for a bride-elect at her own house, her daughter helping as assistant hostess, with an umbrella and a miniature bride and bridegroom over the gift table.

Her health was failing well before the end; she was admitted to a Marion hospital twice within nine days in November 1959. About 1962 the family moved to Flint, Michigan, when another chain took the Kresge location over, and she belonged to the Episcopal church there. She had arthritis, and the treatment of the day was aspirin in high doses, which is hard on the stomach. She died on 3 October 1964 at Hurley Hospital in Flint, aged fifty-seven, of a perforated duodenum, and was cremated at White Chapel three days later. She left two sisters, Mrs. Joseph Walk of Akron and Mrs. William Kirton of Boynton Beach. Two photographs of her survive: a studio portrait, and a colour snapshot of her in her fifties, laughing in a red-and-black plaid coat with the autumn leaves down behind her.

Thomas Jefferson Bell (1875/78 – 1921 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #16 · his father’s father’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Thomas Jefferson Bell — Tom to everyone who knew him — was born in Barbour County, Alabama in the autumn of 1875, one of the eleven children of John T. Bell and Sallie Cox, and he farmed all his life near Louisville, on the country six miles south of Clayton where his grandparents had settled in about 1830. He married Alice May Bradley on 13 August 1895, when she was eighteen and newly out of the guardianship the county had placed her in as an orphan.

They had nine children. Seven were living in January 1920, when the whole household was under one roof at Louisville — Henry at eighteen, Ray at sixteen, William at twelve, Ethel at ten and little Juanita at two — and two were already buried in the churchyard at Bethlehem Baptist, the congregation his people had belonged to since thirteen of their neighbours gathered it in April 1824. His cotton went to Clayton, a county town of three warehouses on the Public Square — J. C. Thomas’s, the Alliance’s and the Farmers Union’s — with the court hall and fire department, the Fryer Hotel, the post office and a water works and electric light plant beside the Central of Georgia tracks.

He died on 30 August 1921, at forty-five, and was laid at Bethlehem beside those two children. The Clayton Record called him a son of Mr. and Mrs. John Bell Sr., pioneer citizens, buried “where two of his children are buried,” and named the five brothers who survived him — Mack and Will of Louisville, Ben, Ed and John of Clayton. The granite his family set over him afterwards gives dates a year earlier than the county’s own, SEPT. 18, 1874 and AUG. 27, 1920, and the two have never been reconciled.

He left nothing to divide. There was no estate at all, and no administration was ever opened in his name — which is the more striking because his own father had died six years before him worth fifty thousand dollars in lands, cotton, insurance and money. What his children eventually got came out of that estate instead: in April 1925 a Barbour County court split one deceased uncle’s share seven ways among them, naming each in turn — Ellie, Ray, Ruth, William, Ethel, the youngest Juanita, and “Henry Bell, Birmingham.”

Alice outlived him by thirty-five years, married again, and was brought home to lie beside him. Their names are cut side by side on one block of grey granite at Bethlehem, under the single word BELL.

Alice May Bradley (1876 – 1956 Montgomery Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #17 · his father’s father’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice May Bradley was born on 8 October 1876 in Barbour County, Alabama, the fourth of the five surviving children of Henry Bradley, clerk of the circuit court, and his wife Elvira Loveless. She lost both of them young. Her father died in February 1887, when she was ten, of an old war wound; her mother died of cholera morbus on a Sunday afternoon in June 1891. They lie together at Clayton City Cemetery under two draped marble stones inside one iron rail.

That October a Barbour County court appointed a guardian for Alice, then fourteen, and her younger sister Katie, twelve. The petitioner was a great-aunt, L. M. Bell, and among the seven signers of the four-thousand-dollar bond were two Bradley relations and John T. Bell — the man who would be her father-in-law four years later. Two years after that, a Eufaula paper printed the probate court’s settlement of the earlier guardianship and listed her by name among “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased”, with her sisters Ida Parker and Annie Fortner and her brother Henry.

She married Thomas Jefferson Bell on 13 August 1895, at eighteen, and bore nine children. In December 1898 her sister Katie eloped to Georgetown and married Tom’s younger brother George, so the two orphaned Bradley girls went into the same farming family at Louisville, and two sons of John T. Bell married two daughters of Henry Bradley.

Widowed in 1921, she married Mack K. Norton — and there is a quiet symmetry in it, because Mack’s first wife had been Katie, who died in 1924. Two Bradley sisters each married a Bell, each was widowed, and the same man married them in turn. By 1935 Alice was living with Mack at 118 East North Street in Dothan, and it was that address her eldest son wrote down in 1942 when a draft board asked who would always know where he was. She had been eighteen at her first marriage and had borne nine children, and she said so to the census-taker in 1940. Mack died in 1954; his obituary called her children his step-sons and step-daughters and named them as Bells — T. R. Bell of Bessemer, W. W. Bell of Jacksonville, and four married daughters.

She outlived her first husband by thirty-five years and died at Montgomery on 29 December 1956, aged eighty. They brought her home to Bethlehem, in Barbour County, and buried her beside Tom under the name Bell — not Norton. Her dates are cut on the right-hand half of his stone: ALICE, OCT. 8, 1876, DEC. 29, 1956.

Andrew Jackson Eaken (1881 Lauderdale Co. MS – 1922)

Ahnentafel #18 · his father’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Andrew Jackson Eaken was born on 1 May 1881 in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, one of the youngest children of Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins and Sarah Hudson Payne. His people had come to Mississippi out of New Hanover County, North Carolina, and he reckoned both his parents North Carolina born.

About 1907 he married Maggie Dillon Swain, of York and Cuba in Sumter County, Alabama — the next county across the state line. In 1910 the two of them were living at Kosters, in Mobile, with their one-year-old daughter Beatrice and Maggie’s young brother Langford Swain lodging in the house. In 1917 he registered for the draft at Princeton, in Jackson County at the far north-east corner of Alabama. He was a man who crossed the state repeatedly, and the clerks spelled him as they heard him: by 1920 the family name was being written “Eakans.”

He was living at 2716 Avenue F South in Birmingham when he died on the night of 20 March 1922, aged forty. The body was sent down to Cuba the next morning, and the funeral and burial took place there, at Clay Memorial Cemetery in his wife’s home village, among her people. He left Maggie a widow at thirty-five. Their children included Alec’s great-grandmother Margaret Beatrice; Andrew Jackson “Jack” Eaken, who became a colonel; Joseph William “Jo Will” Eaken, who rose to be a vice-president of American Airlines; and Melvin Swain Eaken.

The surname moved under him. “Our name was originally spelled Eakins,” his granddaughter wrote in 2009. “My father’s grandfather stopped dotting the ‘i’ and it became an ‘e’. My father dropped the ‘s’, as did his brothers.” Andrew Jackson was the Eaken in the middle of that sentence.
