“Calm” Lawrence (Clarke Co., Alabama)

Ahnentafel #46 · his father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sennie Lawrence’s father was born in Alabama in September 1860, to a father from North Carolina and a mother from South Carolina. His given name has never been settled. The hand that wrote it down in 1900 reads “Calm,” and what it was meant to be nobody knows.

He married Eliza about 1888 — they had been married twelve years when they were counted in 1900 — and they farmed at Coffeeville in Clarke County. Five children were in the house that year: Tomas N., eleven; Sallie E., nine; Mary E., six; Senie E., three; and Alfonso, entered as nought days old, because the enumerator had called on the day the baby was born. Two more followed, Lucy about 1905 among them.

He was not an old Clarke County man. The county’s history of 1882 names hundreds of its families across seven hundred and eighty pages and has no Lawrences in it at all, so his people must have come in from somewhere nearby — Washington or Choctaw or Marengo — recently enough to be strangers; the name is written Larance as readily as Lawrence.

By 1910 he was gone from the household: Eliza was head of the house with six children and no husband in it. A C. J. Lawrence who was very probably him died near Jackson, in Clarke County, in February 1929 — a widower, seventy-two years old by the clerk’s reckoning, born in Monroe County, Alabama — and was buried at Coffeeville.

He is very probably buried at Ulcanush Cemetery in Coffeeville, the same ground as his daughter Sennie and his infant son Alphonso, under one of two home-made markers that stand side by side there: slabs of rough concrete with the lettering scratched into them with a nail while the cement was still wet. His gives only initials, C J Lawrence. The other gives no name at all, only Mrs Lawrence, and a birth year that is his wife’s to the year.

Ahnentafel #47 · his father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Eliza was born in Alabama about 1863 or 1864 and married, about 1888, a Clarke County farmer named Lawrence whose given name nobody has been able to read — the hand that wrote it in 1900 gives “Calm.” They farmed at Coffeeville, and in 1900 she was thirty-six with five children in the house: Tomas N., Sallie E., Mary E., Senie E., and a baby, Alfonso, who was nought days old when the enumerator called. By 1910 her husband had gone, and she was head of the household: “Elizon A. Larance,” with six children under her roof and no man in it. She kept them together for at least another decade.

She died in 1927, in her early sixties. At Ulcanush Cemetery in Coffeeville — the ground where her son Alphonso and her daughter Sennie lie — stands a marker that is very probably hers: a slab of rough concrete, the lettering scratched in by hand while it was still wet, reading Mrs Lawrence · Born 1864 · Died 1927. The 1864 is the year her own age gives. The stone does not give her name. What is written on it, and has been for a hundred years, is the word Mrs.

Richard B. Tuttle (1858 – 1884)

Ahnentafel #48 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Richard B. Tuttle was born on 24 March 1858, the fourth child of Clark Strong Tuttle and his second wife Nancy A. Harbert, at Tuttle's Mills in Otter Creek Township, Vigo County, Indiana. He first appears in the record aged two, in the 1860 census of Otter Creek, in a prosperous miller's house with three hired hands and a servant living in — and with an older brother named Dennis, aged four, whose name he would give his own son.

He followed his father's trade and was recorded as a miller. By the time he was grown the mill had gone: his father sold the 117-acre mill tract in July 1870, dissolved the firm a fortnight later, and was out of the township altogether by the autumn.

On 12 October 1881, at twenty-three, he married Emma S. Fuerstenberger, the daughter of a Bavarian shoemaker in Terre Haute. Some time in the next two years the young couple went to Peoria, Illinois, where their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born on 25 January 1884; nobody has yet found out why they were there.

Terre Haute as he knew it survives in a bird's-eye view drawn in 1880, four years before he died: the grid of streets running back from the Wabash, the bridges, the mills along the river and the Normal School on its square.

They were back in Terre Haute before the year was out, and on the morning of Wednesday 17 December 1884 he died of consumption. He was twenty-six. The Daily Wabash Express carried the notice the next day under ANNOUNCEMENTS — DIED, and it says where he died: the funeral was at ten o'clock that morning from the house of his father-in-law Frank Fuerstenberger on South Third Street. No gravestone for him is known.

Emma S. Fuerstenberger (1863 Terre Haute – 1926)

Ahnentafel #49 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emma S. Fuerstenberger was born in July 1863 in Terre Haute, Indiana, the fifth of the eight children of Francis Fuerstenberger, a Bavarian shoemaker, and his Pennsylvania-born wife Sophia. She grew up in the one house her father occupied for forty years, 521 South Third Street, and was still at home, aged seventeen, when the census-taker came in 1880 and wrote the family name down as “Fuerstebuger.”

On 12 October 1881, at eighteen, she married Richard B. Tuttle, a young miller. Their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born at Peoria, Illinois, on 25 January 1884, and in December of that year Richard died of consumption in her father's house. She was twenty-one, with a baby.

She married twice more — Hamilton B. Martin on 24 May 1891, and on 20 December 1894, at Charleston in Coles County, Illinois, Gifford H. Spear, the record indexing her as “Emma Furstenberger Tuttle.” Spear is the man who finished raising her son, and Dennis repaid him by naming his own firstborn Gifford.

In September 1916 she and Gifford Spear were living at 512 North Eighth Street in Terre Haute and had just come home from a month's holiday in California.

Her sister Anna married James H. Jumper at Terre Haute on 11 October 1876, and the certificate they kept in the family Bible says who married them: H. E. Wulzen, pastor of the German Methodist Episcopal church. John Fuerstenberger signed as witness. The Bible also kept what families keep - a slip of receipt paper listing which minister baptised which child, and, in a small white paper bag, some of young Elmer's hair.

She died on 9 April 1926 as Emma S. Spear. The family Bible records the death under that name, with “Tuttle” pencilled faintly above it — somebody wanting the record to remember which husband she belonged to. Eight years later her son stood in her place when a Fuerstenberger estate was divided five ways among her brothers' and sisters' lines.

Mary Elizabeth Fletcher (d. 1934)

Ahnentafel #51 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Elizabeth Fletcher was born on 21 September 1858 in Indiana, the daughter of John Fletcher and Sarah Wallace. Where the family was through her childhood nobody has managed to find; no John-and-Sarah Fletcher household containing a Mary of the right age appears in the Indiana census indexes for 1860 or 1870.

She surfaces in 1880 at Clinton, in Vermillion County, aged twenty-three, single, and enumerated alone in another family's house as a “houseworker” — a live-in domestic servant. A year later, on 16 June 1881, she married William L. Alexander of the same small town, and in February 1883 their daughter Leona was born. She bore one child and one only.

She was a widow by 1900, and she had not gone under. The census of that June finds her at 314 Mulberry Street in Terre Haute, forty years old, head of her own household, working as a housekeeper and owning the house — with her seventeen-year-old daughter beside her. Two years later Leona married Dennis Tuttle, and Mary moved in with them. She is in their household in 1910, in 1920 and again in 1930: thirty-four years under her daughter's roof.

In December 1902 she was living on North Sixth Street in Terre Haute, and it was in her house that her only child was married: the Daily Tribune said the ceremony would be "at the home of the bride's mother on North Sixth street." Twelve years later she was still travelling with the family — "Mr. and Mrs. Dennis Tuttle and children, Gifford and Dorothy, and Mrs. Mary Alexander, of Terre Haute" went out to West Terre Haute for the weekend in August 1914, and again in September.

She died on 16 April 1934 at East Ellen, Vigo County, aged seventy-five. The family Bible records the death simply as “Mary Elizabeth Alexander (1934).” Her death certificate is the one document that names her parents, and it is the only door this family has into the Fletcher and Wallace lines.

Benjamin Nott/Knott (~1817 OH – 1891 Marietta)

Ahnentafel #52 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Nott — the name is written Knott about as often as Nott, and the 1881 county history prints its own correction, “Benjamin Knott probably should be Benj. Nott, as per 1810 Federal Census of Wash. Co.” — farmed in Adams Township, near Lowell, in Washington County, Ohio. He was born about 1815–17 in Ohio. Nothing yet found names his own parents, though an elder Benjamin Nott “of Adams” died insolvent in 1822, leaving a widow Polly, and if that man was his father he was orphaned as a small boy on a broke farm on Calls Creek.

He married a Margaret; an index entry for a marriage of “Benj. Nott” to Margaret Kirk in Morgan County on 13 March 1836 is the best lead to her surname, but the image has never been read and a rival Benjamin married a Lavina Tewel in Washington County in 1839. The 1860 census shows the household at Lowell — Benjamin forty-three, a farmer; Margaret forty-three; P. J. fifteen; William F. thirteen; and Jos M eleven. That eleven-year-old is believed to be Joseph McGee Knott, whose 1932 death certificate names Benjamin and Margaret as his parents and Lowell as his birthplace; but the census gives no relationships and its implied birth year for Joseph is about two years out from the certificate, so the link is treated as probable rather than proved.

In July 1861, at forty-six, a Benjamin Nott of the county volunteered for Company A of the Thirty-sixth Ohio and was mustered out in 1864 — and it was him: a state register of soldiers’ graves, compiled in Ohio in the twentieth century, sets down the same service on his card, enlisted 29 July 1861, discharged 4 September 1864, private, Company A, 36th Regiment Ohio Infantry. By 1870 the family was in Marietta, where the census lists Benjamin fifty-three, Margaret fifty-two, John W. twenty-three, Sarah twenty, a baby Henry and three boarders. His last years were hard. In December 1890 the probate court recorded “P. J. Nott, guardian of Benjamin Nott, an Insane person” filing an inventory — the guardian was his own child, the P. J. of the 1860 household. He died between that December and the following 6 July, when the final account was settled: the estate was charged with nothing at all, credited with $10.91, and that sum was found due the child who had cared for him. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891, of la grippe — the influenza that went round the world between 1889 and 1891 — and the register of soldiers’ graves lays him in Oak Grove Cemetery at Marietta, lot 90, block 19, under an upright stone.

Erwin R. Lamb (1840 Wayne Co. IN – 1919 Jonesboro)

Ahnentafel #54 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Erwin R. Lamb — Irvin on his own gravestone, Irwin in his obituary; the three spellings are one man — was born on 19 March 1840 in Wayne County, Indiana, the second of the ten children of Elias Lamb, carpenter and farmer, and Susannah (Bish) Lamb, who had married at her father’s house in Clay Township on 23 March 1837. His grandparents were Joseph and Lydia (Adamson) Lamb, Quakers who had brought the family from Randolph County, North Carolina, to Wayne County in April 1829, eleven years before he was born. He was reared on his father’s farm in Clay Township and stayed in the county until he was a grown man.

On 14 March 1860 he took out a licence in Wayne County to marry Melissa J. Bailey, the daughter of a neighbouring farming family; the minister’s return on the same leaf of the register is dated the 15th. It was a double season: his elder brother Azel had married Sarah Ann Bailey three months before, in the next township. The census that summer found the newlyweds still under his father’s roof in Clay Township, on a farm the enumerator valued at eleven thousand dollars. Four children came — Alma S., Martha E., Frank A. and May Belle — and then, on the evening of Monday 29 November 1875, after a few days’ illness, Melissa died at Newport in Vermillion County, aged thirty-one. The family had moved there in 1870 with his parents.

On 18 May 1879 he married Mrs. Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, the widow of a soldier of the 125th Illinois Infantry and the daughter of Arthur Jackson, an early Vermillion County settler who had given three sons to the Union and lost all three. She brought five Hall children with her. The census of 1880 shows the two families together on Main Street in Vermillion — Erwin forty, “Mary E.” forty-one, his own Mattie, Frank and Bell, and her Effie. By 1888 he was living at Newport, and the county’s biographical volume gave him a sketch that spent most of its length on his second wife’s people.

He did not stay in Indiana. He ended his life at Jonesboro, in Craighead County, Arkansas, where he had lived “a number of years,” a member of the First Methodist church and a regular attender until his health failed. He died at St. Bernard’s Hospital at half past midnight on 27 February 1919, aged seventy-eight, after an operation for blood poisoning. The local paper called him “a splendid man” and named as his survivors his wife and two step-children, Mrs. Bertha Conger and A. W. Hall, both of Jonesboro — the Bertha and Allen W. Hall of 1888. His own children, his daughter Martha among them, are not mentioned. He was buried at Jonesboro, under a plain marble block cut IRVIN R LAMB.

He kept the books for other men to the end of his working life: for many years he was clerk at the Old American Hotel in Jonesboro, and he had been retired some time before he died. He and his wife were settled at Jonesboro, in a house on Krewson Avenue, by 1911 at the latest. The last ten days of his life are in the Jonesboro papers almost day by day. He had been ill at home for ten days in October 1918 and in poor health for eight months; on Monday 17 February 1919 an infected foot was amputated at St. Bernard's Hospital, and the paper noted that at his age the operation was a shock to his nervous system; he was reported resting nicely, then improving; the surgeons went back and took the leg off below the knee for gangrene; and he died at half past twelve on the morning of 27 February. Both Jonesboro papers carried him. The Sun called him "an old resident of the city… highly respected by everyone, making a friend of every acquaintance," and named four step-children — A. W. Hall and Mrs. C. C. Conger of Jonesboro, Mrs. Josephine Schweizer of Danville, Illinois, and Mrs. Effie Townsend of Richmond, Illinois. His widow Mary outlived him by two years. She died at the same hospital on 20 February 1921 after a long illness, having lived since his death with her daughter Mrs. Bertha Conger, and was buried from the First Methodist Church.

In May 1890 he was very probably the Erwin Lamb of Plainfield, Indiana, who came down to Terre Haute to stay with two of his children — his daughter, by then Mrs. Mattie Ford, and his son Frank — who were keeping house together on north Third Street.

That he was Martha Ellen Lamb’s father rests on three converging records — her 1945 death certificate names “Erwin Lamb”; the 1880 census puts her in his house as “Mattie E., daughter”; the 1888 sketch lists “Martha E.” among his four children by his first wife — but a two- to three-year disagreement about her birth year runs through them, so the identification is carried as probable.
