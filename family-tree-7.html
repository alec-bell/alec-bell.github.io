Sarah Hudson Payne (1842 MS – 1908)

Sarah Hudson Payne was born on 2 May 1842 in Mississippi, and grew up in Lauderdale County in a crowded house. William Payne, who headed it, was fifty-seven in 1850 and Virginia born; his wife Mary was thirty and born in North Carolina; four boys born in North Carolina and carrying the Eakens name, aged ten to sixteen, the fatherless sons of Andrew Eakin, whose property William Payne managed for them; and four younger children had been born in Mississippi, of whom Sarah was the eldest. Two of the little ones were called Origon and Joel. Whether William Payne was her father or her stepfather has never been settled.

What is certain is the household — and what came of it. On 13 May 1861 she married Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins, who bore the same surname as the orphaned boys who had grown up under that roof.

They farmed in Lauderdale County for forty years. She bore ten or more children: Mary A., Ada J., John N., Minnie, Cassie, Nancy, a Sam, and two she named Orgun and Joel after the little boys she had been raised with. Her youngest, Andrew Jackson Eaken, born in 1880 or 1881, is the one through whom the line runs down to Alec.

She died on 1 May 1908, one day short of her sixty-sixth birthday. Her stone still stands over her in a fenced Mississippi burying ground, a leaning white marble obelisk behind a chain-link fence: SARAH H. EAKINS, BORN May 2, 1842, DIED May 1, 1908, AGE 65 Yrs 11 Mos & 29 Days. Her middle name, Hudson, has never been explained, and her mother’s name is not known.

Joseph L. Swain (1861 MS – 1940 York AL)

Ahnentafel #38 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lee Swain was born on 23 December 1861, the eldest child of Joseph Tolbert Swain and Nancy E. Jane, who had married in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, the year before. He was four or five when his parents crossed the state line into Sumter County, Alabama in the first months after the war, and he grew up at Cuba; in 1880, at nineteen, he stood at the head of a row of eight younger brothers and sisters.

He married twice over, and buried a wife in between. On 10 February 1884 he married Eugenia Simmons, with J. M. Patton the minister — and a son of his would be named Patton. A wife called Leila Eugenia died before the end of 1885 and lies in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, across the line in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, under a stone that gives 1861–1885 and calls her “wife of J. L. Swain”; whether she was the bride of 1884 nobody now knows. Then on 23 December 1885, his own twenty-fourth birthday, he married the woman the register enters as Rebecca A. D. Simmons and everyone else called Dillon. The minister that day was D. C. Langford, and they named a son for him too: Joseph Langford Swain.

They farmed at York and Cuba. In 1900 the children at home were Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford, and he gave his years married as sixteen — counting, it seems, from the first wedding rather than the second. In November 1905 he sold a hundred and sixty acres at the post-office door at Cuba as administrator of the estate of J. T. Swain, very probably his own father, dead the year before. He stood for public office as well: at a meeting of the voters of the third commissioners district at Livingston on 28 August 1911 the vote went fifty-seven to twenty-seven in his favour, and in September he announced himself from Cuba as a candidate for county commissioner, “subject to the action of the Democratic party.” The Livingston paper thought he would make a good commissioner.

Five of their children lie in the family burying ground at Salem Baptist, and the dates make a hard chapter of their life: Marvin, dead at two in 1892; Fletcher, dead at two in 1905; and then Patton, dead at nineteen in January 1916, Lucille Kirkland, dead at eighteen in January 1917, and Myrtle, dead at sixteen the following month. Three grown children in thirteen months.

Cuba, as it stood in 1928, was laid out building by building along one street: the Walker-Shaw planing mill and saw mill at one end, a grist mill and feed store, G. W. McElroy’s blacksmith and carpenter shop, the Cuba Mercantile Co., the Southern Railway depot with its covered platform, a Methodist church and two Holiness churches.

He lived out his life on the land three miles west of York, near the old McConnell Station — a six-room house on twenty acres, with running water and a telephone. He died there at a quarter to one on the afternoon of Thursday 21 November 1940, aged seventy-nine, after a long illness, and the funeral was held in the house the next afternoon. He left his widow, “Mrs. Dillon Simmons Swain,” four daughters — Leila Collier of Meridian, Maggie Bloebaum of Wheaton, Illinois, Pinkie McElroy of Fort Knox and Merle Cobb of Geiger — one son, Langford of Cuba, a brother, John M. Swain of York, and two sisters, Mattie Shamburger and Laura McDonald. A week later the widow and all five surviving children signed a card of thanks to their neighbours, styling him Mr. Joe L. Swain, Sr. He was buried at Salem Baptist; his stone gives his name as JOE L. SWAIN and, under the dates, one word: PAPA.

Dillon Simmons (1863 Sumter AL – 1952 Sumter AL)

Ahnentafel #39 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Dillon Simmons was born on 1 December 1863 in Sumter County, Alabama, a daughter of Ben and Adeline Simmons of Cuba. At sixteen she was still at home in her father’s house, her name written down “Dellon.”

She married Joseph L. Swain of the same small village on 23 December 1885; the register entered her as Rebecca A. D. Simmons, but Dillon is what she was called all her life. He had been married before, and an earlier wife, Leila Eugenia, had died by the end of that year. The Swains farmed at York and Cuba, three miles out near the old McConnell Station, in a six-room house on twenty acres. In 1900 she had six children at home: Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford.

Five of her children lie in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, across the state line in Lauderdale County, Mississippi: two who died as infants, and Patton, Lucille Kirkland and Myrtle, who died grown within thirteen months of one another in 1916 and 1917. Her husband’s obituary of November 1940 named her in print as “his wife, Mrs. Dillon Simmons Swain,” and when her son Joseph Langford Swain died at New Orleans in 1946 she was named as his mother, Dillon Simmons. In May 1951 her Wheaton daughter came down to visit her, and the county paper called her Mrs. Dillon Swain.

Her daughter Maggie Dillon Swain carried her name forward, and it travelled two more generations as a middle name nobody could explain.

She outlived her husband by twelve years and died on 1 November 1952, aged eighty-eight; her four daughters thanked their neighbours in the Sumter County Journal a fortnight later for their kindness “during the illness and passing of our precious mother.” She left a will, and on 6 February 1953 Judge Wilbur E. Dearman of the Sumter County probate court granted letters testamentary on it to three of her daughters — Maggie S. Bloebaum, Pinkie S. McElroy and Merle S. Cobb — who published the notice to creditors through that February and March. She lies beside her husband at Salem Baptist under a matching stone: DILLON S. SWAIN, DEC. 1, 1863 – NOV. 1, 1952, and beneath the dates, answering his PAPA, the single word MAMA.
