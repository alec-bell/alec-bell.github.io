William Curtis Truett (1867 – 1925 New Prospect AL)

Ahnentafel #44 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Curtis Truett was born on 25 July 1867, the son of John Wesley Truett and Emily Caroline Ward, and grew up at Chilton in Clarke County, Alabama, in a house that held three generations at once: his father, a Confederate veteran who could not write his own name; his mother; his brothers and his sister; and his grandmother Alcey, the widow of the preacher William Hampton Truett, who was sixty-seven in 1880 and living under her son’s roof.

On 17 June 1886, not yet nineteen, he married Amanda Webb in Clarke County. They farmed at Clarksville, and by the summer of 1900 there were six children in the house — Tela, Jane, Bigy, Lillie, Tamie and the baby Bessie. He and Amanda could both read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do: the first literate generation on this line. Thomas William, the “Tamie” of that household, was Alec’s great-grandfather.

Amanda was alive in that house in June 1900 and dead or gone not long after, and he married a second time, to Zella Shewmake, born 1 June 1878.

He farmed at New Prospect, a settlement whose little Baptist church had been constituted about 1860 by an Elder C. J. Miles, who for his trouble was paid “one dollar per day while in active service.” When the county’s thirty-four Baptist churches were apportioned in 1892, New Prospect was asked for sixteen dollars — near the smallest sum in the county. All the places of his life sat within a few miles of one another: Chilton, where he grew up; Clarksville, where he farmed in middle life; and New Prospect, with Harris Creek running between them.

Two Truett brothers married two Webb sisters — he took Amanda, and his younger brother John Wesley took Valley. That brother died at his home in Coffeeville on a Wednesday morning in February 1936, of pneumonia after a few days’ illness, and was buried at Prospect church, the Rev. T. C. Paul of Grove Hill officiating. By then the family had scattered: a brother George and a sister, Sarah Giger, were both in Florida, where their mother had gone as an old woman.

He died at New Prospect on 21 December 1925, aged fifty-eight, and is buried in the church graveyard under a low double marker of pale granite, TRUETT across the top, his own name on the right and Zella’s on the left. She outlived him by thirty-five years, and the stone was almost certainly cut when she joined him in 1960. Amanda, the mother of his eldest son, is not on it, and is not buried there.

Calm Lawrence (Clarke Co., Alabama)

Ahnentafel #46 · his father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sennie Lawrence’s father was born in Alabama in September 1860, to a father from North Carolina and a mother from South Carolina. His given name has never been settled. The hand that wrote it down in 1900 reads “Calm,” and what it was meant to be nobody knows.

He married Eliza about 1888 — they had been married twelve years when they were counted in 1900 — and they farmed at Coffeeville in Clarke County. Five children were in the house that year: Tomas N., eleven; Sallie E., nine; Mary E., six; Senie E., three; and Alfonso, entered as nought days old, because the enumerator had called on the day the baby was born. Two more followed, Lucy about 1905 among them.

He was not an old Clarke County man. The county’s history of 1882 names hundreds of its families across seven hundred and eighty pages and has no Lawrences in it at all, so his people must have come in from somewhere nearby — Washington or Choctaw or Marengo — recently enough to be strangers; the name is written Larance as readily as Lawrence.

By 1910 he was gone from the household: Eliza was head of the house with six children and no husband in it. A C. J. Lawrence who was very probably him died near Jackson, in Clarke County, in February 1929 — a widower, seventy-two years old by the clerk’s reckoning, born in Monroe County, Alabama — and was buried at Coffeeville.

He is very probably buried at Ulcanush Cemetery in Coffeeville, the same ground as his daughter Sennie and his infant son Alphonso, under one of two home-made markers that stand side by side there: slabs of rough concrete with the lettering scratched into them with a nail while the cement was still wet. His gives only initials, C J Lawrence. The other gives no name at all, only Mrs Lawrence, and a birth year that is his wife’s to the year.

Richard B. Tuttle (1858 – 1884)

Ahnentafel #48 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Richard B. Tuttle was born on 24 March 1858 at Tuttle’s Mills, in Otter Creek Township, Vigo County, Indiana, the fourth child of Clark Strong Tuttle and his second wife Nancy A. Harbert. He grew up in a prosperous miller’s house, with three hired hands and a servant living in, and with an older brother named Dennis — the name he would one day give his own son.

He followed his father’s trade and was a miller himself, but by the time he was grown the mill had gone: his father sold the 117-acre mill tract in July 1870, dissolved the firm a fortnight later, and was out of the township altogether by the autumn.

On 12 October 1881, at twenty-three, he married Emma S. Fuerstenberger, the daughter of a Bavarian shoemaker in Terre Haute. Some time in the next two years the young couple went to Peoria, Illinois, where their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born on 25 January 1884; why they were there is not known. The Terre Haute they came home to is the city of the bird’s-eye view drawn in 1880 — the grid of streets running back from the Wabash, the bridges, the mills along the river and the Normal School on its square.

He was dead before the year was out. Consumption took him on the morning of Wednesday 17 December 1884, at twenty-six, and the funeral was held at ten the next morning from his father-in-law Frank Fuerstenberger’s house at 521 South Third Street. No gravestone for him is known.

Benjamin Nott — the name was written Knott about as often as Nott — was born in Ohio about 1815 to 1817 and farmed in Adams Township, near Lowell, in Washington County. His own parents are not known. An elder Benjamin Nott “of Adams” died insolvent in 1822, leaving a widow Polly, and if that man was his father he was orphaned as a small boy on a broke farm on Calls Creek.

He married a Margaret, very probably the Margaret Kirk who married a Benj. Nott in Morgan County, up the Muskingum, on 13 March 1836. In 1860 the household stood at Lowell: Benjamin forty-three, a farmer; Margaret forty-three; P. J. fifteen; William F. thirteen; and Jos M eleven. That eleven-year-old is believed to be Joseph McGee Knott, who named Benjamin and Margaret as his parents and Lowell as his birthplace at the end of his own life; the ages do not quite line up, so the link is probable rather than certain.

In July 1861, at forty-six, he volunteered for Company A of the Thirty-sixth Ohio Infantry — enlisted 29 July 1861, a private, discharged 4 September 1864. By 1870 the family had moved to Marietta, where the household was Benjamin fifty-three, Margaret fifty-two, John W. twenty-three, Sarah twenty, a baby Henry, and three boarders.

His last years were hard. In December 1890 the probate court recorded “P. J. Nott, guardian of Benjamin Nott, an Insane person” filing an inventory — the guardian was his own child, the P. J. of the 1860 household. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891 of la grippe, the influenza that went round the world between 1889 and 1891, and the estate was settled that July: charged with nothing at all, credited with $10.91, and that sum found due the child who had cared for him. He lies in Oak Grove Cemetery at Marietta, lot 90, block 19, under an upright stone.
