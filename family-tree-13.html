Pamelia Clark's father (Farmington, Connecticut)

Ahnentafel #386 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Known unknown — the person existed; the name does not survive

Pamelia Clark's father was a Farmington, Connecticut, man of the Revolutionary generation. His daughter was born there on 4 November 1770 — a date her own gravestone in Indiana confirms eighty years later, to the day, through its age line — and she married Gershom Tuttle at Farmington on 8 July 1788. His name has not been recovered.

He is visible only through his children. One of his sons was an officer of the United States Army who died while in charge of the arsenal at Troy, New York; a grandson was General Newman S. Clarke, born in Connecticut, commissioned an ensign in 1812, brevetted brigadier-general in 1847, and commanding the Department of the Pacific when he died at San Francisco in 1860. The official register of army officers confirms the family's account of the general in every checkable particular.

His daughter carried “Strong” as a middle name, and it descended through four more generations — Clark Strong Tuttle, Dennis Clark, Richard Clark. Whether it came from her mother's family or from somewhere else is not established; the standard genealogy of the Strong family of New England contains no Pamelia Clark born in 1770 and no Clark of Farmington, so the Strong descent this chart once quietly assumed is unsupported.

George Roush (1761 Frederick VA – 1845 Meigs OH)

Ahnentafel #480 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

George Roush was born in July 1761 in Frederick County, Virginia — the part of it that became Shenandoah County eleven years later — one of the younger sons of the German immigrant John Adam Roush and his wife Susannah. He grew up on Mill Creek in a house where the Bible was printed in German, and he was eighteen when the Revolution reached him.

He enlisted in the autumn of 1779 and served two months under his own brother, Captain John Roush, “both of Shenandoah County Virginia.” He was drafted again in the spring of 1780 under Captain Joseph Pugh, and a third time in 1781 for three months — that tour, his brother Jonas later swore, as a substitute for another brother, Jacob. Family tradition, collected while his grandchildren were still alive, held that he was at Yorktown and saw Cornwallis hand over his sword; his brother Jonas certainly was. He applied for his pension in 1832 from Ohio and it was allowed as claim S18579. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him as Patriot Ancestor A098975.

About 1781, in Shenandoah County, he married Catharine Zerkel, the eldest daughter of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville. Their children, born over more than twenty years, included Michael, Magdalena, John — who in 1808 married his cousin Regina, Jonas Roush’s daughter — Daniel, Lewis, Catherine, Samuel, Susannah, Jacob, Mary and a second Jonas.

He went down the Ohio ahead of his family about 1798, taking up land in Mason County with his brother Captain John, in whose name the purchase was made; his son Jacob was still born and baptised back in Shenandoah County in 1799. In 1807 he crossed to Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, buying a level tract from Edward W. Tupper west of the hamlet of Dorcas, near Racine, and he lived on it for the rest of his life. He was a mechanic by trade — a good one, the family said — and a farmer, and when Meigs County organised itself in 1819 his name is among its first jurors. He had been a citizen of three counties at the moment each was created.

Catharine died in Virginia, after a journey home she had pined for; her burial place was never found. On 13 August 1815 George married a widow, Catherine “Kitty” Wolfe, at Gallipolis, and they had one daughter, Hannah. In his last illness, the family remembered, he talked about charging the British and told his comrades to get ready, they were coming over the hill.

He died on 31 May 1845 and lies in Weldon Cemetery on the third terrace above the Ohio at Racine, Meigs County. His small marble stone is still legible: “GEORGE ROUSH … DIED May 31, 1845 AGED 84 years.” On 19 August 1933, before about a thousand descendants, the Sons of the American Revolution dedicated a grave marker there; a flat marker lettered “GEORGE ROUSH / CONTINENTAL LINE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / JUL 1761 – MAY 1845” lies at the foot of the headstone today. His German Bible, said to have come from his father out of Germany, was still in the family in 1928, in the hands of a great-granddaughter at Racine; and of the nine brothers who went to that war he is the only one whose face is known.

Catharine Zerkel (Shenandoah Co., Virginia)

Ahnentafel #481 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Catharine Zerkel was George Roush’s first wife and the mother of his children. Two sources that did not consult each other — the family’s own 1928 history and the Daughters of the American Revolution’s record for her husband — agree on her name, and the DAR’s approved applications through their son John are all marked to her rather than to George’s second wife.

The family history makes her the eldest child of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville in Shenandoah County, born 14 August 1763, with Henry Brock and his wife Magdalene standing as her baptismal sponsors. She married George in Shenandoah County about 1781, and by October 1780 — if the reconstruction of her age holds — she was already a married woman sponsoring a baptism herself. Her first child was born in January 1783. Over the next twenty years there were a dozen more, among them John, born 22 November 1785, who married his cousin Regina Roush.

She went west with her husband — to Mason County about 1798, then across the Ohio into Meigs County in 1807 — and the one story the family kept about her is a homesick one. In declining health she pined for the Virginia home, and at last George set out with her, more than three hundred miles over the mountains on horseback and on foot. They came up the Shenandoah and stood the horses in the door yard of the old plantation. “The joy was too great, she was overwhelmed and wept as a child.” She did not live long after, and was buried in Virginia; where, nobody has recorded. A later Zirkle family historian guessed the old Nease–Zirkle graveyard on Holman’s Creek, where her father and mother lie, but he was guessing. Her husband married again in 1815, so she was dead by then; the year itself has never been documented.

Jonas Roush (1763 Frederick VA – 1850 Mason VA)

Ahnentafel #482 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Jonas Roush was born in September 1763 at Holman’s Fort in Frederick County, Virginia — soon Shenandoah County — the youngest of the sons of John Adam and Susannah Roush. He was seventeen when his turn came in the Revolution, and the account of it is his own, sworn before a court in Mason County, Virginia on 6 November 1832.

He “entered the service as a substitute for his Brother Henry Roush who was drafted for a tour of three months in the summer of 1781,” served under Captain All in Colonel Bird’s regiment at Falmouth near Fredericksburg, and went out again on a second three-month tour. That second tour took him to the siege of Yorktown, and he was there on 19 October 1781 when Cornwallis’s army surrendered. He fell ill near Fredericksburg while escorting prisoners. The pension was allowed at twenty dollars a year from 4 March 1831 as claim S4785; the Daughters of the American Revolution list him as Patriot Ancestor A098985. His brother Henry’s own Revolutionary record is certified out of the same sheaf of paper.

He married Barbara Fox, of a Shenandoah County family settled near Forestville. Their daughter Regina, born 30 November 1788, married her cousin John Roush in 1808 — the marriage that doubles back through Alec’s tree, so that Jonas and his brother George both stand as ancestors. Other children were Abraham, John, Adam and Philip.

Like his brothers he came down the Ohio. He was living in Mason County, Virginia when he applied for his pension in 1832, and that same year he was named executor of his brother Daniel’s will, which left the residue of the estate “equally divided among the children of my brothers Jonas, George, Henry, Philip and Jacob.” His wife died in the 1830s, and the pension was transferred to Meigs County, Ohio in 1837; the family history says he spent his last years in his daughter Regina’s house in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township.

He died on 14 December 1850, aged eighty-seven, and lies in Gilmore Cemetery at Minersville, Meigs County, Ohio — the burying ground of that settlement, which the 1928 historians could only describe as “a family lot.” John and Regina were laid beside him in graves that were never marked. His own stone is still there, worn but standing, and two later Revolutionary War markers have been set by it: an upright government headstone, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT / VA TROOPS / REV WAR / 1763 1850,” and a flat bronze, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT VA REGIMENT / REVOLUTIONARY WAR.”

Barbara Fox (d. 1837)

Ahnentafel #483 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Barbara Fox married Jonas Roush, probably in Shenandoah County, Virginia in the 1780s. Her people were a Fox family settled near Forestville; the compiler of the 1928 Roush history admitted he knew nothing more of them than that the first federal census showed them to be a prominent family in the neighbourhood.

She is named as Jonas’s wife both by that family history and, independently, by the Daughters of the American Revolution’s record for him. Her daughter Regina, born 30 November 1788, married her cousin John Roush in 1808 and is Alec’s ancestor twice over. There were other children — Abraham, John, Adam, Philip — and the family moved down the Ohio with the rest of the Roush connection into Mason County.

Her husband’s pension file records only that his wife died in February 1837, without naming her; it was that year he gave up his own household and went to live with Regina across the river in Ohio. A stone in Gilmore Cemetery at Minersville, Meigs County, reads “Barbara, wife of Jonas Roush, died Oct. 21, 1836, aged 70 years.” It is very probably hers, though the stone gives no maiden name — Barbara, not Barbara Fox — and there is more than one Jonas Roush buried in that cemetery. What places this one in her generation is the arithmetic: a wife dead in 1836 at seventy was born about 1766, which is the patriot Jonas’s own generation and nobody else’s; the younger Jonases of the family were not yet born in 1766 and their wives were not seventy in 1836. The stone and the pension are four months apart — 21 October 1836 against February 1837 — and nobody has reconciled them. Either way she was dead before her husband crossed the river, and she lies in the same ground he would be buried in fourteen years later.

Henry Roush Sr. (1752 – 1831 Plants Cemetery)

Ahnentafel #500 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Henry Roush was born in 1752 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, the fourth son in the list of John Adam and Susannah Roush’s children; the family’s own historian introduces him as “one of the oldest of the children of John and Susannah Roush.”

He was twenty-three when the war began, and he was in it from the first season. A Virginia State Library archivist certified the record in 1926 from the rolls of companies paid off at Romney: Henry Rouse (Roush) served as a private in Captain John Tipton’s company for eighty-eight days and was paid £6-12s-0d on a receipt dated 25 October 1775. In the summer of 1781 he was drafted again and his younger brother Jonas went in his place — a fact that survives because Jonas swore to it in his own pension application fifty years later. The Daughters of the American Revolution hold a patriot record, A098977, for a Henry Roush born 1752 in Frederick County and died 26 October 1831 in Meigs County, Ohio, which matches him in birth year, death date and death place.

He married Dorothea, and they raised ten children in Shenandoah County near Solomon’s Church — Michael, Christina, Henry Jr., Elizabeth, Susannah, Anthony, Dorothy, Eva and Balser among them. Anthony, born 23 January 1792, is the son through whom this family descends. In 1793 Henry put his hand to the Solomonburg church deed, signing “HENRICH ROUSH” in German on the same page where his wife made her mark; it is why the association that later marked his grave called him the Church Builder.

He followed the Roush migration down the Ohio to Letart Falls in Meigs County, and he settled his children on land as he went: a deed to Henry Jr. in October 1805, seventy-five acres to Anthony for three hundred dollars in July 1812, and in June 1817 land to his son Balser and to his daughters Susanna Linscott and Catharine “for natural love and affection.”

He died on 26 October 1831, aged seventy-nine, and lies in Plants Cemetery above the river at Plants, Meigs County, Ohio. The little stone the 1928 compilers found — “Henry Roush died October 26, 1831, aged 79 years” — has been joined by a granite block carrying a plaque from his descendants: “HENRY ROUSH 1752–1831, ‘The Church Builder’, One of nine brothers who fought in the Revolutionary War, Erected by His Descendants.” A bronze tablet at its foot reads “HENRY ROUSH / PVT CAPT TIPTON’S CO / VA STATE TRP / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / 1752–1831.” The markers were dedicated by the Sons of the American Revolution at the Roush family’s annual meeting on 19 August 1933, on the same afternoon as his brother George’s, a few miles down the Ohio.

Dorothea — Dorothy in the records her neighbours wrote, Dolly in the family — was the wife of Henry Roush Sr. Her surname has never been recovered. The 1928 family historian wrote it once as “Dorothea Nease (?)”, with his own question mark, and once, a few hundred pages later, as plain “Dorothy (Nease) Roush”; the Daughters of the American Revolution’s record for her husband gives her as Dorothea Nehs. Nehs is how the Nease family spelled itself in German, and there were Neases standing as baptismal sponsors for her children and a John Nease serving as trustee beside Henry and Dorothea on a church deed in 1796. None of it is yet a record of her birth.

She married Henry about 1777 and bore ten children in Shenandoah County — Michael, Christina, Henry, Elizabeth, Susannah, Anthony, Dorothy, Eva, Balser. Anthony, born in January 1792, carries this line. In 1793 she witnessed the Solomonburg church deed: her husband signed his name in German, and she signed “DOROTHY ROUSH (her mark)” on the same page.

She went with the family down the Ohio to Letart Falls, and she is believed to have died about the same time as Henry, in 1831. She lies in Plants Cemetery in Meigs County beside him. When the 1928 compilers went looking they found a marker to his left whose “inscription … is now too far gone to be recovered but it seems undoubtedly to be his wife Dorothy.” Nearly a century on, a broken and wholly illegible stone still stands beside his monument, and Find a Grave records her plot simply as “Unmarked.”

The Rickard patriarch came out of Germany to Maryland, and from Maryland his family moved on to Mason County in what is now West Virginia. Four of his sons — Michael, Jacob, John and Joseph — made that second move together, and the Rickards of that county descend from them. Two daughters, Elizabeth and Susannah, married into the Roush family: Elizabeth to Anthony Roush about 1810, Susannah to Lewis Roush, while Michael Rickard married their sister-in-law Mary Roush in 1816 — three marriages binding the two families in both directions.

His name is lost. When the Roush family historian came to write the sentence in 1928, he printed a dash where it should have gone: “She was of the ——— Rickard family, who moved first to the state of Maryland from Germany, four brothers, Michael, Jacob, John and Joseph coming from there to Mason County, where their descent is mostly to be found today.” A blank is at least an admission that the man existed. The small burying ground at White Church in Mason County, where his son-in-law Anthony Roush lies under a stone with no epitaph, is the likeliest place a name still survives.

John Bell of Camden District (candidate) (~1710/30 – 1786 Camden SC)

Ahnentafel #512 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

This man is a candidate, not a proven ancestor, and the whole line above him waits on him.

If the abstract is right, he is the John Bell who sat on Bear Swamp in Duplin County, North Carolina before the Revolution — seated there by 1762, selling his plantation to his eldest son Frederick in 1765 for a hundred pounds and going on living on it, still “Sr” in 1773, and then gone from every North Carolina record. Frederick Bell is documented in Camden District, South Carolina by May 1777, so the son’s crossing is proved; the father’s is not. Somewhere after January 1773 a John Bell came four hundred miles south to the Black River country, and on 18 October 1786 he made a will there.

That will survives only as a printed abstract, and the abstract is careful in a way that matters. It claims three people in his own voice, by rank: my eldest son Frederick Bell, my eldest daughter Penelope Stuckey, wife Mary. Then it hands money to two more men — to John Bell, and … sterling to William Rasor Bell — with nothing attached but a bare to. Which of those two, if either, was his son is precisely what nobody knows, and a 2004 message-board condensation that said “son William Rasor Bell” was putting in a word the abstract does not contain.

What supports the candidacy is circumstantial and good. John Stuckey of Duplin married a Penelope. Lewis Stuckey’s 1792 deed says his property “descended to me from my Grandfather John Bell,” and by 1794 Lewis is “now of the County of Salem, South Carolina.” In 1790 there was exactly one Frederick Bell in the state. And Frederick Bell is the man who administered this estate.

What stands against it is that nobody can read the original. The Camden probate books for 1786 to 1791 burned with the Claremont courthouse in 1801; the printed abstract’s page 5 is copyright-walled on every free lane; and the surviving packet — South Carolina Archives L 49055, Apartment 6, Package 180, thirteen frames on film C9228 — has never been read by anyone on this project. The route through Penelope’s husband is closed for good: John Stuckey left no Duplin estate file because a sheriff had already stripped him, selling his plantation on the west side of Bear Swamp to satisfy a judgment of £1,658 11s 0½d.

And if he is who the Bertie County papers suggest — a son of Robert Bell of Chowan and Bertie, who died intestate in 1738 — then his own father could not write. Robert signed with a big interlocked R B, while John and his brothers all signed their names.
