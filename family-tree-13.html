Rebecca Moize (b. ~1800 NC – Sumter Co. AL 1850)

Ahnentafel #161 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Rebecca Moize was born about 1800 to 1805 in North Carolina, into a Moize family that had held the same few square miles of Orange County for generations and would still be in Lebanon Township there in 1880. Her parents are not known for certain; a family account names them Frederick Moize and Anne Ragland, and Frederick was buying land on Ellerbe Creek within three months of her wedding.

On 3 September 1817, at about seventeen, she married Moore Stephens in Orange County. Within seven years the couple were in Greene County, Alabama, and by the 1840s in Sumter County. She bore a large family — in 1850 seven of her children were still at home, from Benjamin W. at twenty-one down to William K. at six — and when Moore’s sister Esther Hitt died she took in Esther’s two orphaned children as well, Rebecca J. and Richard, both ten years old and listed in the household between her own.

She was still beside her husband at the next census, in Newton County, Mississippi, in 1860: “Rebeca Stevens, 55, born North Carolina” — forty-three years and two states from an Orange County wedding at seventeen. She was alive after that; when and where she died is not recorded.

Daniel Jones (~1806 NC – bef. 1869 Sumter Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #162 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Daniel Jones was born about 1806 in North Carolina, one of a Jones sibship that drifted to Alabama a few at a time. His brother Joel went out to Sumter County ahead of the rest and died there about 1843, and an undated power of attorney has the others acting over his estate — “Daniel Jones, Elijah Jones, Sarah… and Absalom Price” over the estate of “our dec’d Brother Joel Jones.” Daniel went too, and the Alabama births of his younger children put his own arrival in the middle 1830s.

He was in Sumter County and of full age by 1839, when he stood surety on a $2,200 Orphans’ Court guardianship bond. In 1850 he was farming at dwelling 1186 — Daniel Jones, forty-four, born North Carolina; Susan, thirty-five, born North Carolina; and Elizabeth 13, James 12, Margaret 10 and Mary 6, all born in Alabama. Two doors up the road lived Henry Stephens with Daniel’s eldest daughter Sarah Ann as his wife, and six houses beyond that Henry’s father Moore.

Moore Stephens was more than a neighbour. In March and April 1853 the two men, with Jesse Thomas, were jointly bound for $1,200 and then $2,800 on the administration of Thomas Fowler’s estate, and in January 1854 for $5,000 on the guardianship of Willis Thomas’s two small children. In October 1854, after Sarah’s death, Henry Stephens married Daniel’s second daughter Elizabeth.

He was dead by the last day of 1869, when the Sumter County probate court appointed Henry Stephens guardian of three Stephens boys, each described as “a minor heir of Daniel Jones deceased” — grandsons who could only inherit that way through a parent already dead, and the dead parent was Sarah. That he was her father is probable rather than proved; no document calls her his daughter.

Rev. William Hampton Truett (1806 Sumter SC – 1870 Henry Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #176 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Hampton Truett was born in 1806 in Sumter District, South Carolina, and became a preacher. His was the generation that carried the family out of the Carolina low country and onto the Alabama frontier — Darlington and Sumter districts to Henry County, in the south-eastern corner of Alabama, and from there west again.

On 10 October 1831, in Henry County, Wiley Peebles, a justice of the peace, married him to Alcey Wiggins and entered it as number 191 in the county book. She was a daughter of the Daniel Wiggins family, who had made the same journey out of Darlington. The Truetts and the Wigginses went on marrying each other in Alabama for another hundred years.

Their son John Wesley was born on 20 October 1833. A daughter, Winnie, married a Joshua Johnson, and the Johnsons’ son James Lafayette “Fate” Johnson was born in Clarke County in 1861.

He died in 1870 in Henry County, Alabama. For a preacher he left remarkably little behind him: no grave that anyone has found, no place in the register of the state’s Baptist ministers, and not a line in the history of the county where his widow and his son ended up. An Elige Truitt, who died in 1864 and is said to have been his brother, lies in the same Oak Grove graveyard at Chilton where his son John Wesley was buried fifty years later.

Alcey Wiggins (1813 – 1880)

Ahnentafel #177 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alcey Wiggins was born about 1813, almost certainly in Darlington District, South Carolina, and was very probably one of the two small girls in Daniel Wiggins’s house when the family was counted in Sumter District in 1820. Her parents, Daniel Wiggins and Susannah Truitt, had married at Darlington in 1800 and would end their lives in Henry County, Alabama.

She married at eighteen: on 10 October 1831, in Henry County, Wiley Peebles, a justice of the peace, married her to William Hampton Truett, a preacher. Their son John Wesley was born on 20 October 1833. A daughter, Winnie, married Joshua Johnson, and another daughter, Martha, became Martha Powers.

Widowed in 1870, she went to live with her son. In 1880 she was in John Wesley Truett’s household at Chilton in Clarke County, about sixty-seven years old — three generations of the family under one roof: the old preacher’s widow, her son the Confederate veteran who could not write, and her grandson William Curtis, Alec’s great-great-grandfather. She died that same year.

The Truetts and the Wigginses kept marrying each other. Her husband’s brother married her sister; Truett–Wiggins matches run on in the Alabama records into the twentieth century; and the neighbour who swore at Abbeville in 1870 that her daughter-in-law was an heir of the Ward estate was an Abijah Wiggins — very probably her own brother.

William H. Ward (~1806 SC – 1869 Henry Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #178 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William H. Ward was born about 1806 in South Carolina and came west to the south-eastern corner of Alabama, to Henry County. He married more than once: several of the children in his house had been born years before the licence the clerk issued on 11 October 1844 to him and Syntha Armstrong, whom Wm. R. Kelly, a justice of the peace, married to him on 18 December that year.

In 1850 he was farming with four hundred dollars of land — “W. H. WARD, 44, m, Farmer, $400, S.C.” — with Syntha, thirty-five and South Carolina-born like himself, and eight children, every one of them born in Alabama. By 1860 he was at Rowville, Beat 5, aged fifty-three, with Cyntha and the younger children.

On 28 October 1854 he put his name to the best document he left behind. His daughter Emily Caroline was seventeen and wanted to marry John Truett, and Alabama required a parent’s consent for a bride under twenty-one, so the clerk copied his words straight into the marriage book: “I give my free and unqualified consent that license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” It is signed Wm. H. Ward in a firm, practised, unmistakably literate hand — which is worth noticing, because the bridegroom and the bride both signed their own papers with an X for the rest of their lives.

He died on 6 February 1869, without a will. Six weeks later his eldest son John E. Ward and his son-in-law C. J. Hutto swore to the date and asked the Probate Court at Abbeville for letters, and on 18 September the court set a day to hear his widow’s claim for dower, with notice ordered published for thirty days in the Henry County Register.

He left about a thousand dollars of land and twenty-five hundred dollars of personal estate, and most of the personal estate was other men’s promises: a hundred and thirty-nine dollars and ninety-seven cents in specie, thirty dollars and thirty cents in greenbacks, and eleven notes running from twenty-four dollars to three hundred and thirty, several of them marked doubtfull. The rest of the inventory is a working farm, counted down to the last tool: seven weeding hoes at three dollars, three grubbing hoes at two, four plough stocks, two iron wedges, an ox ring and staple. The family’s lawyers were Oates & Cowan of Abbeville, and W. C. Oates himself signed the receipt for their hundred and five dollars.

His land — three hundred and twenty acres, the north-west quarter of Section 5 and the north-east quarter of Section 6 in Township 6, Range 27 — could not be divided, so it was sold at the courthouse door at Abbeville on 12 December 1870 to his own son William H. Ward, named for him, for twelve hundred and sixty dollars on twelve months’ credit; the sale was confirmed on 11 December 1872. The petition for it, sworn on 28 September 1870, names every heir: the widow Cynthia; Ann Elizabeth Hutto; John E. Ward; Mary Nichols; Martha Driggers; William H. Ward; and the minors George R., Eliza and Isaiah C. Ward, each “over fourteen and under twenty-one.” One name was left off — the daughter who had gone away west — and six weeks later a neighbour, Abijah Wiggins, put her back under oath: among the heirs of full age, he swore, was “the wife of JOHN TRUETT.”

His widow was still at Rowville in 1880. That absent daughter, an old woman in Florida fifty years on, remembered him dying in Clarke County rather than at home.

Syntha / Cyntha Ward (~1813 SC – 1880/1900)

Syntha Ward — the clerks also wrote Cyntha, Cynthia and Cintha — was born in South Carolina about 1813, and both her own parents came from North Carolina. In October 1844 the Henry County clerk issued a licence to William H. Ward and “Syntha Armstrong,” and a justice of the peace, Wm. R. Kelly, married them on 18 December 1844. She was about thirty, and Armstrong may well be a first husband’s name rather than her own; a Wade Armstrong had married a Cynthia Bright in the same county in 1837. Since the marriage came eight years after her husband’s daughter Emily Caroline was born, she was probably that girl’s stepmother rather than her mother, and who Caroline’s mother was is an open question.

She kept his house at Rowville, where there were at least ten children between his and theirs. In 1850 she is “Syntha, 35” on the Henry County schedule beside her husband and eight children; in 1860 she is “Cyntha, 47” at Rowville, Beat 5. Her youngest son was given the unusual name Caraway — or Conway; it is written both ways — which is exactly the way a mother’s maiden surname usually travels, and the best clue anyone has to hers.

Her husband died without a will in February 1869, and on the 18th of September the court appointed a day to hear her application for dower, with notice to be published in the Henry County Register and J. H. Simonton named guardian ad litem for the minor heirs. Her dower was eighty acres, measured off by five freeholders on 23 November 1869 and chosen, the writ insisted, by men not connected to any of the parties by blood or by marriage. She took property at the estate sale against her own share, and rented the estate land for 1870 jointly with her son William, signing both papers with a mark.

In 1880 she was still at Rowville, sixty-six, widowed, keeping house, entered as MOTHER in the household of her youngest son Isaiah. She died sometime between 1880 and 1900, before Alabama began registering deaths. In every paper of her husband’s estate — the dower petition, the writ, the commissioners’ return, the sale petition, the depositions, her own receipts — she is Mrs Cynthia Ward, widow of the deceased, and nothing else; whatever family name she was born with has not survived.

Colonel Gershom Tuttle III (1769 – 1823)

Ahnentafel #192 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Gershom Tuttle was born on 11 June 1769 at Bristol, Connecticut — the third Gershom Tuttle in a row in this family, and almost certainly the son of the second, the Revolutionary conductor of teams, though that one link has never been put beyond doubt.

He married Pamelia Strong Clark at Farmington on 8 July 1788, when he was nineteen. In 1791 he and Joseph Tuttle, then of Wardsborough in Vermont, bought a fifty-acre lot at Wilmington in that state; by the mid-nineties the family had moved west to Paris, in Oneida County, New York, where he was the junior of two Gershom Tuttles in one small town. About 1800 he went north again into the new Black River country and opened a farm of 275 acres some five miles from the site of Watertown, in the town of Rutland.

He was there from the beginning. When Rutland held its first town meeting in March 1803, in David Coffeen’s house, he was one of the three men chosen highway commissioner — the same meeting that set a ten-dollar bounty on wolves. In 1806, with Seth Bailey, he built the first grist mill on that stretch of the Black River. He was a charter member of the Masonic lodge warranted at Watertown on 17 June 1806, entered on its roll as “Gershom Tuttle, Jr.” From 1808 he was one of the county’s commissioners for loaning the moneys of the state, and in 1810 he took five shares at ten dollars in the Black River Navigation Company. He traded, made potash, and kept a tavern on the great highway to the lake country.

The tavern is what his neighbours remembered. About 1803 a hundred men of Rutland and Watertown assembled there, by appointment, to settle a bet about which town could out-wrestle the other, the winners promised all they could eat and drink. The first Congregational church of Watertown, organised in 1803, met in his ball-room when it had nowhere else, and he was one of its original members. In June 1809 the county court fined him and Jabez Foster eight dollars each for not turning up as grand jurors. His family’s book remembered him as “a man of excellent character, genial temper, very fond of a joke, a capital story teller,” and that is the man everything else about him shows.

He was commissioned second major of the Jefferson County militia on 5 April 1805 and, on 4 April 1808, lieutenant-colonel of the 76th Regiment — the regiment the county histories simply call Tuttle’s. In the War of 1812 the Watertown militia belonged to it and were turned out en masse whenever invasion threatened; at the Battle of Sackets Harbor on 29 May 1813 they stood “in the edge of the woods, back of the clearing,” under Colonel Gershom Tuttle. He resigned his commission in 1814. (A second and quite unrelated Colonel Gershom Tuttle, of Massachusetts and of the regular army, died at that same post during the war and lies under the monument there.)

In 1816 he took the family on to Vigo County, Indiana, and settled in Otter Creek Township; a federal land clerk entered him in November 1817 first as “Gershom Tuttle, Jeff’n Co N.Y.” and, three weeks later, as “Gershom Tuttle, Fort Harrison.” He became a county commissioner in 1820. The last summer of his life a sheriff was advertising a half-quarter section, “the property of Gersham and Chester Tuttle,” for sale on execution at the suit of John F. King, and his patent for eighty acres came through under James Monroe’s hand on 15 July 1823, seven weeks before he died. On 29 August he made a will leaving everything to Pamelia for life and naming no child at all.

He died on the Friday night of 5 September 1823 in Otter Creek Township, in the fifty-fourth year of his age. Terre Haute’s first newspaper reported that “on Sunday last his remains were consigned to the earth, accompanied with Masonic honors by the Terre Haute Lodge of which he was a member” — which is how a Watertown lodge roll of 1806 came to matter eight hundred miles away. The estate turned out to be insolvent and was still in court nine years later. His grave has never been located.
