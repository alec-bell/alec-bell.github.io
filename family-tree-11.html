Pamelia Strong Clark (1770 Farmington – 1851)

Ahnentafel #193 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Pamelia Strong Clark was born on 4 November 1770 at Farmington, Connecticut. Clark was her surname; Strong was a middle name, and it descended four more generations — Clark Strong Tuttle, Dennis Clark, Richard Clark — long after anyone remembered where it came from. On 8 July 1788, aged seventeen, she married Gershom Tuttle of Bristol.

She spent the next twenty-eight years moving west with him: Wilmington in Vermont, Paris in Oneida County, and about 1800 the new Black River country of Jefferson County, New York, where her husband farmed 275 acres, built a mill and kept the tavern that gave the neighbourhood its ball-room. Her son Chester was born at Sackets Harbor in 1796; Cynthia at Watertown in 1803; Amanda in 1810; Clark Strong in April 1811; Asa in 1813. In 1816 the family went on to Vigo County, Indiana.

Her husband died on 5 September 1823, leaving a will that gave her everything for life, with power to provide “for any of our children for whom she may think necessary to provide.” Three weeks later she gave bond as executrix, with Abraham and Henry Markle as her securities, and the inventory of the whole estate came to a little over six hundred dollars. It was insolvent. Her co-executor Nathaniel Huntington was out of the sheriff's reach by 1829 and dead by 1831.

What followed is the hardest stretch of any life on this chart. The probate court cited her in January 1829, again in November 1829, and summoned her again on 2 November 1830 to show cause “why she has not made a final settlement of the estate of Gursham Tuttle deceased, of which she is the executrix.” In March 1831 she petitioned to be let out of it, through a solicitor, in words that are still painful to read: the estate's business was “long since closed,” but “the papers of said Estate were left in the possession of Nathaniel Huntington Esq. who is dead, and they are now in so confused and mutilated a situation that no accurate statement of the condition of the Estate could be made from them.” On 29 December 1831 the court issued a writ of attachment for her body for contempt. The sheriff executed it on 2 February 1832 and charged eighty-three and a half cents in fees. Bail was set at ten dollars.

She outlived her husband by twenty-eight years and followed one of her children ninety miles south into the hills of Brown County. She died on 22 April 1851, and her stone at Southview Cemetery, Nashville, reads: In Memory of PAMELIA TUTTLE DIED April 22 1851 Aged 80 Y's, 5 M's, 18 D's. Count the age back and it lands, to the day, on 4 November 1770 — her own stone confirming the birthday the family book recorded.

According to the Tuttle family genealogy of 1883, her brother was an officer of the United States Army who died while in charge of the federal arsenal at Troy, New York.

Joseph Lamb (~1782 NC – 1855 Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #216 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lamb was born in North Carolina about 1782 — compiled records give 25 March 1782, which squares with the county historian’s note that he died in 1855 “at the age of 73.” The family was living in Randolph County, about sixteen miles north of Asheboro, in 1814, when his son Elias was born there. He married Lydia Adamson — the 1806 bond is made out for “Lidya Addison,” with her brother Samuel Adamson standing bondsman — and they were members of the Society of Friends. Nine children were born to them, of whom seven grew up: Ezekiel, Esther, Smith, Elias, Mournen, Joseph and Ithamar.

In April 1829 he took the family out of North Carolina for Indiana — the ordinary Quaker departure from the slaveholding South — and settled in Clay Township, Wayne County, on land adjoining Perry Township that a widow, Eve Shoemaker, had first taken up in 1821. He farmed there for the rest of his life, and his sons and his wife’s brother settled around him: when the Clay Township school section was sold in 1832 the buyers included Samuel Adamson, and his son Ithamar was living on it forty years later.

Lydia died first — in 1844 by his son Elias’s account, though other records say 1837. Joseph died on 29 July 1855, aged seventy-three, and lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery in Wayne County under a small marble stone, weathered but still readable: JOSEPH LAMB DIED 29th Day of the July of year 1855.

Lydia Adamson married Joseph Lamb in North Carolina in November 1806; the marriage bond was made out for “Lidya Addison,” and her brother Samuel Adamson stood bondsman — he later married Joseph’s sister Naomi, so two Adamsons married two Lambs. In April 1829 she came north with her husband and children to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township; her brother Samuel was one of the buyers when the township school section was sold in 1832. She was the only one of the four Lambs and Bishes of her generation on this chart not to be given a stone, or at least not one that survives.

John Bish was of Fairfield County, Ohio — his daughter Susannah was born there in November 1818, “about 18 miles east of Columbus” — and a Find a Grave memorial puts his own birth in Shenandoah County, Virginia, on 20 January 1788, and his marriage to Ann Elizabeth in Rockingham County, Virginia, in 1810. In 1836 he brought the family to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township. He lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery, Row 9 of the north section, in the same burying ground as Joseph Lamb. The inscription has almost gone: the name is a shadow, but “AUG. 20, 1866” and “AGED” can still be made out.

John Roush (1785 – 1861 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #240 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Roush was born on 22 November 1785 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, a son of George Roush and Catharine Zerkel, and was baptised into the Lutheran faith on 5 January 1786, with another John Roush and his wife Susannah standing as witnesses.

On 18 May 1808 he married his own cousin, Regina Roush, the daughter of Jonas Roush. The county's oldest marriage book records it on its first page, in the family's older spelling: “John Rausch to Regenna Rausch, May 18, 1808,” by Francis Watkins, Justice of the Peace. Eleven children followed, and the New Haven Lutheran church register is said to have carried the birth and baptism of every one of them.

Benjamin, the fifth, was born at Graham Station on the Virginia side in August 1816; Hannah was born in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, in May 1820. Between those two births John and Regina crossed the river for good, into the few square miles where the Roush and Nease families who later intermarried were founding neighbours - the same ground where their great-great-grandson John G. Roush would be born more than fifty years on. He was known in the family as “Blackbird John.”

He could not write. Every deed he made he signed “John his + mark Roush.” In the twenty months between July 1848 and March 1850 he settled land on four Roush men - Abel, James, John Junior and Benjamin. On 9 August 1849 he cut the east strip of Section 25, Town 3, Range 12 in two on a single day before a single justice and two witnesses: seventy acres running north from the south-east corner to John Roush Junior, and sixty-nine acres and twenty poles running south from the north-east corner to Benjamin, each for five hundred dollars. Not one of those deeds says father, or son, or heir. No wife joined in any of them to release her dower, and there was no Regina in his house when the census taker came in 1850.

He was sixty-six then, living four doors from Benjamin in Sutton Township. He died on 21 January 1861. Regina's father Jonas had spent his last years in her home and lies in the same ground. For most of a century John and Regina's graves were unmarked - the 1928 family history says the two graves just north of Jonas's were pointed out to the compilers by people who knew, “altho we have had them definitely pointed out to us.” Today a granite stone at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville carries the name ROUSH, and underneath, JOHN 1785 - 1861 and REGINA 1788 - 18. Whoever ordered it still could not supply her death year, and the cutter left the last two figures off.

Regina Roush (b. 1788)

Ahnentafel #241 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Regina Roush was born on 30 November 1788, the second child of Jonas Roush - a private of the Virginia line who was at the siege of Yorktown and saw Cornwallis surrender - and his wife Barbara Fox.

On 18 May 1808, at nineteen, she married her cousin John Roush before Francis Watkins, a justice of the peace, who entered the couple on the first page of the county's oldest marriage book as “John Rausch to Regenna Rausch.” A Roush had married a Roush: both of them grandchildren of the same Shenandoah Valley family. Eleven children were born to them. Benjamin, the fifth, arrived at Graham Station in August 1816; Hannah, in May 1820, was born after the move across the Ohio into the Nease Settlement.

Her father came to live with her. The 1928 family history says Jonas “spent his last years in her home in the Nease Settlement near where he lies buried,” and his own Revolutionary pension file, written down from his sworn statement and quite independent of the book, records that he removed to Meigs County, Ohio in June 1837 to live with a daughter. He died there on 14 December 1850. The Daughters of the American Revolution have twice approved lineages that run through “child REGINA - spouse JOHN ROUSH.”

When she died is not known and was not known in 1928, when the compilers of the family book wrote plainly that it “has not been ascertained.” There are indications. She was not in her husband's household in 1850, and in none of the five deeds he made in 1848-50 did a wife join to release her dower, which Ohio law of the day would normally have required of a married man - so she had very probably died before the summer of 1848.

She lies at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville, beside her husband and near her father. The granite over the two of them reads JOHN 1785 - 1861 and REGINA 1788 - 18, and there the inscription stops.

David Altman (~1788 Pennsylvania – 1850/70)

George Duckworth (Bury district, Lancashire)

George Duckworth was a hatter in the Bury district of Lancashire. He is known from one line of one page: on 21 January 1839, when his son James married Ann Collins at the parish church of St Mary, Bury, the curate entered the groom's father as “George Duckworth” and his rank or profession, in the column kept for it, as “Hatter.”

Hatting was Bury's trade beside cotton and coal, and it was a skilled one; his son went down the pit instead, was entered in the same register as a collier, and signed the book with a mark. A Richard Duckworth stood as witness that day and signed with a mark too - very likely George's brother or his other son. Whether George lived to see his son and daughter-in-law take their children across the Atlantic about 1850 is not known. Nothing else of his life has been traced.

Adam Collins lived at Freetown, in Bury, Lancashire. His daughter Ann was under twenty-one when she married James Duckworth, a collier, at St Mary's church on 21 January 1839, and because she was a minor her father's consent was required - which is the only reason his name is in the register at all. His own occupation was written in the last column of the page; the word is too far gone to read with confidence, and it has been left blank rather than guessed at.

Anthony Roush (b. 1792 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #250 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Anthony Roush was born on 23 January 1792 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, near Solomon's Church, and was baptised there on 18 March 1792 with Michael Levermore standing sponsor. He was the seventh child of Henry Roush and his wife Dorothy - a Henry who, at twenty-three, had served eighty-eight days as a private in Captain John Tipton's company and been paid off at Romney in October 1775, and for whom his younger brother Jonas later stood substitute.

The family came down into the Ohio Valley, and Anthony with them. On 11 July 1812, when he was twenty, his father deeded him seventy-five acres for three hundred dollars. He married first Elizabeth Rickard, described in the family record as “a sister to the Rickard brothers,” about 1810, and after her a second wife, Catherine Hoffman. His children were set down in a numbered sequence, and Nancy, born on 16 February 1832, was the eleventh of them; she married George Nease in 1848 and is the ancestor of this line.

He probably fell heir to a share of his father's portion of the six-thousand-acre estate in Graham District, Mason County - land that had been fought over in the courts since a petition of 1806.

He lies, the family book says, in the Rickard cemetery in the White Church community of Mason County, under a small stone with no epitaph. No photograph of it has been found and he has no memorial on Find a Grave.
