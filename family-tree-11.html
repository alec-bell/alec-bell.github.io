Moore Stephens (b. ~1796 NC – Sumter Co. AL 1850)

Ahnentafel #160 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Moore Stephens was born about 1796 in North Carolina, one of at least ten children of a Stephens household in Orange County whose father’s name has not survived. On 3 September 1817 he took out a marriage bond in Orange County to marry Rebecca Moize. His brother Charles stood bondsman and Joseph A. Woods witnessed it; both men signed with a mark, so Moore could not write his own name.

Within a few years the whole family picked up and went to Alabama — they were in Greene County by 1824 — and Moore worked his way west again into Sumter County. On 12 October 1846 the Greene County Orphans’ Court divided the estate of Mary Thorn among ten heirs at law in equal shares, and he was one of them, due $107.50 and receipting $95. The nine others are his brothers and sisters: William, Lewis, Charles and James Stephens, Nancy Barnett, Mary Barnett, Lucinda Ball, Elizabeth Beavers and Esther Hitt. He bought nothing at the sale, because by then he no longer lived in the county; the clerk’s cost bill files him under “Heirs of Sumter Co., Ala.”

The 1850 census shows what his house actually held. At dwelling 1190 in Sumter County were Moore, fifty-four, Rebecca, fifty, and the children Benjamin W. 21, James M. 18, John F. 16, Mary A. R. 13, Charles L. 12, Easter C. 10 and William K. 6 — and two more, Rebecca J. and Richard Hitt, both ten, the orphans of his dead sister Esther, being raised alongside his own. He had named a daughter Easter for that sister. His son Henry farmed six houses up the road. The slave schedule for the same county that year records Moore Stephens holding one enslaved person, a Black girl of about seven, born around 1843, whom the schedule was designed not to name.

His marriage bond survives in the North Carolina archives: on 3 September 1817 he and Charles Stephens bound themselves to the Governor in five hundred pounds that there was no lawful cause to obstruct his marriage to Rebecca Moize. Both of them signed with a mark.

In 1853 and 1854 he stood surety three times in the county court alongside his neighbour Daniel Jones — twice on the administration of Thomas Fowler’s estate, for $1,200 and $2,800, and once on a $5,000 guardianship of Willis Thomas’s small children. By 1860 he had moved on again: the census of Newton County, Mississippi, lists “Moore Stevens, 66, born North Carolina,” with Rebeca 55 and five children still at home. Henry brought his family to the same county. When Moore died is not known. The Newton County courthouse burned in September 1876, “supposed to have been of incendiary origin,” and the probate records that would have dated his death burned with it.

Rebecca Moize (b. ~1800 NC – Sumter Co. AL 1850)

Ahnentafel #161 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Rebecca Moize was born about 1800 to 1805 in North Carolina, into a Moize family that had held the same few square miles of Orange County for generations and would still be in Lebanon Township there in 1880. Her own parents have never been identified; the marriage bond that records her wedding does not name a father, because the bondsman carried the groom’s surname rather than hers.

On 3 September 1817, at about seventeen, she married Moore Stephens in Orange County. Within seven years the couple were in Greene County, Alabama, and by the 1840s in Sumter County. She bore a large family — the 1850 census of Sumter County lists seven of her children still at home, from Benjamin W. at twenty-one down to William K. at six — and when Moore’s sister Esther Hitt died she took in Esther’s two orphaned children as well, Rebecca J. and Richard, both ten years old and sitting in the household list between her own.

She was still beside her husband at the next census. In 1860 the enumerator of Newton County, Mississippi, wrote down “Rebeca Stevens, 55, born North Carolina” in Moore’s household — forty-three years and two states after an Orange County wedding at seventeen. She was alive after 1860; when and where she died is not recorded.

Daniel Jones (~1806 NC – bef. 1869 Sumter Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #162 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Daniel Jones was born about 1806 in North Carolina and was almost certainly the father of Sarah Ann Jones, who married Henry H. Stephens. He belonged to a North Carolina Jones sibling set — an undated power of attorney names “Daniel Jones, Elijah Jones, Sarah… and Absalom Price” acting over the estate of “our dec’d Brother Joel Jones” of Sumter County, Alabama. Joel had gone out to Alabama ahead of them and died there about 1843; Daniel went too.

He was in Sumter County and of full age by 1839, when he stood surety on a $2,200 Orphans’ Court guardianship bond. The 1850 census finds him at dwelling 1186 — Daniel Jones, forty-four, born North Carolina; Susan, thirty-five, born North Carolina; and Elizabeth 13, James 12, Margaret 10 and Mary 6, all born in Alabama. The Alabama births of the younger children put the family’s arrival in the state in the middle 1830s. Two doors up the road lived Henry Stephens with Daniel’s eldest daughter as his wife, and six houses beyond that Henry’s father Moore.

Moore Stephens was more than a neighbour. In March and April 1853 the two men, with Jesse Thomas, were jointly bound for $1,200 and then $2,800 on the administration of Thomas Fowler’s estate, and in January 1854 for $5,000 on the guardianship of Willis Thomas’s two small children. In October 1854, after Sarah’s death, Henry Stephens married Daniel’s second daughter Elizabeth.

He was dead by the last day of 1869, when the Sumter County probate court appointed Henry Stephens guardian of three Stephens boys, each described as “a minor heir of Daniel Jones deceased.” Grandsons can only inherit that way through a parent who has already died, and the dead parent is Sarah. The identification of Daniel as her father rests on that convergence rather than on any document that calls her his daughter, and it is recorded here as probable, not proved.

Rev. William Hampton Truett (1806 Sumter SC – 1870 Henry Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #176 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Hampton Truett was born in 1806 in Sumter District, South Carolina, and became a preacher. His was the generation that carried the family out of the Carolina low country and onto the Alabama frontier — Darlington and Sumter districts to Henry County in the south-eastern corner of Alabama, and from there west again.

He married Alcey Wiggins, a daughter of the Daniel Wiggins family who had made the same journey from Darlington. The family record of that marriage gives 10 October 1831 at Benton County, Alabama, and that cannot be right: Benton County was not created until 18 December 1832, out of Creek land ceded that March. Either the date is right and the place is not, or the county name was written back onto an older event by a later hand.

Their son John Wesley was born on 20 October 1833, and a daughter Winnie married a Joshua Johnson; their grandson, James Lafayette “Fate” Johnson, was born in Clarke County in 1861. The Truetts and the Wigginses went on marrying each other in Alabama for another hundred years.

He married Alcy Wiggins on 10 October 1831 in Henry County, Alabama; Wiley Peebles, justice of the peace, performed it and entered it as number 191 in the county book.

He died in 1870 in Henry County, Alabama. He is a hard man to find in print. Holcombe's register of Alabama Baptist ministers of 1803–1840 does not carry his name, and the 1882 history of Clarke County — where his widow and son ended up — names only two Truetts in seven hundred and eighty pages, neither of them a preacher. No grave has been found. Truitt, 1814–1864, lies in the same Oak Grove Cemetery at Chilton where his son John Wesley is buried, and the family trees make him William Hampton's brother; no record has yet been read that proves it.

Alcey Wiggins (1813 – 1880)

Ahnentafel #177 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alcey Wiggins was born about 1813, almost certainly in Darlington District, South Carolina. The 1820 census of Sumter District puts Daniel Wiggins there with two girls under ten in the house, and she fits one of them; Daniel and his wife Susannah Truitt had married at Darlington in 1800 and would end their lives in Henry County, Alabama.

She married William Hampton Truett, a preacher, about 1831 or 1832 — the family's record of the date and the family's record of the place cannot both be right, because the Alabama county it names did not yet exist. Their son John Wesley was born on 20 October 1833. A daughter, Winnie, married Joshua Johnson, and there was a daughter Martha who became Martha Powers.

Widowed in 1870, she went to live with her son. The 1880 census of Chilton in Clarke County finds her at about sixty-seven in John Wesley Truett's household — one enumerator's page holding three generations of the family at once: the old preacher's widow, her son the Confederate veteran who could not write, and her grandson William Curtis, Alec's great-great-grandfather. She died that same year.

She married at eighteen: Alcy Wiggins and William Hampton Truett, 10 October 1831, before Wiley Peebles, justice of the peace in Henry County.

The Truetts and the Wigginses kept marrying each other. Her own husband's brother married her sister; Truett×Wiggins marriages run on in the Alabama records into the twentieth century; and the man who swore the deposition that named her daughter-in-law among the heirs of the Ward estate at Abbeville in 1870 was an Abijah Wiggins — very probably her own brother.

Evidence

Living in her son John Wesley’s household in the 1880 Clarke County census, aged about 67 — one enumerator’s page tying Gens 6, 7 and 8 together.

William H. Ward (~1806 SC – 1869 Henry Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #178 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William H. Ward was born about 1806 in South Carolina and came west to the south-eastern corner of Alabama. The 1850 census of Henry County finds him at family 646 — “W. H. WARD, 44, m, Farmer, $400, S.C.” — with his wife Syntha, thirty-five and also South Carolina-born, and eight children, all of them born in Alabama. He was invisible to the indexers for a century and a half because the enumerator wrote the dwelling and family numbers one line low, onto his wife's line, so the machines made Syntha the head of the house and pushed him up into somebody else's household.

On 28 October 1854 he put his name to one of the better documents in this family's record. His daughter Emily Caroline was seventeen and wanted to marry John Truett, and Alabama required a parent's consent for a bride under twenty-one. The clerk copied his words straight into the marriage book: “I give my free and unqualified consent that license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” It is signed Wm. H. Ward in a firm, practised, unmistakably literate hand — which is worth noticing, because the bridegroom and the bride both signed their own papers with an X for the rest of their lives.

By 1860 he was at Rowville, Beat 5, aged fifty-three, with Cyntha and the younger children. Then he died, intestate, somewhere between 1860 and September 1869 — and probably in 1868 or 1869, because by 18 September 1869 his administrators, his son-in-law C. J. Hutto and his eldest son John E. Ward, were already in the Probate Court of Henry County applying for the widow's dower, with notice ordered to be published for thirty days in the Henry County Register.

He died on 6 February 1869 -- his son and his son-in-law swore to the date six weeks later when they asked the Probate Court for letters. He left about a thousand dollars of land and twenty-five hundred dollars of personal estate, most of it other men's promises: eleven notes, from twenty-four dollars to three hundred and thirty, several of them marked 'doubtfull'. The family's lawyers were Oates & Cowan of Abbeville, and the receipt for their hundred and five dollars is signed by W. C. Oates himself.

His land could not be divided, so it was sold. The petition of 28 September 1870 names every heir: the widow Cynthia; Ann Elizabeth Hutto; John E. Ward; Mary Nichols; Martha Driggers; William H. Ward, named for his father; and the minors George R., Eliza and Isaiah C. Ward, each “over fourteen and under twenty-one.” One name was left off — the daughter who had gone away west — and six weeks later a neighbour, Abijah Wiggins, put her back in under oath: among the heirs of full age, he swore, was “the wife of JOHN TRUETT.” His daughter said in 1920 that he had died in Clarke County; his widow was still at Rowville in 1880. Both are recorded, neither is proved.

Syntha / Cyntha Ward (~1813 SC – 1880/1900)

Ahnentafel #179 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Syntha Ward — the clerks also wrote Cyntha, Cynthia and Cintha — was born in South Carolina about 1813, and both of her own parents were born in North Carolina. She married William H. Ward about 1833 or 1834, probably in South Carolina, since no Alabama marriage of a Ward to a Cynthia is indexed in those years. They had at least ten children.

She is on the 1850 schedule of Henry County, Alabama as “Syntha, 35”, next to her husband and eight children, and on the 1860 schedule at Rowville Beat 5 as “Cyntha, 47”. Her youngest son was given the unusual name Caraway — or Conway; the records read it both ways — which is exactly the way a mother's maiden surname usually travels, and it is the best clue anyone has to hers.

Her husband died intestate before September 1869, and on the 18th of that month the court appointed a day to hear the application for her dower, with notice to be published in the Henry County Register and J. H. Simonton named guardian ad litem for the minor heirs. When the estate's land was sold the following year she is “Cynthia Ward, widow of decedent”; when the neighbour Abijah Wiggins swore his deposition at Abbeville in November 1870 she is “Mrs Cyntha Ward the widow of deceased.” In 1880 she was still at Rowville, sixty-six, widowed, keeping house, entered as MOTHER in the household of her youngest son Isaiah. She does not appear in the 1900 census of Alabama, so she died in those twenty years — before the state began registering deaths, which is why there is no certificate for her.

Her dower was eighty acres, measured off by five freeholders on 23 November 1869 and chosen, the writ said, so that none of them was connected to any of the parties by blood or by marriage. She signed everything with a mark, and the clerks spelled her Cynthia, Cyntha and Syntha on the same file.

Four documents in her husband's estate name her, and every one of them calls her only Mrs Cynthia Ward, widow of the deceased. Her maiden name has not survived in anything this project has read.
