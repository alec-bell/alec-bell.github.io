William Payne (~1791/93 Virginia – 1860/78)

Ahnentafel #74 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

William Payne was born in Virginia about 1791–93, and by the 1840s he was farming in Lauderdale County, Mississippi. In 1850, at fifty-seven, he headed a household of eleven: his wife Mary, thirty, born in North Carolina; four boys born in North Carolina who carried the Eakens surname and were orphans in his care — John W., sixteen, James O., fourteen, Lewis T., twelve, and George W., ten; four younger children born in Mississippi, the eldest of them Sarah H., aged eight; and an eighteen-year-old named Martha Moore.

The arrangement was formal as well as domestic. He was guardian of the Eakens boys, his wife Mary was administratrix on the Eakens estate, and a William Eakens served as administrator alongside her. He was, in short, a Virginian in Mississippi raising another family’s orphaned sons beside his own young children — and in May 1861 a daughter of that house, Sarah Hudson Payne, married Joel W. Eakins, who bore the same surname as the boys he had raised.

He married Mary Eakins, the widow of Andrew Eakin, at Lauderdale on 28 May 1840, two years before Sarah was born, and became guardian of her four Eakin sons; for nine years after the wedding the court went on calling her by either name as she wound up her first husband’s estate. In June 1851 the court granted him guardianship of the property of two of his younger children, Orrigon and Joel, whom it called “his minor children”. Whether Sarah was his own daughter has not been formally proved, and so his place at the head of this line is still graded a likelihood rather than a certainty.

Mary, whose maiden name is not known, was born in North Carolina between about 1812 and 1820 — the census-takers could not agree on her age. She was first the wife of Andrew Eakin, and after his death she took out letters on his estate in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, as its administratrix. Four boys born in North Carolina were his heirs: John William, James Owen, Lewis T. and George W. Eakin.

On 28 May 1840, as Mary Eakins, she married William P. Payne at Lauderdale; the licence had been taken out three days before, and a member of the county Board of Police married them. The Eakin boys grew up in the Payne house with William as their guardian, and in May 1842, two years into the marriage, Sarah was born — the eldest of the four children born there in Mississippi, with Ann, Origon and Joel after her.

For nine years the court went on calling her by both names as it worked through her first husband’s affairs: “Mrs Mary Payne” in April 1849, “Mrs Mary Eakin” a month later, and in August 1849, when she handed what remained of Andrew Eakin’s estate to her husband as the boys’ guardian and was discharged, “Mrs Mary Payne late Mary Eakin”. In 1860 the family was at Markwell, in Beat 2. William died before 1880, and Mary, a widow, was then living in the household of her son Joel.

Joseph Tolbert Swain was born on 24 May 1838 in Wilcox County, Alabama. By his early twenties he had moved west into Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where on 6 February 1860 he married Nancy E. Jane Phillips; their first child, Joseph Lee, was born there that December.

He enlisted on 8 March 1862 at Lauderdale Station, a private in Company C of the regiment that ended the war as the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry, bringing his own horse. The army described him then: twenty-four, five feet eight, dark complexion, black eyes, black hair, a farmer born in Wilcox County, Alabama. Within five months he was discharged with consumption — “phthisis pulmonalis” — and paid off at Columbus in August 1862, having already signed his claim to the back pay over to another man. In September 1863 he enlisted again, at Enterprise; he was sick in the recruiting camp in the summer of 1864, and in October 1864 he was discharged by order of General Hood. A portrait of him survives — a man in a dark coat and a hard round hat, with a heavy beard down his chest and a direct, unsmiling stare.

When the war was over he did not wait. His two eldest children were born in Mississippi, in 1861 and 1864; every child from 1866 onward was born in Alabama. He moved his wife and two small children across the state line into Sumter County in the first months after the surrender, when he was about twenty-seven, and settled at Cuba. By 1880 the house there held him and Nancy, both forty-two, with seven children at home, from Joseph L. at nineteen down to John M. at six.

On 3 July 1875 he walked into the registrar's office at Cuba, in Precinct No. 18 of Sumter County, and took the registration oath ahead of that August's referendum on a new state constitution. The printed oath he swore made him promise that he would “never countenance or aid in the secession of this State from the United States” and that he “accept[ed] the civil and political equality of all men” — ten years after he had ridden with the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry. The registrar W. H. Lewis wrote his name for him and he made his mark: he could not write. Against his name the registrar put down his living in a word: farmer, near Cuba. Two lines below him on the same page, registering the same day, was B. A. Simmons, the miller and farmer whose daughters his son Joseph Lee would marry nine and ten years later.

Two more babies, Jesse and Hassiel, were born and died in 1876–79. Nine children in all are known, and four of them were still living in 1940: Joseph Lee, John M. Adeline Simmons (1829 AL – 1900 Kewanee MS)

Ahnentafel #79 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Adeline was born on 7 October 1829, probably in Walton County, Florida, though in later life she was put down as Alabama-born as often as Florida-born. Her maiden name was most likely Peavy, and her father Levi Daniel Peavy. She married Ben Simmons on 11 May 1847, at seventeen, and bore nine or ten children over the next twenty years, the last about 1867 when she was thirty-seven. At least two did not live out infancy.

She kept house at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama, through the war and after it, with a few years in Mississippi in the early 1850s that show up only in where her middle children were born. In 1870 she was “Adline,” forty-one, with six children at home; in 1880 she still had a sixteen-year-old daughter under the roof, written down as “Dellon.” It is through one of her daughters that Alec's line runs, and which one is still unsettled, so her place in it is probable rather than certain.

A sepia photograph of her survives from about 1875: a woman seated in a dark dress with a lace collar and a tasselled ribbon at her throat, hands still, looking straight out.

Her husband died in April 1894. She outlived him by six years and died at Kewanee, in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, at seventy. Her tall marble headstone at Salem Baptist, beside his, was cut with a weeping-willow panel: IN MEMORY OF ADELINE, WIFE OF B. A. SIMMONS, BORN OCT. 7, 1829, DIED SEPT. 25, 1900, AGED 70 YRS. 11 MOS. 16 DAYS.
