Emily Caroline Ward (1836 Henry AL – 1922 Levy FL)

Ahnentafel #89 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emily Caroline Ward was born on Christmas Day 1836 at Abbeville in Henry County, Alabama, the second daughter of William H. Ward, a South Carolina-born farmer with four hundred dollars of land at Rowville. Who her mother was is not known: the Syntha who kept her father’s house married him only in December 1844, eight years after Caroline was born, and was very probably her stepmother. In 1850 she was twelve, one of eight children at home.

She married at seventeen. Because she was under twenty-one her father had to consent, and the clerk copied his words into the Henry County marriage book above the licence: “I give my free and unqualified consent that license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” The licence issued on 28 October 1854, and she and John Wesley Truett married five days later. Neither bride nor groom could write.

They moved west to Clarke County, and the children came — Sarah Ann in 1858, William Curtis in 1867, George in 1872, John Wesley in 1875, Elisha Dozier in 1877, Carrie in 1878. In 1880 she was “Emlie C.” at Chilton, keeping house for her husband, four children and her mother-in-law Alcey. Sarah married William Green Geiger on 12 December 1878, a match that would decide where Caroline spent her last years.

When her father’s Henry County estate was broken up in 1870 she was so far away that the administrators left her off the petition altogether; six weeks later a neighbour, Abijah Wiggins, swore her back into it — among the heirs of full age, he said, was “the wife of JOHN TRUETT.” Her husband went back to Abbeville in person on 26 December 1871 and collected three hundred dollars of her share.

She was widowed on 17 April 1914 and applied for her Confederate widow’s pension three months later. In April 1915, at Coffeeville, she swore that she had been born at Abbeville on the 25th day of December 1836, and signed with her mark. Then, in 1917, at eighty, she left Alabama for good: Sarah and William Green Geiger moved the household to Hardeetown in Levy County, Florida, and the old woman went with them. On 13 December 1920 she put her X on an Alabama pension form with the word ALABAMA struck out and “Florida” written in, gave her address as Hardeetown and her income as “NONE.”

Two likenesses of her survive: the family group with her husband and their son George standing behind, and a head-and-shoulders portrait of a dark-haired woman with her hair parted in the middle, kept in a cousin’s scrapbook and copied so many times that the face has gone to patches of black and white.

She died at Chiefland at six in the evening on 30 November 1922, of what the doctor called paralysis of the bowels after a fortnight, with old age contributing. She was eighty-five. The informant was simply “Son,” who gave her father’s name as John Ward and answered the next three questions with a question mark. She is buried at Hardeetown Methodist Cemetery in plot S5, the only Truett in the ground, with her daughter Sarah on one side and Sarah’s mother-in-law on the other: three old women in a row. Her stone reads CAROLINE W. TRUETT — the W for Ward, her maiden name cut into her own marker — over a birth year, 1829, that is seven years wrong and came from the son standing at the registrar’s desk two days after she died.

Clark Strong Tuttle (1811 Jefferson Co. NY – 1876/80)

Ahnentafel #96 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Clark Strong Tuttle was born in Jefferson County, New York, on 27 April 1811 — that is his family Bible’s date; the printed Tuttle genealogy says the 28th. His father, Colonel Gershom Tuttle, kept a farm and a famous tavern outside Watertown; his mother was Pamelia Strong Clark, and he carried both of her surnames and used them in full, unusually enough that a United States land officer wrote him down in 1837 as Clark Strong Tuttle.

He was six when the family crossed to Indiana in the autumn of 1817 and took up land near Fort Harrison on the Wabash, and he grew up there. His father died in September 1823, when Clark was twelve, leaving an estate that proved insolvent and took nine years to close. In 1830, aged nineteen, he had no Tuttle household in the county to live in: he was counted in the house of Henry Markle, who had married his sister Amanda.

He came of age in April 1832 and married Harriet M. Sweany two weeks later, on 11 May. Their children, in the Bible’s hand, were Gersham, Aula, Hannah, Chester, Josephine, Frank and a second Aula — and four of the first five died as infants or small children and lie together under “C.S. & H. TUTTLE” stones at Otter Creek Union Cemetery. The second Aula was named for the sister who had died four years before she was born. Harriet herself died on 3 June 1851, aged thirty-seven; five weeks later, on 10 July, Clark married Nancy A. Harbert, and by her he had Edward, Emma, Dennis, Richard B., Harriet B. and Dollie A.

He was a miller, and a rich one: $9,500 in land in 1850, and in 1860 $14,000 in land and $9,215 besides, with three hired hands and a servant living in and every school-age child in the house at school. He took United States patents on four parcels in 1839 and 1841, became a county commissioner in October 1858, and was postmaster at Ellsworth — which was the post office at his own Tuttle’s Mills. The 1874 county atlas gave the mill a plate of its own: a four-storey frame mill under a smoking stack with box cars on the siding, captioned C. S. TUTTLE & CO., MILLERS & DEALERS IN GRAIN, FLOUR, MEAL &c., ELSWORTH, VIGO CO., IND., with his name, his son Frank’s and a partner’s beneath it; the township plat in the same atlas is lettered “C. S. Tuttle · Mill · ELSWORTH STA.” The mill was the town.

Not everything went well. In October 1869 a postal detective charged his son Frank, who ran the office under him, with robbing the mail, and the Terre Haute paper reported that “Mr. Clark Tuttle, the father of young Frank, is terribly distressed at the result of this unfortunate affair.” The following summer he sold out: the 117-acre mill tract on 12 July 1870 for $6,000, the partnership of C. S. Tuttle & Co. dissolved on the 26th, the home parcels gone by 24 October. By 1878 the mill “has been standing idle for four years.”

He was still alive and still public-spirited in June 1876, when the committee for the Centennial Fourth of July listed him among its vice-presidents as “Clark Tuttle, Otter Creek.” He was dead before May 1880, when his daughter was being described in print as the daughter of “the late C. S. Tuttle.” His widow Nancy was buried from Union Church in Otter Creek in April 1883. No stone marks either of them.

Nancy A. Harbert (b. ~1826 Ohio)

Ahnentafel #97 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Nancy A. Harbert — the Vigo County clerk wrote it Herbert — was born in Ohio about 1826 or 1827. Nothing is known of her parents, or of how she came to Indiana.

On 10 July 1851 she married Clark Strong Tuttle, the miller of Otter Creek Township, before Harvey W. Allen, justice of the peace. His first wife Harriet had died five weeks earlier and had left him young children and a mill to run, and four of Harriet’s children were already buried in the Otter Creek ground. Nancy had six children of her own by him — Edward, Emma, Dennis, Richard B., Harriet B. and Dollie A. — and this family descends from her.

The house she kept in 1860 was a prosperous one: her husband a miller worth $14,000 in land, three hired hands and a servant living in, and every school-age child at school. She was thirty-three that summer.

She outlived her husband by several years. She died on 25 April 1883 and was buried the next day out of Union Church in Otter Creek Township, the church attached to the cemetery where her stepchildren lay. The notice gave her no name of her own — Mrs. Clark S. Tuttle, widow of the late Clark S. Tuttle, the miller. No stone survives for her.

Francis Fuerstenberger (~1826 Bavaria – 1894)

Ahnentafel #98 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Francis Fuerstenberger — Frank, to Terre Haute — was born in Bavaria in 1826 or 1827. He is the immigrant on this line, and the town he came from and the ship he came on are both lost.

On 26 February 1852, in Clark County, Illinois, he married a young widow, Sophia Flint. The county clerk, J. Stockwell, licensed “Francis Fiestenburgen” and Sophia Flint, “now both of this County,” and the minister certified that he had joined them “according to the usual custom and laws of the State of Illinois.” Their first child was born at Marshall, in that county, the following November.

About 1854 they came to Terre Haute, and from that year until the day he died he lived in one house: 521 South Third Street. He was a shoemaker, and by 1870 he owned $2,000 of real estate; a few doors away lived another Bavarian in the leather trade — the immigrant quarter of a river city in miniature. In October 1864 the Terre Haute post office advertised the letters nobody had called for, and there in the gentlemen’s column, between Foulke and Fort, stood “Fuerstenberger F.”

Eight children were born to him and Sophia. In October 1881 his daughter Emma married Richard B. Tuttle, a young miller; three years later, in December 1884, Richard died of consumption, and the funeral was held at ten in the morning from Frank’s own house on South Third Street. The widow and the baby stayed.

On the Wednesday before 1 March 1890 he and Sophia kept their fortieth wedding anniversary at the South Third Street house, “very delightfully,” afternoon and evening. She was dead within four months.

He died in September 1894, aged sixty-eight. The Saturday Evening Mail gave him a paragraph on its front page as “an old and well known citizen” and noted the thing he was known for: forty years in Terre Haute, and all of them in the one house. He lies at Highland Lawn Cemetery, Section 3, Lot 162, under a shared obelisk with Sophia, the inscription recording his age to the day — sixty-eight years and twenty-four days. Forty years after his death an inheritance suit was still naming the heirs of five of his eight children.

Ahnentafel #99 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sophia was born in Pennsylvania in 1828. She had been married once before: when she stood up with Francis Fuerstenberger in Clark County, Illinois, on 26 February 1852, she was Sophia Flint.

She and Francis had eight children between 1852 and 1870 — John Fredrick, James W., Anna, Clara Rebecca, Emma, Daniel, George and Eliza. John Fletcher married Sarah Wallace, and their daughter Mary Elizabeth was born in Indiana on 21 September 1858. Beyond that almost nothing of him survives — not where he married, not where he lived, not where he is buried.

Elias Lamb (1814 NC – 1890 Vermillion Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #108 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Elias Lamb was born on 24 September 1814 in Randolph County, North Carolina, about sixteen miles north of Asheboro, the son of Joseph and Lydia (Adamson) Lamb, Quakers. In April 1829, when he was fourteen, his parents took the family out of North Carolina and settled in Clay Township, Wayne County, Indiana, where they farmed until they died. Nine children had been born to them; seven grew up.

He was reared on his father’s farm and learned the carpenter’s trade, which he worked at alongside farming all his life. On 23 March 1837 he married Susannah Bish at the house of her father, John Bish, in Clay Township; the licence had been issued the day before, and Richard Jobes, Justice of the Peace, joined them. When the wedding was paid for he took stock of what he owned and found “a few carpenter tools and $20. in money”; her dowry was about twenty-five dollars’ worth of goods, including a heifer calf. They spent part of the twenty dollars on simple furniture and began housekeeping on his father’s farm on 7 June 1837. In April 1839 they bought a farm adjoining it; that September they swapped farms and moved three-quarters of a mile south, and there they stayed thirty-one years. In 1841 he and Susannah left the Society of Friends and united by letter with the United Brethren church, to which he belonged for the last forty-one years of his life.

Ten children were born to them, five sons and five daughters, of whom one, an infant son, died in June 1844. In politics he was a Free-Soiler until 1856 — “that is he believed it wrong for one man to enslave his fellow man” — and then a Republican from the year Frémont ran.

In November 1870 they sold up and bought the Montgomery farm near Perrysville, in Vermillion County; in March 1873 a farm a mile and a half north-west of Newport; and on 3 March 1887 the house in Newport where they spent their last years. On the night of 12 October 1883 burglars broke into the Newport house and beat Elias badly — one eye closed, a bullet grazing Susannah’s head and splitting her ear — until he reached the yard bell-rope and rang the alarm. He never fully recovered from it; three years on, the paper said he had received injuries from which he would probably never recover.

Their golden wedding fell on 23 March 1887. Fifty-eight relatives and friends came; a neighbour, M. G. Rhoads, read out a history of the two old people; two ministers performed the marriage ceremony over again; a senator made some remarks; and then everyone went in to a sumptuous dinner. The presents were listed in the paper down to a china catsup mug and a silver butter knife. They counted that day nine living children, twenty-nine grandchildren and eight great-grandchildren — forty-eight in all, most of them in the room.

He took sick one Monday in late August 1890 and died at four o’clock on the Sunday morning, 31 August, within a month of his seventy-sixth birthday. The funeral was preached at the United Brethren church by his pastor, the Rev. R. B. Van Allen, and “his remains were followed to the Thomas cemetery by a large concourse of relatives” — the burying ground at Newport, where his stone still stands, cut ELIAS LAMB DIED AUG. 30 1890 AGED 76 YEARS.
