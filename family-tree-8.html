Emma S. Fuerstenberger (1863 Terre Haute – 1926)

Ahnentafel #49 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emma S. Fuerstenberger was born at Terre Haute, Indiana, in July 1863, the fifth of the eight children of Francis Fuerstenberger, a Bavarian shoemaker, and his Pennsylvania-born wife Sophia. She grew up in the one house her father occupied for forty years, 521 South Third Street, and was still at home there at seventeen.

On 12 October 1881, at eighteen, she married Richard B. Tuttle, a young miller. Their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born at Peoria, Illinois, on 25 January 1884, and in December of that year Richard died of consumption in her father’s house. She was twenty-one, with a baby.

She married twice more: Hamilton B. Martin on 24 May 1891, and then, on 20 December 1894 at Charleston in Coles County, Illinois, Gifford H. Spear. It was Spear who finished raising her son, and the son repaid him by naming his own firstborn Gifford. In September 1916 she and Gifford Spear were living at 512 North Eighth Street in Terre Haute and had just come home from a month’s holiday in California.

Her elder sister Anna had married James H. Jumper at Terre Haute on 11 October 1876, before H. E. Wulzen, pastor of the German Methodist Episcopal church, with John Fuerstenberger signing as witness — the Fuerstenbergers were a German Methodist family five years before Emma’s own wedding. The Bible the Jumpers kept held what families keep: a slip of receipt paper noting which minister had baptised which child and, in a small white paper bag, some of young Elmer’s hair.

She died on 9 April 1926 as Emma S. Spear. The family Bible enters the death under that name, with “Tuttle” pencilled faintly above it — somebody wanting the record to remember which husband she belonged to. Eight years later her son stood in her place when a Fuerstenberger estate was divided five ways among her brothers’ and sisters’ lines.

Mary Elizabeth Fletcher (d. 1934)

Ahnentafel #51 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Elizabeth Fletcher was born in Indiana on 21 September 1858, the daughter of John Fletcher and Sarah Wallace. Where the family lived while she was growing up is not known.

She surfaces at Clinton, in Vermillion County, in 1880, aged twenty-three, single, and living in another family’s house as a “houseworker” — a domestic servant. A year later, on 16 June 1881, she married William L. Alexander of the same small town, and in February 1883 their daughter Leona was born. She bore one child and one only.

She was a widow by 1900, and she had not gone under. That June she was at 314 Mulberry Street in Terre Haute, forty years old, head of her own household, working as a housekeeper and owning the house — with her seventeen-year-old daughter beside her. By December 1902 she had moved to North Sixth Street, and it was in her house that her only child was married. After that she moved in with Leona and Dennis Tuttle and stayed for the rest of her life, more than thirty years under her daughter’s roof. She was still travelling with them in 1914, going out to West Terre Haute for the weekend in August and again in September.

In October 1910 her grandson Gifford’s seventh-birthday cake was a present from the boy’s grandmother, and she, living in the house, is the likelier of his two grandmothers to have baked it.

She died on 16 April 1934 at East Ellen, Vigo County, aged seventy-five. The family Bible records it simply: “Mary Elizabeth Alexander (1934).”

Benjamin Nott (~1817 OH – 1891 Marietta)

Ahnentafel #52 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Nott — the name was written Knott about as often as Nott — was born in Ohio about 1815 to 1817 and farmed in Adams Township, near Lowell, in Washington County. His own parents are not known. An elder Benjamin Nott “of Adams” died insolvent in 1822, leaving a widow Polly, and if that man was his father he was orphaned as a small boy on a broke farm on Calls Creek.

He married a Margaret, very probably the Margaret Kirk who married a Benj. Nott in Morgan County, up the Muskingum, on 13 March 1836. In 1860 the household stood at Lowell: Benjamin forty-three, a farmer; Margaret forty-three; P. J. fifteen; William F. thirteen; and Jos M eleven. That eleven-year-old is believed to be Joseph McGee Knott, who named Benjamin and Margaret as his parents and Lowell as his birthplace at the end of his own life; the ages do not quite line up, so the link is probable rather than certain.

In July 1861, at forty-six, he volunteered for Company A of the Thirty-sixth Ohio Infantry — enlisted 29 July 1861, a private, discharged 4 September 1864. By 1870 the family had moved to Marietta, where the household was Benjamin fifty-three, Margaret fifty-two, John W. twenty-three, Sarah twenty, a baby Henry, and three boarders.

His last years were hard. In December 1890 the probate court recorded “P. J. Nott, guardian of Benjamin Nott, an Insane person” filing an inventory — the guardian was his own child, the P. J. of the 1860 household. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891 of la grippe, the influenza that went round the world between 1889 and 1891, and the estate was settled that July: charged with nothing at all, credited with $10.91, and that sum found due the child who had cared for him. He lies in Oak Grove Cemetery at Marietta, lot 90, block 19, under an upright stone.

Margaret was the wife of Benjamin Nott of Adams Township, Washington County, Ohio, and the mother of Joseph McGee Knott. Her maiden name has not survived: when her son died in 1932 the family could give only “Margaret.”

She was born about 1817, in Ohio by one account and in Virginia by another. In 1860 she was at Lowell with Benjamin and the children, aged forty-three; in 1870, in Marietta, she was fifty-two. She was very probably the Margaret Kirk who married a Benj. Nott in Morgan County, up the Muskingum, on 13 March 1836 — the Kirks of Meigs Creek in Morgan County were a real family, and one of them kept a cabin that sheltered Virginia migrants walking west in 1817, which would account for a daughter whose birthplace an enumerator once wrote as Virginia.

A Mrs. Margaret Nott of Lowell, aged eighty-three, died on Sunday 15 January 1899, and her body was carried up the Muskingum by the steamer Sonoma for burial at Oak Grove Cemetery, Marietta — the cemetery where Benjamin lies. The paper called her the widow of “the late Thomas Nott, of Lowell,” so whether that was our Margaret going home to her husband’s grave, or another Nott widow of the same name and age in the same village, is not settled.

Erwin R. Lamb (1840 Wayne Co. IN – 1919 Jonesboro)

Ahnentafel #54 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Erwin R. Lamb — Irvin on his own gravestone, Irwin in the Jonesboro papers; the three spellings are one man — was born on 19 March 1840 in Wayne County, Indiana, the second of the ten children of Elias Lamb, carpenter and farmer, and Susannah (Bish) Lamb, who had married at her father’s house in Clay Township on 23 March 1837. His grandparents were Joseph and Lydia (Adamson) Lamb, Quakers who had brought the family from Randolph County, North Carolina, to Wayne County in April 1829, eleven years before he was born. He was reared on his father’s farm in Clay Township and stayed in the county until he was a grown man.

On 14 March 1860 he took out a licence in Wayne County to marry Melissa J. Bailey, the daughter of a neighbouring farming family; the minister married them the next day. It was a double season: his elder brother Azel had married Sarah Ann Bailey three months before, in the next township. That summer the newlyweds were still under his father’s roof in Clay Township, on a farm worth eleven thousand dollars. Four children came — Alma S., Martha E., Frank A. and May Belle — and then, on the evening of Monday 29 November 1875, after a few days’ illness, Melissa died at Newport in Vermillion County, aged thirty-one. The family had moved there in 1870 with his parents.

On 18 May 1879 he married Mrs. Mary Elizabeth (Jackson) Hall, the widow of a soldier of the 125th Illinois Infantry and the daughter of Arthur Jackson, an early Vermillion County settler who had given three sons to the Union and lost all three. She brought five Hall children with her. In 1880 the two families were together on Main Street in Vermillion — Erwin forty, “Mary E.” forty-one, his own Mattie, Frank and Bell, and her Effie. By 1888 he was living at Newport. In May 1890 he was very probably the Erwin Lamb of Plainfield, Indiana, who came down to Terre Haute to stay with two of his children — his daughter, by then Mrs. Mattie Ford, and his son Frank — who were keeping house together on north Third Street.

He did not stay in Indiana. He and his wife were settled at Jonesboro, in Craighead County, Arkansas, in a house on Krewson Avenue, by 1911 at the latest, and he kept the books for other men to the end of his working life: for many years he was clerk at the Old American Hotel. He was a member of the First Methodist church and a regular attender until his health failed. He spent seven weeks in St. Bernard’s Hospital in 1914.

His last illness is in the Jonesboro papers almost day by day. He had been ill at home for ten days in October 1918 and in poor health for eight months; on Monday 17 February 1919 an infected foot was amputated at St. Bernard’s, and the paper noted that at his age the operation was a shock to his nervous system; he was reported resting nicely, then continuing to improve; the surgeons went back and took the leg off below the knee for gangrene; and he died at half past twelve on the morning of 27 February 1919, aged seventy-eight. One paper called him “a splendid man”; the other, “an old resident of the city… highly respected by everyone, making a friend of every acquaintance.” Six men carried him. His survivors were given as his wife and his step-children — A. W. Hall and Mrs. C. C. Conger of Jonesboro, Mrs. Josephine Schweizer of Danville, Illinois, and Mrs. Effie Townsend of Richmond, Illinois. His own children, his daughter Martha among them, were not mentioned. George Frost (Paterson, New Jersey)

Ahnentafel #56 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

George Frost was born in England about 1820, at Macclesfield in Cheshire, into a family “engaged largely in the silk manufacture.” He entered the trade himself in 1838, while still in his early boyhood, and his department from the first was throwing — the twisting of raw silk into yarn.

He married Frances W. Bell, also English. Their children included Mary; Frank, born at Macclesfield in April 1858; Harry B., about March 1860; Morris, about 1863, still in England; and Charles, the first of them born in America, in New Jersey about 1866. The family came over in 1864 and settled at Paterson, and he commenced business in this country about 1870 with but four hands.

By June 1880 he was at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, fifty-eight years old, a silk manufacturer with rheumatism, and two of his sons were working in the mill. That year he took Frank and Harry B. into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, with a frontage of a hundred feet on Madison Street and, as one visitor put it, “an air of taste too rarely seen in factory buildings.” They threw yarn exclusively on commission, and were the first house in Paterson to install the celebrated Atwood machinery. By the middle eighties the firm was advertising from 36 to 52 Madison Street as makers of “Organzine, Tram, Floss, etc. On Commission,” and in September 1885 a fire in Straight Street singed the back of a new mill they were putting up.

The Albion Mill in 1887 was a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street, running from No. 34 to No. 52, with GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING lettered along the pavement. Reeling was done on the first floor, spinning on the first and second, winding and doubling on the third, winding on the fourth, and there was a carpenter’s repair shop in the basement. One engine of 350 horsepower drove the whole of it, an artesian well was sunk in the yard, and about three hundred hands worked there. His neighbours were the rest of the trade — Wm. Strange & Co. and Doherty & Wadsworth across the street, Jno. Dunlop’s silk twist mill and Naef Bros. & Co. round the corner, Benj. Eastwood making silk machinery a block away.

In February 1887, when the Paterson silk dyers struck and the whole trade seized up, George Frost & Sons told their four hundred hands that the Albion Mills would not reopen until the strike was settled. The news went out on the wire and was reprinted as far away as Topeka, Grand Island and Victoria, British Columbia. It is the one day his name travelled.

He died four months later, at Paterson, on 27 June 1887, aged sixty-seven, and was buried — all but certainly — at Cedar Lawn Cemetery, remembered there in the terms he would have chosen: “senior member of the firm, George Frost & Sons, silk manufacturers; native of Macclesfield, England.”
