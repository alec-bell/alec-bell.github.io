George Frost (Paterson, New Jersey)

Ahnentafel #56 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

George Frost was born in England about 1820, at Macclesfield in Cheshire, where his family were “engaged largely in the silk manufacture.” He entered the trade himself in 1838, while still in his early boyhood, and his department from the first was throwing — the twisting of raw silk into yarn.

He married Frances W. Bell, also English. Their children included Mary, Frank (born at Macclesfield in April 1858), Harry B. (about March 1860), Morris (about 1863, still in England) and Charles, who was born in New Jersey about 1866; Harry’s obituary says the family came to America in 1864 and settled in Paterson. An industrial history of the city published in 1882 says he “commenced business in this country about 1870 with but four hands.”

By June 1880 he was at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, fifty-eight years old and set down as a silk manufacturer, with rheumatism noted against his name in the health column; two of his sons were working in the mill. That year he took Frank and Harry B. into partnership, and George Frost & Sons built the Albion Mill, with a frontage of a hundred feet on Madison Street and, the 1882 history remarked, “an air of taste too rarely seen in factory buildings.” They threw yarn exclusively on commission, and were the first house in Paterson to install the celebrated Atwood machinery. By the middle eighties the firm was advertising from 36 to 52 Madison Street as makers of “Organzine, Tram, Floss, etc. On Commission,” and in September 1885 a fire in Straight Street singed the back of a new mill they were putting up.

In February 1887, when the Paterson silk dyers struck and the whole trade seized up, George Frost & Sons told their four hundred hands that the Albion Mills would not reopen until the strike was settled. The paragraph went out on the wire from the New-York Tribune and was reprinted as far away as Topeka, Grand Island and Victoria, British Columbia. It is the one day his name travelled.

The Albion Mill can still be looked at. Paterson's fire-insurance surveyors drew it in 1887, a long brick block on the north side of Madison Street running from No. 34 to No. 52, lettered along the pavement GEO. FROST & SONS — SILK THROWING. The surveyors wrote the work down floor by floor: reeling on the first, spinning on the first and second, winding and doubling on the third, winding on the fourth, and a carpenter's repair shop in the basement. One engine of 350 horsepower drove the whole of it, there was an artesian well sunk in the yard, and against the block the surveyor noted “300 hands.” The same sheet puts the mill among its competitors — Wm. Strange & Co. and Doherty & Wadsworth across the street, Jno. Dunlop's silk twist mill and Naef Bros. & Co. round the corner, Benj. Eastwood making silk machinery a block away.

He died four months later, at Paterson, on 27 June 1887, aged sixty-seven, and lies in Cedar Lawn Cemetery. The burial entered there is almost certainly his, and it remembers him in the terms he would have chosen: “senior member of the firm, George Frost & Sons, silk manufacturers; native of Macclesfield, England.” He is named as Harry B. Frost’s father twice over: on his son’s 1896 Detroit marriage record, where the clerk wrote “father Geo Frost, mother Frances W Bell”, and again thirty-four years later, when Harry died and the Paterson paper called him “the second son of George and Frances W. Frost.”

Frances W. Bell (Paterson, New Jersey)

Ahnentafel #57 · his mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Frances W. Bell was born in England, and came, like her husband, out of the Cheshire silk country; the 1880 census of Paterson sets down her own birthplace, and both her parents’, as England. She married George Frost, a Macclesfield throwster, and bore the children who carried the firm on — Mary, Frank, Harry B., Morris, and Charles, the youngest and the first of them born in America.

In June 1880 the census found her at 309 Straight Street, Paterson, keeping house for a household of seven: her husband, a daughter at home, three sons, the two eldest boys already working in the family’s silk mill. Her surname is a coincidence that has to be said plainly — this Bell family has no connection whatever to the Alabama Bells elsewhere in this history — but the name did carry forward: her grandson, born in Pennsylvania in 1900, was christened Harry Bell Frost.

She is named twice in print. Once on her son Harry’s 1896 Detroit marriage record, where the clerk wrote the groom’s mother as “Frances W. Bell”; and once more thirty-four years later, when Harry died at Wyckoff and the Paterson paper, still knowing whose child he was, called him “the second son of George and Frances W. Frost.”

She outlived George by a dozen years. A Cedar Lawn burial that is very probably hers records “Frances W Frost … widow of George,” who died at Paterson on 5 January 1899, her last address 189 Tyler Street.

Mathew Chalmers (~1821 Scotland – 1893 Orange NJ)

Ahnentafel #58 · his mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mathew Chalmers was a Scot who settled in Essex County, New Jersey. He is known from the parents’ columns of his daughter’s own marriage record: when Katharine Chalmers, thirty years old, a schoolteacher, born at Orange, New Jersey, married the Paterson silk manufacturer Harry B. Frost at Detroit on 10 June 1896, she gave the clerk her father as Mathew Chalmers and her mother as Ellen King. That one line of a Michigan register is the whole of his documentary existence in this family.

The rest comes at one remove. Two of his grandchildren were given their grandmothers’ maiden surnames as middle names — Katherine King Frost and Harry Bell Frost — which is how this family marked descent. Two more of his daughters, Mrs. Margaret Jordan and Miss Sarah Chalmers, were living at East Orange in 1932, when their sister died. And every census that records Katharine agrees that both her parents were born in Scotland: this is where the line crosses to the old country.

The 1870 census finds the whole family at Orange, in the second ward: Mathews Chalmers, forty-nine, born in Scotland, a retired dealer in stores with real estate worth $4,600 and $1,500 more in personal property; his wife Ellen, forty-four, also Scottish; and four New Jersey-born daughters — Ellen B., thirteen, Margaret, seven, Catharine, four, and Sarah, one. Katharine is the four-year-old, and Margaret and Sarah are the two sisters who outlived her in 1932. That household is what ties the family in this tree to a pair of graves at Rosedale Cemetery in Montclair, four miles from Orange, where a flat granite stone is cut MATTHEW CHALMERS, 1821–1893, beside his wife Ellen’s. He died in 1893, when Katharine was twenty-seven and teaching school.

Moses B. Roush (1842 – 1925 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #60 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Moses B. Roush was born on 30 January 1842 in Meigs County, Ohio, in the riverside settlement the Roushes and the Neases had founded together, and which was called the Nease Settlement. He was a son of Benjamin “Bennie” Roush and Sophia Altman, and he appears in their Sutton Township household aged seven in 1850 and eighteen in 1860.

He went to the war late. He entered service on 23 July 1864, aged twenty-two, as a private in Company A - and afterwards Company B - of the 28th Ohio Volunteer Infantry, Ohio's “2nd German Regiment,” raised at Camp Dennison from German-speaking volunteers and by 1864 taking whatever recruits the state had left. He served the last twelve months of the war, from the Atlanta summer through to three months after Appomattox, and was discharged on 6 July 1865.

On 1 September 1870 he married Sarah Duckworth, the daughter of a Lancashire collier, at Orange Township in Meigs County; her brother Adam married in the same township eight days later. They had Sarah Ann in 1871, Charles Benjamin in 1874, John G. in 1877 and Richard in 1880. In March 1880 Moses applied for an invalid pension on his war service; eleven months later, on 4 March 1881, Sarah died, leaving him four children under ten. He married Lydia E. in Mason County in August 1883 and took the family across the Ohio into Graham District, where he lived about twenty years and had five more children. In June 1900 he and his son John were enumerated on the same page at Hartford village, five lines apart.

Lydia died in 1906. In 1908 he buried his son John and two granddaughters. He married a third time, to Margaret A. Zerkle, in 1911. From 1920 the government paid his invalid pension at an increased rate, and raised it again in 1923.

In January 1887 his name went into the Point Pleasant paper in a notice headed "ATTENTION! — The boys who wore the blue": the pensioners of the county were being called before the United States Board of Examining Surgeons, which sat every Wednesday at Dr. Hoy's office, and the Register printed him in the list as "Moses Roush, Co. A. 28th Ohio Vols." He had to be re-examined before the quarter closed on 31 March or lose his place on the pay roll. The notice ran three weeks running.

In May 1883 the Grand Army of the Republic mustered in Washington Post No. 13 at New Haven, Mason County, with fourteen charter members; its Officer of the Guard was a Moses Roush — very probably this one. He stood surety on his widowed daughter-in-law's $500 guardianship bond in May 1909, and he was still doing road work with a team for the county in his sixties — 'Moses B Roush, team and labor' appears in the Graham District road accounts printed in 1907 and 1910.

He died at six in the evening on 23 August 1925 at New Haven, aged eighty-three years six months and twenty-three days, of general arteriosclerosis with senility, after Dr. R. S. Bryant had attended him for three days. The informant was his son Charles B. Roush - and it is that certificate, naming “Bennie” Roush of Graham Station and Sophia Aultman of Portsmouth, Ohio, that gave this family its grandparents. He was buried at Broad Run, under a barrel-topped stone of the same pattern as his parents', reading FATHER & MOTHER / MOSES B. ROUSH / BORN JAN. 30, 1842. His widow filed her pension claim eight days after he died, and the money that paid for the end of his life had been granted for a war that ended sixty years before.

Sarah Mary Duckworth (~1851 PA – 1881)

Ahnentafel #61 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Mary Duckworth was born in June 1851 in Pennsylvania, the fourth child and the first American child of James Duckworth, a Lancashire collier, and his wife Ann Collins. Her three elder brothers and sisters had been born in England and her two younger brothers in Pennsylvania and Ohio, so she is the one who dates the family's crossing: they were still in England when Adam was born in March 1847, and in Pennsylvania by the time she arrived.

By 1860 the Duckworths had moved on to the Ohio Valley coalfield, and the census taker at Syracuse, Meigs County, wrote the family name down as “Deckworth” and Sarah's age as nine. She was still at home in the summer of 1870, aged nineteen, and on 1 September that year she married Moses B. Roush at Orange Township in the same county - a Union veteran nine years her senior. Her brother Adam married eight days later in the same township. It was a busy fortnight for the family.

They had four children: Sarah Ann in 1871, Charles Benjamin in 1874, John G. in 1877 and Richard, born at Syracuse in 1880. She named the youngest for her own brother. The June 1880 census finds the little household in Sutton Township - and her widowed mother Ann keeping house five doors away.

Sarah died on 4 March 1881, aged twenty-nine, leaving four children under ten; her youngest was a baby. Her mother was living at the end of the row when it happened. She is buried at Broad Run Cemetery in Mason County - the same ground her husband, her son John and her grandchildren would come to - and her stone gives June 1851 to 4 March 1881.

John (F.) Bell (1807 SC – 1890 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #64 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Bell was born on 13 January 1807 in South Carolina. His son’s obituary says his parents brought the family to Alabama from Darlington District about 1830 and settled in the neighbourhood where they stayed for the next century. He is certainly in Alabama by 1836, because in that year he served as a private in Captain White’s company, Regiment of Alabama Volunteers, in the Creek War — a fact nobody in this family knew until 2026, and one that survives only because the government eventually paid him for it in land. He was settled in Barbour County before 1837, and every one of his children was born in Alabama, the eldest, Mary, about 1836 or 1837.

He began buying early. On 19 July 1838 John H. Miller sold him about 189 acres in Township 10, Range 26 for five hundred dollars, Ann Caroline Miller releasing her dower the same afternoon. In January 1841, from Alabama, he sold off a forty-acre Georgia gold-lottery lot in what had just become Forsyth County for thirty dollars, and signed the deed himself — John F Bell — which is worth knowing in a generation where so many men made their mark.

The censuses follow him across half a century. In 1840 there is a boy and a girl under five in the house. In 1850 he is forty-three and farming, with his wife Laney — South Carolina born like him — and Mary 13, William 11, John 9, Kervin 4 and Laney 2. Two of those children matter to this family: William, who would die a Confederate pensioner in 1924, leaving an estate that scattered across his nieces and nephews and so paid out the seven shares that finally proved who Tom Bell’s children were; and John, who would become John T. Bell and father Thomas Jefferson Bell. In 1860 he returns four thousand dollars in land and ten thousand in personal estate, and in the Alabama cotton country of that year a figure like that is largely a statement about people: a John Bell of Barbour County stands in the 1860 slave schedule, and although the name is common the identification is probable. In August 1870 he is sixty-three, with Laney at sixty-four and two grown children still at home, Mary and Archibald — and the very next dwelling on the page is his son John T. Bell, twenty-seven, with a wife and four small children. By June 1880 he is seventy-two and a widower, and he tells the enumerator, in the first census year that asked, that both his own parents were born in South Carolina.

In April 1853 President Franklin Pierce signed his patent: warrant 4615, forty acres and eleven hundredths of an acre, for the Creek War service. He sold that same forty to William Bounds in 1858, and for a generation afterwards his neighbours’ deeds navigated by “the east corner of the Bell forty.” In 1876, at sixty-nine, he gave a crop lien in his own voice — “I John F Bell being indebted to J. C. Jones … thirty five dollars … to make a crop the present year on my plantation in Barbour County Alabama.”

On 1 August 1890, at eighty-three, he divided the home place by deeds of gift — 203⅓ acres each to his son Curvin and his daughter Mary, each to take effect after his death, “I however, holding and retaining the ownership and comfort of said above described lands during my natural life time.” The boundary runs past “the big gate leading to the house” on the public road from Clayton to Louisville. That is why he left no probate file: he had given the farm away and kept the comfort of it. He died on 21 November 1890, and the following November his heirs petitioned to sell 604 acres containing “the John Bell homestead and out houses” for division — in equal thirds to William, to John T., and to the five children of the daughter who had married a Farrior. The boundaries of that tract name the neighbours, and they explain a good deal: the Daniel Norton land to the south, which is why two Bell wives later married a Norton, and the old William Beasley place, in whose little family burying ground he himself lies.
