Elias Lamb (1814 NC – 1890 Vermillion Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #108 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Elias Lamb was born on 24 September 1814 in Randolph County, North Carolina, about sixteen miles north of Asheboro — his own obituary says so, and so does the county biographical volume of 1888 that gave him a sketch in his old age. He was the son of Joseph and Lydia (Adamson) Lamb, Quakers. In April 1829, when he was fourteen, his parents took the family out of North Carolina and settled in Clay Township, Wayne County, Indiana, where they farmed until they died. Nine children had been born to them; seven grew up.

He was reared on his father’s farm and learned the carpenter’s trade, which he worked at alongside farming all his life. On 23 March 1837 he married Susannah Bish at the house of her father, John Bish, in Clay Township. When the wedding was paid for he took stock of what he owned and found “a few carpenter tools and $20. in money”; her dowry was about twenty-five dollars’ worth of goods, including a heifer calf. They spent part of the twenty dollars on simple furniture and began housekeeping on his father’s farm on 7 June 1837. In April 1839 they bought a farm adjoining it; that September they swapped farms and moved three-quarters of a mile south, and there they stayed thirty-one years. In 1841 he and Susannah left the Society of Friends and united by letter with the United Brethren church.

Ten children were born to them, of whom one, an infant son, died in June 1844. In politics he was a Free-Soiler until 1856 — “he believed it wrong for one man to enslave his fellow man,” as the account of his golden wedding put it — and then a Republican from the year Frémont ran.

In November 1870 they sold up and bought the Montgomery farm near Perrysville, in Vermillion County; in March 1873 a farm a mile and a half north-west of Newport; and on 3 March 1887 the house in Newport where they spent their last years. On the night of 12 October 1883 burglars broke into the Newport house and beat Elias badly — one eye closed, a bullet grazing Susannah’s head and splitting her ear — until he reached the yard bell-rope and rang the alarm. He never fully recovered from it.

Their golden wedding fell on 23 March 1887. Fifty-eight relatives and friends came; a neighbour, M. G. Rhoads, read out a history of the two old people; two ministers performed the marriage ceremony over again; a senator made some remarks; and then everyone went in to a sumptuous dinner. The presents were listed in the paper down to a china catsup mug and a silver butter knife. They counted that day nine living children, twenty-nine grandchildren and eight great-grandchildren — forty-eight in all, most of them in the room.

He took sick one Monday in late August 1890 and died at four o’clock on the Sunday morning, 31 August — the Newport Hoosier State printed a column headed “Death of Elias Lamb” the following Wednesday — within a month of his seventy-sixth birthday. The funeral was preached at the United Brethren church by his pastor, the Rev. R. B. Van Allen, and “his remains were followed to the Thomas cemetery by a large concourse of relatives” — the burying ground at Newport, where his stone still stands, cut ELIAS LAMB DIED AUG. 30 1890 AGED 76 YEARS.

Susannah (Bish) Lamb (1818 Fairfield OH – 1908 Illinois)

Ahnentafel #109 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah Bish was born on 17 November 1818 in Fairfield County, Ohio, about eighteen miles east of Columbus, a daughter of John and Ann Elizabeth Bish — the county biographical volume of 1888 names her parents, and the newspaper account of her golden wedding the year before gives her birthplace to the mile. She was brought up among Lutherans, had what the newspapers of her old age politely called “a very limited education in the old fashioned ‘deestrict schools’,” and learned, in the phrasing of the same account, “the various mysteries of house keeping.” In 1836, by the same 1888 account, she came with her parents to Wayne County, Indiana.

On 23 March 1837, four months past her eighteenth birthday, she married Elias Lamb at her father’s house in Clay Township. Her dowry to the “joint stock company” was about twenty-five dollars’ worth of goods, including a heifer calf. They kept house first on his father’s farm, then on farms of their own three-quarters of a mile away, for thirty-one years. Ten children were born to her; one, an infant son, died in June 1844. In 1841 she and Elias left the Society of Friends — his people’s church, not hers — and joined the United Brethren by letter.

The family moved to Vermillion County in November 1870, and in 1887 into a house in Newport. Four years before that, on the night of 12 October 1883, burglars broke in on them; Elias was badly beaten and a bullet grazed Susannah’s head and split her ear. Their golden wedding, on 23 March 1887, drew fifty-eight relatives and friends, two ministers to perform the ceremony over again, and a table of presents; they counted forty-eight descendants, most of them present.

She outlived Elias by eighteen years. In her last years she made her home with her son-in-law Levi S. Clendenning near Sugar Grove, Illinois, and she died there at three o’clock on the morning of Wednesday 8 April 1908, at ninety. Eight of her children survived her. The funeral was held in the church at Sugar Grove the next morning, and she was brought back to Indiana and buried beside Elias in the Thomas burying ground at Newport, under a stone that says only SUSANNAH, wife of E. LAMB.

Milo Bailey (Clay Twp., Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #110 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Milo Bailey is a candidate, not yet an ancestor. He was the son of John Bailey, a Virginian who came out of Kentucky about 1810 to Richmond, Indiana, moved on to Perry Township and then, in 1859, to Clay Township in Wayne County, where — as the county historian put it in 1872 — “his son Milo resides”; John Bailey died in 1863, aged seventy-two. The same page of that history lists “Ithamar Lamb, Milo Bailey, and Lewis Strickler” living on one tract of school land in Clay Township, which is to say that the Baileys and the Lambs were neighbours on the ground.

That neighbourliness is the reason his name is on this chart. In one winter, two Lamb brothers married two Bailey women out of the same township: Azel E. Lamb and Sarah Ann Bailey in December 1859, and Irvin R. Lamb and Melissa J. Bailey in March 1860. Melissa was born in Wayne County on 24 July 1844, so her father was a Wayne County Bailey of exactly Milo’s generation and place. But no record found so far names Milo Bailey’s daughters, and until one does the connection is a reconstruction rather than a fact. A caution runs with it: an unrelated Milo Lamb, of the other North Carolina Lamb family in the same county, sits in the same records and has been mistaken for him before.

Benjamin “Bennie” Roush (1816 – 1897)

Ahnentafel #120 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Benjamin Roush was born on 30 August 1816 at Graham Station, in Mason County, Virginia - the fifth child, by the family's own record, of John “Blackbird John” Roush and his wife Regina. He was the last of them born on the Virginia bank: his sister Hannah arrived in May 1820 in the Nease Settlement across the river in Meigs County, Ohio, so the family crossed in the years between.

On 23 May 1839 he married Sophia Altman at Orange Township, Meigs County. The justice of the peace who joined them did not get round to filing his return until the following July, when he certified two weddings at once in a single sentence. Sophia was not yet sixteen. Twelve children were born to them; nine of them are in the house in the 1860 census.

He farmed in Sutton Township. In 1850 he was thirty-four, Virginia-born, with seven children and an older woman, Mary Roush, thirty-six and also born in Virginia, living in the household. Four dwellings up the same page was John Roush, sixty-six, born in Virginia. In August 1849 that John Roush - a man who could not write and signed every deed with a mark - sold Benjamin sixty-nine acres and twenty poles in the north-east corner of Section 25, Town 3, Range 12 for five hundred dollars; on the same day, before the same justice and the same two witnesses, the rest of that same strip went to John Roush Junior. No deed says why.

By 1870 Benjamin and Sophia were at Syracuse, the salt and coal village on the river, in the same census year as the English Duckworth family whose daughter their son Moses would marry that September. By 1880 they had crossed back into West Virginia and were living in Waggener District, Mason County.

He died on 5 March 1897. Sophia outlived him by sixteen months. They lie together at Broad Run Cemetery under a barrel-topped stone on a rough-faced plinth: FATHER & MOTHER / BENJAMIN ROUSH / BORN AUG. 30, 1816 / DIED MAR. 5, 1897. To his own son, giving the names to a county clerk in 1925, he was “Bennie.”

Sophia Altman (1823 OH – 1898 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #121 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sophia Altman was born on 1 June 1823 in Ohio. Her son's death certificate, written eighty-four years later, says she came from Portsmouth, in Scioto County, sixty miles down the same river.

She married Benjamin Roush at Orange Township, Meigs County, on 23 May 1839, at fifteen years and eleven months - nine days short of her sixteenth birthday. Ohio required a parent's consent for a bride that young, so a paper naming her father was filed at the Meigs County courthouse that May; it has never been found, and her parents remain unproven.

Twelve children were born to the marriage. Nine of them are in the Sutton Township household in 1860: Moses, Joseph, Sarah, Livina, Zillah, Hannah, Maria and the rest. Six are known by name and fate - Moses B., Sarah Elizabeth Kauff, Mary Lovina Findley, Maria Ann Goodnite, Serepta Ann, and Dillia McDaniel - and four of the daughters lived long enough for Ohio and West Virginia to register their deaths, each certificate naming Sophia as their mother, spelled Altman on some and Aultman on others.

The family farmed Sutton Township through the 1850s and 1860s, were at Syracuse in 1870, and by 1880 had crossed into Waggener District, Mason County, West Virginia. Sophia died back in Meigs County, Ohio, on 4 July 1898, sixteen months after Benjamin, on Independence Day. She was buried beside him at Broad Run, under a stone that calls her only SOPHIA, HIS WIFE - the granite does not give her maiden name at all.

James Duckworth (~1813/18 Lancashire – 1874/80 OH)

Ahnentafel #122 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

James Duckworth was born in Lancashire about 1813 to 1818, the son of George Duckworth, a hatter of the Bury district. He went down the pit instead. On 21 January 1839, described in the register as “of full age, Bachelor, Collier, of Wood Head Hill,” he married Ann Collins of Freetown at the parish church of St Mary, Bury, after banns, by the curate J. F. Amos. Both of them signed with a mark; so did one of the witnesses, Richard Duckworth.

Three children were born to them in England - Mary about 1842, William about 1844, Adam in March 1847 - and a daughter later told an Ohio registrar that her parents came from Manchester, nine miles south of Bury and the name a Lancashire family abroad would naturally use. Then the family crossed the Atlantic, very probably in 1850 or 1851: they are not in the 1850 United States census, and Sarah was born in Pennsylvania in 1851. Ralph followed there about 1856, and by about 1859, when Richard was born, they had moved on to Ohio. It is the classic path of an English mining family - the Pennsylvania coalfield first, then the Ohio Valley pits.

In 1860 the census taker at Syracuse, Meigs County, put the name down as “Deckworth” and James at forty-seven, with Ann and six children in the house. In 1870 he was still the head of the household, aged fifty-six. Sarah married Moses Roush that September.

On 24 April 1874 the Meigs County probate court heard a case headed “In the Matter of the Insanity of James Duckworth.” James was present. On the sworn testimony of Wm Duckworth - almost certainly his own son William - and of Henry and August Bartels and David Thomas, the court found him insane, of less than two years' duration, with a legal settlement in Sutton Township, and entitled to admission to the Lunatic Asylum of the State of Ohio at Athens, which had opened only three months before. Dr. Isaac Irwin was ordered to file his medical certificate; in the meantime the court directed that the patient be held in the county jail “separate and apart from those who are charged with crime.”

He was dead by June 1880, when Ann was enumerated a widow. Where he died, and where he lies, is not known. A man sent to a state asylum leaves no will and no inventory, which is why there is no estate to find.

Ann Collins (b. ~1818 Lancashire – aft. 1860)

Ahnentafel #123 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Ann Collins was born in Lancashire after 21 January 1818 - that much is fixed, because she was still under twenty-one on that day in 1839, when she married. She was the daughter of Adam Collins of Freetown, in Bury, and it is only because she was a minor and her father's consent was required that his name was written into the register at all.

She married James Duckworth, a collier of Wood Head Hill, at St Mary's, Bury, on 21 January 1839. Neither of them could write; both made their mark. Three children came in England - Mary about 1842, William about 1844 and Adam in March 1847, named for her father. Then the family crossed to America, most likely in 1850 or 1851, and Sarah was born in Pennsylvania, Ralph about 1856, and Richard in Ohio about 1859.

In 1860 she was forty-three and keeping a house of eight at Syracuse, Meigs County, in the Ohio Valley coalfield. Her children married into the same English and Cornish mining families around them: Mary to John W. Treadwell, himself a Manchester man; William to Sarah Pascoe, a Cornish name; Sarah to Moses Roush, and Adam eight days after Sarah, in the same township, in September 1870.

By June 1880 James was gone. Ann, sixty-two and widowed, kept her own house in Sutton Township - and the census taker put her down five households from her daughter Sarah's family, where Sarah had four children under ten. Sarah died the following March, and her mother was right there when it happened. After that Ann disappears from the record; nothing has been found of her death or burial.

Her maiden name survived by a single thread. In 1924 her eldest daughter died in Ohio, and the certificate reads: “MOTHER: ANN COLLINS, England.”

George Nease (1819 – 1899 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #124 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

George Nease was born on 10 December 1819 - or on 21 November 1820, which is what his own death register says - in Virginia, into one of the Nease families of Mason County who had come down from the Shenandoah Valley with the Roushes and settled both banks of the Ohio.

On 15 December 1848, if the family genealogy is right, he married Nancy Roush. The record side agrees with everything around the claim: in 1850 the census of Mason County, Virginia, shows George, thirty-one, Nancy, eighteen, and an infant daughter, Susan M.; in 1860 the enumerator spelled the surname “Neoce” and the household contained a six-year-old Lewis J. Archibald "Archy" Bell (~1770/75 – bef. 1839 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #128 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Archibald Bell — “Archy” to the clerks who wrote down his name, and the spelling is his neighbours’, not an index’s: a Darlington proof clause of 1817 calls him ARCHEY on the same page where the same hand writes ARCHIBALD three times — was born about 1770 to 1775. If the reconstruction holds he was a son of William Rasor Bell, a planter of Salem in Sumter District who died leaving nine sons, two daughters and a widow named Mary. No document says so.

He farmed on both banks of Lynches Creek, the water that divides Sumter District from Darlington, and his own paperwork crossed the line with him: he is “of the same place” in Darlington in 1807, “of the district of Sumpter” in 1810, and “of the State and District aforesaid” in Darlington again in 1817. He bought land in Darlington in 1806 and sold a hundred and twenty-seven acres of it in February 1817, between Bay Branch and Newmans Swamp. His wife Martha released her dower twice, in October 1809 and February 1817, both times before the same justice of the peace, John Huggins, whose family would later marry into his. He was witnessing and swearing to deeds across the creek in Sumter from 1810, and in 1812 James Hutson senior conveyed land to “Archd Bell of the district of Sumpter” — a Darlington deed whose own index, in the same volume, writes the name out as Hudson James to Archd Bell. In 1815 he mortgaged a 345-acre tract on Lynches Creek to Zachariah Cantey — the same ground a surveyor had laid out for William Rasor Bell thirty years before.

Martha died, and he married Margaret, a Dixon widow with grown sons of her own; that is why one of his children is called Ezekiel Dixon Bell and why John H. Dixon, his stepson, was the executor he trusted with everything. They had five more children, with names that read like a roll-call of his neighbours: Benjamin Sumter, Margaret Louisa, Ezekiel Dixon, Hugh McCallum, Columbus Campbell. He was sued in 1830 for a witness fee. He was still buying land from Robert Severance in 1836, at sixty, the man who had been the household directly below his on the 1820 census page.

He made his will on 4 October 1839, weak in body but of sound mind, and it was filed on the 22nd. He left Margaret the 275-acre plantation for her life or her widowhood, then to the five children of the second marriage; the residue he divided among six more — “John Bell, Celey Amoson, Harriet Bell, Martha Bell, William Wesley Bell, and Jane Bell.” He left a set of blacksmith’s tools and thirteen geese. And he directed that the rest of his property be sold to pay his debts, “including my negroe boy Limus” — a boy of about fifteen who had already been pledged to Henry Scarborough nine months earlier. When the appraisers came on 26 October they valued Limus at seven hundred dollars against an estate of eleven hundred and eighteen. Sixty-three per cent of everything Archy Bell owned was one enslaved child. After the sale he disappears from the record.

His widow remarried a Severance, which tripped the will’s widowhood clause and forced the partition of 1846–47 — and the “Margaret Severance” who bought the plantation in May 1847 was the widow buying her own place back.

Whether the John Bell he named first among his older children is the John Bell who went to Alabama about 1830 is the question this family has not yet answered, and seven adversarial reviews have refused to answer it for him.

Joseph Eakins (d. 1805 New Hanover Co. NC)

Ahnentafel #144 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Eakins arrives in the records of New Hanover County, North Carolina all at once, in 1790, aged about twenty-four and already able to pay. On 11 June that year James Fleming, a Wilmington merchant, conveyed to “Joseph Eakins of the same place, Gentleman,” for fifteen pounds currency, two tracts: a hundred acres beginning at a black oak below the old track path, and three hundred acres on the east side of Moores Creek. Nothing before that date has been found — no father, no brother, no uncle of the name anywhere in the county, and no Eakins household in the county at all in the 1790 census. He came from somewhere else, and where that was is still the oldest open question on this side of the family.

He worked that land for fifteen years. In December 1793 he sold a parcel to Jacob Moore, signing himself “planter” this time rather than gentleman. In 1796 he went to the state land office and took out a patent of his own — grant No. 482, two hundred and ten acres on the east side of the Widow Moore's Branch, beginning at a pine on the east side of the New Road in the meadow, witnessed by Samuel Ashe, Governor, Captain General and Commander in Chief, at Raleigh. The boundary calls run past “a pine, Parker's corner, now Eakins.”

He married, almost certainly, Elizabeth Pigford, and they had at least five children: John, born about 1795, William, Joseph, Sarah and Deborah.

He died in the first months of 1805, leaving children too young to act for themselves. At the March 1805 term the county court sent three men out to set aside his widow's year's provision, and they wrote down what they left in the yard to keep the household alive for twelve months: the corn in hand with the bacon, four cows and calves, one four-year-old steer, and the mark or right of hogs. On 11 April the rest went under the hammer — cattle, hogs, sheep, plantation tools, household furniture — and made $1,657.57. The inventory came in that June. Edward and Jacob Pigford were the administrators; Timothy Pigford took the children; Solomon Pigford stood security on the estate's notes. He also left three people he had held in slavery, Simon, Rachel and Cesar, whose hired-out labour went on paying the estate's bills for years afterwards, including the schooling of his sons.

The family never moved off that ground. His son Joseph bought the adjoining 138 acres in 1834, the deed running from “Joseph Eakins's own corner” along “Eakins's line”; and the Eakins Family Cemetery still stands on the Moores Creek land, in what is now Pender County. That son, born in 1797 and eight years old when his father died, lived to ninety-three; his is the oldest grave in it, and the newest is from 2019.

probably Elizabeth Pigford (New Hanover Co., North Carolina)

Ahnentafel #145 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Elizabeth Pigford was, in all probability, the wife of Joseph Eakins of Moores Creek and the mother of his children. No marriage record has been found, and the notes keep the word “probably” in her name.

What is certain is that when Joseph died in the first months of 1805 the Pigfords closed around his family. Edward and Jacob Pigford administered the estate and ran the April auction. Solomon Pigford stood security on its notes. Timothy Pigford was guardian of the minor children for fourteen years, until the eldest, John, was old enough to take over at the February term of 1819. Four Pigfords in one court file is not neighbourliness; it is kin. And a Pigford was still selling Eakins land to an Eakins in 1834.

She is the “Widow Eakins” to whom the county court allotted a year's provision in March 1805 — the corn and the bacon, four cows and calves, a four-year-old steer and the mark or right of hogs that would keep her children fed while the estate was settled — and very likely the “Elizabeth Eakens” who appears in the 1819 accounts holding a note for $220.76.

Her father was almost certainly William “Lost Billie” Pigford of Sill's Creek, whose 1801 will, as the family's 1967 historian transcribed it, leaves a shilling each to a list of children that includes “my daughter Elizabeth Aiken” — Aiken and Eakins being one name in New Hanover clerks' hands. But a second, independent transcription of the same will, posted on the county's own genealogy website, gives that clause without her in it. Until the original will book is read, that is exactly where she stands: a woman the records keep pointing at and never quite name.
