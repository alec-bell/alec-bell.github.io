William Curtis Truett (1867 – 1925 New Prospect AL)

Ahnentafel #44 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Curtis Truett was born on 25 July 1867, the son of John Wesley Truett and Emily Caroline Ward, and grew up at Chilton in Clarke County, Alabama, in a house that held three generations at once: his father, a Confederate veteran who could not write his own name; his mother; his brothers and his sister; and his grandmother Alcey, the widow of the preacher William Hampton Truett, who was sixty-seven in 1880 and living under her son’s roof.

On 17 June 1886, not yet nineteen, he married Amanda Webb in Clarke County. They farmed at Clarksville, and by the summer of 1900 there were six children in the house — Tela, Jane, Bigy, Lillie, Tamie and the baby Bessie. Bessie and Jane were still living in 1960, when his second wife’s obituary named them as her step-daughters — Mrs. Bessie Shewmake of Satsuma and Mrs. Jane Hutchinson of Juniper, Georgia. He and Amanda could both read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do: the first literate generation on this line. Thomas William, the “Tamie” of that household, was Alec’s great-grandfather.

Amanda was alive in that house in June 1900 and dead or gone not long after, and he married a second time, to Zella Shewmake, born 1 June 1878.

He farmed at New Prospect, a settlement whose little Baptist church had been constituted about 1860 by an Elder C. J. Miles, who for his trouble was paid “one dollar per day while in active service.” When the county’s thirty-four Baptist churches were apportioned in 1892, New Prospect was asked for sixteen dollars — near the smallest sum in the county. All the places of his life sat within a few miles of one another: Chilton, where he grew up; Clarksville, where he farmed in middle life; and New Prospect, with Harris Creek running between them.

Two Truett brothers married two Webb sisters — he took Amanda, and his younger brother John Wesley took Valley. That brother died at his home in Coffeeville on a Wednesday morning in February 1936, of pneumonia after a few days’ illness, and was buried at Prospect church, the Rev. T. C. Paul of Grove Hill officiating. By then the family had scattered: a brother George and a sister, Sarah Giger, were both in Florida, where their mother had gone as an old woman.

He died at New Prospect on 21 December 1925, aged fifty-eight, and is buried in the church graveyard under a low double marker of pale granite, TRUETT across the top, his own name on the right and Zella’s on the left. She outlived him by thirty-five years, and the stone was almost certainly cut when she joined him in 1960. Amanda, the mother of his eldest son, is not on it, and is not buried there.

Amanda Webb (1868 – aft. 1900 Clarke Co.)

Ahnentafel #45 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Amanda Webb was born in July 1868 in Alabama and married William Curtis Truett in Clarke County on 17 June 1886, when she was not quite eighteen and he not quite nineteen.

The Webbs were old Clarke County people. A William Webb came into the county about 1812 and died at his home there in February 1853 at about seventy-eight; the Webb mill on the Tallahatta was built about 1836; and Webbs were among the families forted up at McGrew’s Fort in the Indian troubles of 1810. Whether Amanda belonged to them is not known.

Calm Lawrence (Clarke Co., Alabama)

Ahnentafel #46 · his father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sennie Lawrence’s father was born in Alabama in September 1860, to a father from North Carolina and a mother from South Carolina. His given name has never been settled. The hand that wrote it down in 1900 reads “Calm,” and what it was meant to be nobody knows.

He married Eliza about 1888 — they had been married twelve years when they were counted in 1900 — and they farmed at Coffeeville in Clarke County. Five children were in the house that year: Tomas N., eleven; Sallie E., nine; Mary E., six; Senie E., three; and Alfonso, entered as nought days old, because the enumerator had called on the day the baby was born. Two more followed, Lucy about 1905 among them.

He was not an old Clarke County man. The county’s history of 1882 names hundreds of its families across seven hundred and eighty pages and has no Lawrences in it at all, so his people must have come in from somewhere nearby — Washington or Choctaw or Marengo — recently enough to be strangers; the name is written Larance as readily as Lawrence.

By 1910 he was gone from the household: Eliza was head of the house with six children and no husband in it. A C. J. Lawrence who was very probably him died near Jackson, in Clarke County, in February 1929 — a widower, seventy-two years old by the clerk’s reckoning, born in Monroe County, Alabama — and was buried at Coffeeville.

He is very probably buried at Ulcanush Cemetery in Coffeeville, the same ground as his daughter Sennie and his infant son Alphonso, under one of two home-made markers that stand side by side there: slabs of rough concrete with the lettering scratched into them with a nail while the cement was still wet. His gives only initials, C J Lawrence. The other gives no name at all, only Mrs Lawrence, and a birth year that is his wife’s to the year.

Eliza was born in Alabama about 1863 or 1864 and married, about 1888, a Clarke County farmer named Lawrence whose given name nobody has been able to read — the hand that wrote it in 1900 gives “Calm.” They farmed at Coffeeville, and in 1900 she was thirty-six with five children in the house: Tomas N., Sallie E., Mary E., Senie E., and a baby, Alfonso, who was nought days old when the enumerator called. Two more followed, Lucy about 1905 among them — seven children in all.

By 1910 her husband had gone, and she was head of the household: “Elizon A. Larance,” with six children under her roof and no man in it. She kept them together for at least another decade.

She died in 1927, in her early sixties. At Ulcanush Cemetery in Coffeeville — the ground where her son Alphonso and her daughter Sennie lie — stands a marker that is very probably hers: a slab of rough concrete, the lettering scratched in by hand while it was still wet, reading Mrs Lawrence · Born 1864 · Died 1927. The 1864 is the year her own age gives. The stone does not give her name. What is written on it, and has been for a hundred years, is the word Mrs.

The index of the Clarke County death register gives her maiden name as Shoemake and names her mother as a woman called Polly Hutto; it records that she died at Coffeeville on 29 January 1927 and was buried at Ulcanush Church the next day. That comes from the index, not from the certificate itself, which has not been read.

Richard B. Tuttle (1858 – 1884)

Ahnentafel #48 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Richard B. Tuttle was born on 24 March 1858 at Tuttle’s Mills, in Otter Creek Township, Vigo County, Indiana, the fourth child of Clark Strong Tuttle and his second wife Nancy A. Harbert. He grew up in a prosperous miller’s house, with three hired hands and a servant living in, and with an older brother named Dennis — the name he would one day give his own son.

He followed his father’s trade and was a miller himself, but by the time he was grown the mill had gone: his father sold the 117-acre mill tract in July 1870, dissolved the firm a fortnight later, and was out of the township altogether by the autumn.

On 12 October 1881, at twenty-three, he married Emma S. Fuerstenberger, the daughter of a Bavarian shoemaker in Terre Haute. Some time in the next two years the young couple went to Peoria, Illinois, where their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born on 25 January 1884; why they were there is not known. The Terre Haute they came home to is the city of the bird’s-eye view drawn in 1880 — the grid of streets running back from the Wabash, the bridges, the mills along the river and the Normal School on its square.

He was dead before the year was out. Consumption took him on the morning of Wednesday 17 December 1884, at twenty-six, and the funeral was held at ten the next morning from his father-in-law Frank Fuerstenberger’s house at 521 South Third Street. No gravestone for him is known.

Emma S. Fuerstenberger (1863 Terre Haute – 1926)

Ahnentafel #49 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Emma S. Fuerstenberger was born at Terre Haute, Indiana, in July 1863, the fifth of the eight children of Francis Fuerstenberger, a Bavarian shoemaker, and his Pennsylvania-born wife Sophia. She grew up in the one house her father occupied for forty years, 521 South Third Street, and was still at home there at seventeen.

On 12 October 1881, at eighteen, she married Richard B. Tuttle, a young miller. Their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born at Peoria, Illinois, on 25 January 1884, and in December of that year Richard died of consumption in her father’s house. She was twenty-one, with a baby.

She married twice more: Hamilton B. Martin on 24 May 1891, and then, on 20 December 1894 at Charleston in Coles County, Illinois, Gifford H. Spear. It was Spear who finished raising her son, and the son repaid him by naming his own firstborn Gifford. In September 1916 she and Gifford Spear were living at 512 North Eighth Street in Terre Haute and had just come home from a month’s holiday in California.

Her elder sister Anna had married James H. Jumper at Terre Haute on 11 October 1876, before H. E. Wulzen, pastor of the German Methodist Episcopal church, with John Fuerstenberger signing as witness — the Fuerstenbergers were a German Methodist family five years before Emma’s own wedding. The Bible the Jumpers kept held what families keep: a slip of receipt paper noting which minister had baptised which child and, in a small white paper bag, some of young Elmer’s hair.

She died on 9 April 1926 as Emma S. Spear. The family Bible enters the death under that name, with “Tuttle” pencilled faintly above it — somebody wanting the record to remember which husband she belonged to. Eight years later her son stood in her place when a Fuerstenberger estate was divided five ways among her brothers’ and sisters’ lines.

William L. Alexander lived at Clinton, in Vermillion County, Indiana, on the Wabash upriver from Terre Haute. Mary Elizabeth Fletcher (d. 1934)

Ahnentafel #51 · his mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Elizabeth Fletcher was born in Indiana on 21 September 1858, the daughter of John Fletcher and Sarah Wallace. Where the family lived while she was growing up is not known.

She surfaces at Clinton, in Vermillion County, in 1880, aged twenty-three, single, and living in another family’s house as a “houseworker” — a domestic servant. A year later, on 16 June 1881, she married William L. Alexander of the same small town, and in February 1883 their daughter Leona was born. She bore one child and one only.

She was a widow by 1900, and she had not gone under. That June she was at 314 Mulberry Street in Terre Haute, forty years old, head of her own household, working as a housekeeper and owning the house — with her seventeen-year-old daughter beside her. By December 1902 she had moved to North Sixth Street, and it was in her house that her only child was married. After that she moved in with Leona and Dennis Tuttle and stayed for the rest of her life, more than thirty years under her daughter’s roof. She was still travelling with them in 1914, going out to West Terre Haute for the weekend in August and again in September.

In October 1910 her grandson Gifford’s seventh-birthday cake was a present from the boy’s grandmother, and she, living in the house, is the likelier of his two grandmothers to have baked it.

She died just after four o’clock on the afternoon of 16 April 1934, at the Tuttles’ place on the rural route out of Terre Haute at East Glenn, in Lost Creek Township, aged seventy-five. Her heart had been failing for twenty years. It was Leona who gave the particulars for the certificate — her mother’s birthday, and the names of the grandparents she had never known, John Fletcher and Sarah Wallace. She was buried at Highland Lawn two days later. The family Bible records it simply: “Mary Elizabeth Alexander (1934).”

Widowed, she lived in Dennis and Leona’s household in 1910, 1920 AND 1930 — a forty-year presence in the house that corroborates this generation independently of the register. Her death is in the family Bible.
