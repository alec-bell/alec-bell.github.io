Caswell Massey Stephens (1852 Sumter Co. – 1935)

Ahnentafel #40 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Caswell Massey Stephens was born on 8 January 1852 in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of Henry H. Stephens and Sarah Ann Jones. He was named, at the very least, for a neighbour: in 1850 a twenty-four-year-old North Carolina man called Caswell Massey farmed the dwelling that stood between the Stephens house and the Joneses’. Whether he was kin to either family nobody has yet established. His mother died before he was three, and his father married her sister Elizabeth in October 1854. By 1860 the family had moved to Newton County, Mississippi, where the census catches him at eight.

On the last day of 1869, not yet eighteen, he stood in the Sumter County probate court before Judge J. A. Abrahams, said he was over fourteen, and chose his own father as his guardian — he and his brothers Calvin and John were the “minor heirs of Daniel Jones deceased,” their mother’s father, and his bond was set at $800. There was enough property in it that his father was filing annual settlements two years later.

In October 1871, aged nineteen, he married a widow thirteen years his senior. The register enters her as “Mrs. A. M. James,” and the officiant was H. H. Stephens, Minister — his own father. Her gravestone never gives her a first name either: it calls her A. M., wife of C. M. Stephens. Their children included Martha Alice, born 14 April 1874, who married John William Knight, and John Willis, born 12 March 1876; a son Rufus L. belongs to one marriage or the other. She died on 8 January 1891 — his thirty-ninth birthday — and on 19 April that year he married again, near Cuba. The county paper gave the whole of it in eleven words: “Married — Near Cuba, on the 19th, Mr C M Stephens and Mrs I Dearman.” The bride was Isabella, a Britt by birth and a Dearman by her first marriage, and she was about forty-five.

He farmed on into old age at Halsell, in Choctaw County, just over the Sumter line. In May 1928 his son Rufus died intestate at Livingston leaving about a thousand dollars; the probate papers record the only heir as “The Father Caswell M Stephens of Livingston Ala., age 76 years,” and he declined to administer it. He died at eight o’clock on the morning of Sunday 10 February 1935 — the county paper of the 14th already reports him buried, though his stone was later cut with the 17th —, at the home of his daughter Mrs. Alice Knight, after a short illness. The Sumter County Journal ran the notice on its front page. Funeral services were at eleven on the Monday at Siloam Church, Revs. Ezell and Dearman officiating, and he was buried in the church cemetery at Siloam, where his first wife had lain for forty-four years. He left two sons, J. W. of Livingston and H. R. of Halsell, one daughter, two brothers — Bob Stephens of Kewanee and Bill Stephens of Cuba — and thirteen grandchildren and fifteen great-grandchildren.

The woman the family remembers as Ava Marilla Shelton was born on 21 November 1839, and nothing she left behind gives her a first name. Her gravestone calls her A. M.; the marriage register calls her Mrs. A. M. James; the initials are all there is.

She came to Caswell Massey Stephens as a widow. On 2 October 1871 — the alphabetical copy of the same register types the 5th — the Sumter County register entered “C. M. Stephens · A. M. Stephens, born Nov. 21, 1839, died Jan. 8, 1891, and below it four lines of verse now almost weathered away. Her husband’s flat granite marker lies in the grass beside her.

Francis R. Williams lived on the Mississippi side of the Alabama line, in the strip of pine country where Greene and Clarke Counties meet. He married Mary J. Lewis, and their daughter Mary Price Williams was born at State Line, in Greene County, on 18 March 1887 — a railroad village that takes its name from sitting flat on the Alabama border.

The one record that shows him keeping house is the 1900 census of Beat 3, Clarke County, Mississippi, where he appears as Francis R. Williams with his wife Mary J. and four children: Mary Price, Jesse F., Etta S. and Knox A. Six years later his daughter crossed the line into Sumter County, Alabama, to marry J. W. Stephens. Nothing else of his life has been found, and nothing at all of his parents; he carries the commonest surname in the American record, in a county whose nineteenth-century histories were never written.

William Curtis Truett (1867 – 1925 New Prospect AL)

Ahnentafel #44 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Curtis Truett was born on 25 July 1867, the son of John Wesley Truett and Emily Caroline Ward. He grew up at Chilton in Clarke County, Alabama, and the 1880 census catches the whole family on one page: his father, a Confederate veteran who could not write his own name; his mother; his brothers and sister; and his grandmother Alcey, widow of the Reverend William Hampton Truett, living in the house at sixty-seven. Three generations under one roof.

On 17 June 1886, not yet nineteen, he married Amanda Webb in Clarke County. The 1900 census finds them at Clarksville with fourteen years of marriage behind them and six children in the house — Tela, Jane, Bigy, Lillie, Tamie and the baby Bessie — and it records both parents as able to read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do. Thomas William, the “Tamie” of that schedule, was Alec's great-grandfather.

Amanda is gone from the record after 1900, and he married again, to Zella Shewmake, born 1 June 1878. He farmed at New Prospect, a settlement whose little Baptist church had been constituted about 1860 by an Elder C. J. Miles, who for his trouble was paid “one dollar per day while in active service.” When the county's thirty-four Baptist churches were apportioned in 1892, New Prospect was asked for sixteen dollars — near the smallest sum in the county.

All the places of his life sit within a few miles of each other on the 1928 county map — Chilton, where he grew up; Clarksville, where the census found him in 1900; and New Prospect, where he farmed, with Harris Creek running between them.

He died at New Prospect on 21 December 1925, aged fifty-eight, and is buried in the church graveyard. His stone is a low double marker of pale granite, TRUETT across the top, his own name on the right and Zella's on the left; she outlived him by thirty-five years and the marker was almost certainly cut when she joined him in 1960. Amanda, the mother of his eldest son, is not on it, and is not buried there. His own county paper printed nothing when he died, though the issues for the weeks after are all there to read.

Amanda Webb (1868 – aft. 1900 Clarke Co.)

Ahnentafel #45 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Amanda Webb was born in July 1868 in Alabama and married William Curtis Truett in Clarke County on 17 June 1886, when she was not quite eighteen and he not quite nineteen.

The 1900 census is the one full look anyone gets at her. On 19 June 1900 the enumerator James A. Coate Jr. called at dwelling 118 at Clarksville and wrote her down as Amanda, wife, born July 1868, aged thirty-one, fourteen years married, mother of six children and six living — and all six were in the house with her: Tela, thirteen; Jane, twelve; Bigy, ten; Lillie, eight; Tamie, six; and Bessie, one. Tamie is Thomas William, born that September of 1893, and Alec descends from him. Both she and her husband are marked as able to read and write, which neither his father nor his grandmother could do. Two dwellings down the same page lived a Tom P. Webb, born September 1862, a farmer — the right surname at the right door, though nothing yet says he was her brother.

After that she is gone from the record. Her husband married again, to Zella Shewmake, and it is Zella's name, not hers, on the other half of his gravestone at New Prospect. No grave has been found for Amanda anywhere.

The Webbs were old Clarke County people: a William Webb came into the county about 1812 and died at his home there in February 1853 at about seventy-eight, the Webb mill on the Tallahatta was built about 1836, and Webbs were among the families forted up at McGrew's Fort during the Indian troubles of 1810. Whether Amanda belonged to them has not been shown.

Richard B. Tuttle (1858 – 1884)

Ahnentafel #48 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Richard B. Tuttle was born on 24 March 1858, the fourth child of Clark Strong Tuttle and his second wife Nancy A. Harbert, at Tuttle's Mills in Otter Creek Township, Vigo County, Indiana. He first appears in the record aged two, in the 1860 census of Otter Creek, in a prosperous miller's house with three hired hands and a servant living in — and with an older brother named Dennis, aged four, whose name he would give his own son.

He followed his father's trade and was recorded as a miller. By the time he was grown the mill had gone: his father sold the 117-acre mill tract in July 1870, dissolved the firm a fortnight later, and was out of the township altogether by the autumn.

On 12 October 1881, at twenty-three, he married Emma S. Fuerstenberger, the daughter of a Bavarian shoemaker in Terre Haute. Some time in the next two years the young couple went to Peoria, Illinois, where their son Dennis Clark Tuttle was born on 25 January 1884; nobody has yet found out why they were there.

Terre Haute as he knew it survives in a bird's-eye view drawn in 1880, four years before he died: the grid of streets running back from the Wabash, the bridges, the mills along the river and the Normal School on its square.

They were back in Terre Haute before the year was out, and on the morning of Wednesday 17 December 1884 he died of consumption. He was twenty-six. The Daily Wabash Express carried the notice the next day under ANNOUNCEMENTS — DIED, and it says where he died: the funeral was at ten o'clock that morning from the house of his father-in-law Frank Fuerstenberger on South Third Street. No gravestone for him is known.

Benjamin Nott/Knott (~1817 OH – 1891 Marietta)

Ahnentafel #52 · his mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Benjamin Nott — the name is written Knott about as often as Nott, and the 1881 county history prints its own correction, “Benjamin Knott probably should be Benj. Nott, as per 1810 Federal Census of Wash. Co.” — farmed in Adams Township, near Lowell, in Washington County, Ohio. He was born about 1815–17 in Ohio. Nothing yet found names his own parents, though an elder Benjamin Nott “of Adams” died insolvent in 1822, leaving a widow Polly, and if that man was his father he was orphaned as a small boy on a broke farm on Calls Creek.

He married a Margaret; an index entry for a marriage of “Benj. Nott” to Margaret Kirk in Morgan County on 13 March 1836 is the best lead to her surname, but the image has never been read and a rival Benjamin married a Lavina Tewel in Washington County in 1839. The 1860 census shows the household at Lowell — Benjamin forty-three, a farmer; Margaret forty-three; P. J. fifteen; William F. thirteen; and Jos M eleven. That eleven-year-old is believed to be Joseph McGee Knott, whose 1932 death certificate names Benjamin and Margaret as his parents and Lowell as his birthplace; but the census gives no relationships and its implied birth year for Joseph is about two years out from the certificate, so the link is treated as probable rather than proved.

In July 1861, at forty-six, a Benjamin Nott of the county volunteered for Company A of the Thirty-sixth Ohio and was mustered out in 1864 — and it was him: a state register of soldiers’ graves, compiled in Ohio in the twentieth century, sets down the same service on his card, enlisted 29 July 1861, discharged 4 September 1864, private, Company A, 36th Regiment Ohio Infantry. By 1870 the family was in Marietta, where the census lists Benjamin fifty-three, Margaret fifty-two, John W. twenty-three, Sarah twenty, a baby Henry and three boarders. His last years were hard. In December 1890 the probate court recorded “P. J. Nott, guardian of Benjamin Nott, an Insane person” filing an inventory — the guardian was his own child, the P. J. of the 1860 household. He died between that December and the following 6 July, when the final account was settled: the estate was charged with nothing at all, credited with $10.91, and that sum was found due the child who had cared for him. He died at Marietta on 6 May 1891, of la grippe — the influenza that went round the world between 1889 and 1891 — and the register of soldiers’ graves lays him in Oak Grove Cemetery at Marietta, lot 90, block 19, under an upright stone.
