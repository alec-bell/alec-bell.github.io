Elvira Loveless (fl. 1876–1891 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #35 · his father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Elvira Loveless was born in Alabama in August 1849, of Alabama parents. At eighteen she married Henry Bradley of Barbour County on a licence issued at the courthouse on 18 December 1867 — “Henry Bradly and Elvira Loveless, both of full age” — and J. S. Williams, judge of probate, signed the authorisation to marry them the next day. Whoever married them never sent the licence back: the printed return underneath it, “I hereby certify that the above named persons were duly married by me on the ___ day of ___”, was left blank, so the county book records the authority and never the wedding.

She bore at least eight children and buried three of them — one at birth in 1870, a boy of eleven in 1879, a baby in 1884. Five grew up: Ida, born about 1871, who married Lee Parker; Annie, who married a Fortner of Dothan; a son Henry; and the two youngest, Alice, born 8 October 1876, and Katie, born in 1879. In 1880 the census-taker found her at Clayton, thirty-one and keeping house, with all five of them under her roof.

Her husband, the clerk of the circuit court, died of an old war wound in February 1887. Three years a widow, in January 1890 she bought Jake Fortner’s house at Mt. Andrew and moved the family there, and her daughter Annie was married in that house the following September. Then, on a Sunday afternoon in the summer after, it was her turn. She had dressed her children for Sunday school that morning; when she was taken ill they sent for the doctor, and sent too late. She died at Clayton on 14 June 1891 of cholera morbus, aged forty-one, and the paper called her “Mrs. Bradley, relict of former Clerk Henry Bradley,” leaving five children.

Four months later a Barbour County court took the two youngest, Alice and Katie, into guardianship. The order speaks only of the father, because that is the form of words the clerk used; in fact the girls had lost both parents.

She lies at Clayton City Cemetery beside her husband, inside the same iron rail, under a draped marble tablet with a star cut into the arch and a worn verse on the plinth. It gives her no surname at all — only ELVIRA, WIFE OF HENRY BRADLEY, born in August 1849 and dead in June 1891, the day-figures too worn now to be certain of.

Joseph William Eakins (~1838 NC – 1902 MS)

Ahnentafel #36 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph William Eakins — “Joel W.” on most of the paper and “William J.” on some of it — was born in December 1838 or 1839 in New Hanover County, North Carolina, a son of John Eakins Sr. and Nancy Costin. In 1850 he was twelve, one of nine children in his father’s house on the Moores Creek land the family had farmed since before the Constitution was ratified.

By the end of the 1850s the family’s reach had extended to Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where an uncle, William Eakins, had already settled, and where four orphaned Eakens boys born in North Carolina were being raised in the household of William Payne. On 13 May 1861, a month after the war began, Joel married Payne’s daughter Sarah Hudson Payne. The marriage happened inside a house that had already fused the two families for a decade.

Two of his North Carolina kin went the other way that same May: his brother John J. B. Eakins and his cousin Owen J. enlisted at Lower Black River in the Moore’s Creek Rifle Guards, Company E of the 18th North Carolina, and both were dead within two years — John at Sharpsburg, Owen at Chancellorsville.

He and Sarah farmed in Lauderdale County and had ten or more children. By 1880 the house held Mary A., Ada J., Jno. N., Minnie, Cassie E., Orgun, Joel P., Nancy and the newborn Andrew J.; a son Sam came later. Two of those names, Orgun and Joel, were Sarah’s own younger brothers’ names, carried over from the Payne household she had grown up in. In 1900, asked for his particulars, he gave his birth as December 1838, his birthplace as North Carolina and his marriage as 1861.

He died in 1902 in Mississippi, probably in Lauderdale County in the June of that year. His other lasting mark on the family was a piece of handwriting: he stopped dotting the “i” in Eakins, and his descendants have been Eakens, and then Eaken, ever since.

Sarah Hudson Payne was born on 2 May 1842 in Mississippi, and grew up in Lauderdale County in a crowded house. William Payne, who headed it, was fifty-nine in 1850 and Virginia born; his wife Mary was thirty and born in North Carolina; four boys born in North Carolina and carrying the Eakens name, aged ten to sixteen, were orphans being raised there; and four younger children had been born in Mississippi, of whom Sarah was the eldest. They farmed in Lauderdale County for forty years. She bore ten or more children: Mary A., Ada J., John N., Minnie, Cassie, Nancy, a Sam, and two she named Orgun and Joel after the brothers she had been raised with. She died on 1 May 1908, one day short of her sixty-sixth birthday. Her stone still stands over her in a fenced Mississippi burying ground, a leaning white marble obelisk behind a chain-link fence: SARAH H. Joseph L. Swain (1861 MS – 1940 York AL)

Ahnentafel #38 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (5 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lee Swain was born on 23 December 1861, the eldest child of Joseph Tolbert Swain and Nancy E. Jane, who had married in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, the year before. He was four or five when his parents crossed the state line into Sumter County, Alabama in the first months after the war, and he grew up at Cuba; in 1880, at nineteen, he stood at the head of a row of eight younger brothers and sisters.

He married twice over, and buried a wife in between. On 10 February 1884 he married Eugenia Simmons, with J. M. Patton the minister — and a son of his would be named Patton. A wife called Leila Eugenia died before the end of 1885 and lies in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, across the line in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, under a stone that gives 1861–1885 and calls her “wife of J. L. Swain”; whether she was the bride of 1884 nobody now knows. Then on 23 December 1885, his own twenty-fourth birthday, he married the woman the register enters as Rebecca A. D. Simmons and everyone else called Dillon. The minister that day was D. C. Langford, and they named a son for him too: Joseph Langford Swain.

They farmed at York and Cuba. In 1900 the children at home were Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford, and he gave his years married as sixteen — counting, it seems, from the first wedding rather than the second. In November 1905 he sold a hundred and sixty acres at the post-office door at Cuba as administrator of the estate of J. T. Swain, very probably his own father, dead the year before. He stood for public office as well: at a meeting of the voters of the third commissioners district at Livingston on 28 August 1911 the vote went fifty-seven to twenty-seven in his favour, and in September he announced himself from Cuba as a candidate for county commissioner, “subject to the action of the Democratic party.” The Livingston paper thought he would make a good commissioner.

Five of their children lie in the family burying ground at Salem Baptist, and the dates make a hard chapter of their life: Marvin, dead at two in 1892; Fletcher, dead at two in 1905; and then Patton, dead at nineteen in January 1916, Lucille Kirkland, dead at eighteen in January 1917, and Myrtle, dead at sixteen the following month. Three grown children in thirteen months.

Cuba, as it stood in 1928, was laid out building by building along one street: the Walker-Shaw planing mill and saw mill at one end, a grist mill and feed store, G. W. McElroy’s blacksmith and carpenter shop, the Cuba Mercantile Co., the Southern Railway depot with its covered platform, a Methodist church and two Holiness churches.

He lived out his life on the land three miles west of York, near the old McConnell Station — a six-room house on twenty acres, with running water and a telephone. He died there at a quarter to one on the afternoon of Thursday 21 November 1940, aged seventy-nine, after a long illness, and the funeral was held in the house the next afternoon. He left his widow, “Mrs. Dillon Simmons Swain,” four daughters — Leila Collier of Meridian, Maggie Bloebaum of Wheaton, Illinois, Pinkie McElroy of Fort Knox and Merle Cobb of Geiger — one son, Langford of Cuba, a brother, John M. Swain of York, and two sisters, Mattie Shamburger and Laura McDonald. A week later the widow and all five surviving children signed a card of thanks to their neighbours, styling him Mr. Joe L. Swain, Sr. He was buried at Salem Baptist; his stone gives his name as JOE L. SWAIN and, under the dates, one word: PAPA.
