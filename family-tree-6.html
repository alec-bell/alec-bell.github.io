William Rasor Bell (fl. 1784 – 1803/09 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #256 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

He appears in 1784 in Claremont County, already styled “Planter,” buying a neighbour’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle — and three years later giving every bit of it back to that neighbour’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in the language of that century usually means family. He farmed the Black River country beside Frederick Bell and the Newmans, proved deeds across the line in Darlington, and on the day after Christmas 1803, “very sick & weak in body,” divided a plain farmer’s world — a plantation, stock, feather beds, a cow already called his daughter Elizabeth’s — among a wife, two daughters and nine sons, the second of whom was Archibald.

Gershom Tuttle II (1738 Farmington Conn. – 1818)

Ahnentafel #384 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Revolutionary patriot

Gershom Tuttle II, remembered as "the Patriot," was born 22 August 1738 in Farmington, Connecticut (in the New Cambridge parish, now Bristol), and died 5 January 1818. During the Revolutionary War he served as a teamster, conducting wagon trains of army provisions — an operation that hauled some 3,000 barrels of supplies, roughly 500 tons, from Connecticut into New York State. The Connecticut General Assembly paid him for this service in 1780, and he is recognized today as Daughters of the American Revolution Patriot Ancestor A131791.

He married Tabitha Mitchell at Deerfield, Massachusetts, on 22 December 1757. The couple later moved first to New Hampshire, then to Clinton in Oneida County, New York, and finally to Watertown, New York, where Gershom served as the first deacon of the town's first Congregational church, organized in 1803.
