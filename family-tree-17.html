Lois Allis (b. 1707/8 Hatfield; m. 1737)

Ahnentafel #769 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Lois Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 13 January 1707/8, the second daughter of William Allis and his wife Elizabeth. While she was small the family moved up the Connecticut River to Sunderland, and it was there, in her father’s own parish, that she married on 17 October 1737. She was twenty-nine. Her husband, Gershom Tuttle, was a Connecticut man who had come north to take up land his father held in one of the new frontier townships; the town clerk entered them plainly — “Tuttle, Gershom, and Lois Allis, were m. Oct. 17, 1737.”

They went back to Connecticut, to the New Cambridge parish of Farmington on Chippen’s Hill, and the Farmington register fills in the next twelve years: Gershom in 1738, Oliver in 1739, Eunice in 1743, Hannah in 1745, Samuel in 1747, Huldah in 1749. A daughter born about 1751 was given her mother’s name. That younger Lois died on 5 January 1769, in her eighteenth year, and the stone raised over her at Bristol names both her parents — “Lois Daugtr of En[s]n Garshum & Mrs Lois Tutle” — the one contemporary monument that puts husband and wife on the same page.

The Allis connection held across two generations: her elder sister Mary had married Joseph Mitchell of the same valley, and in 1757 Mary’s daughter Tabitha married Lois’s son Gershom, so the cousins married and the same Allis parents stand twice in this pedigree.

She was still living in May 1777, when her husband made his will at Farmington and left “my wellbeloved Wife Lois” the use of a third of his estate for life. Where she died, and when, is not known. She is not buried beside him: the 1896 transcription of every ancient cemetery in Claremont, New Hampshire, found only three Tuttles there and none of them his wife, and the Bristol burying ground that holds her daughter does not hold her either.

Joseph Mitchell (1705 Deerfield – 1794 Ashfield)

Ahnentafel #770 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Mitchell was born in 1705, the third son of Michael Mitchell and Sarah Catlin, into a household that had come through the Deerfield raid of the year before unharmed while his mother’s family was all but destroyed. He was bred a weaver, not a farmer.

On 2 November 1726 he married Mary Allis, daughter of William Allis of Sunderland; their first child, a son Joseph, was born at Sunderland four months later. Then came daughters — Hannah, who married Zadock King in 1751; Mary, married in 1753; Thankful; Lydia; and, on 9 February 1736/7, Tabitha, who in 1757 married Gershom Tuttle of Farmington and so carried this line forward. There was a son Abner as well. The family moved from Sunderland to Deerfield, and after that to Ashfield, up in the hills to the west.

At Ashfield he seems to have kept the first public house in the town. From about 1763 a Joseph Mitchell — this man or his son of the same name — kept an inn on the east side of Bellows Hill; it was to “the house of Joseph Mitchell, innholder” that the voters were summoned for Ashfield’s first legal town meeting on 6 January 1766, where Joseph Mitchell was chosen constable, and the town went on meeting in his tavern until the meeting house was finished in January 1771. Joseph Mitchell was also one of the handful of men who signed the covenant when the church there was gathered. His daughter Mary’s husband, Samuel Belding, was the town’s first selectman and the man who served the warrant.

Mary died on 8 November 1783. Nine years later, on 14 April 1792, describing himself as of Deerfield, Joseph made his will. He gave twelve pounds each to his daughters Mary, wife of Samuel Belding, Hannah Bigelow, and “my Daughter Tabitha Wife of Gersham Tuttle”; eight shillings each to five grandchildren, the children of “my late Son Joseph”; and the land and the residue to his son Abner. He died on 16 September 1794, at Ashfield, and the will was proved at Northampton on 25 November.

John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #960 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there. The tradition carried down into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County — later Dunmore, then Shenandoah — and farmed it; the family says he kept a mill and a tannery as well, and more than thirty land transactions stand in his name or his sons’. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: the sons Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas, and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family has always said that nine sons went out of that house.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: he furnished supplies to the army between 1780 and 1783 and took a Shenandoah County claim certificate, number 2453, for the property impressed from him. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He was a Lutheran, and the family were tanners and builders of churches. He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. When the old burying ground’s stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868 his went with them, and it is kept upright there under glass with a Sons of the American Revolution patriot star beside it. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, two hundred years after his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

Alec’s descent reaches him three times over, because two of his grandchildren married each other. This is the first of the three paths, and it runs through his son George, the Revolutionary soldier who lies at Weldon Cemetery in Ohio — a son by the family’s own settled account rather than by the word of any surviving record.

Susannah married John Adam Roush about 1739, soon after he landed at Philadelphia, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #964 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there. The tradition carried down into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County — later Dunmore, then Shenandoah — and farmed it; the family says he kept a mill and a tannery as well, and more than thirty land transactions stand in his name or his sons’. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: the sons Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas, and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family has always said that nine sons went out of that house.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: he furnished supplies to the army between 1780 and 1783 and took a Shenandoah County claim certificate, number 2453, for the property impressed from him. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He was a Lutheran, and the family were tanners and builders of churches. He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. When the old burying ground’s stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868 his went with them, and it is kept upright there under glass with a Sons of the American Revolution patriot star beside it. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, two hundred years after his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

Alec’s descent reaches him three times over, because two of his grandchildren married each other. This is the second of the three paths, and it runs through his youngest son Jonas, who stood at Yorktown and watched Cornwallis’s army surrender — a son by the family’s own settled account rather than by the word of any surviving record.

Susannah married John Adam Roush about 1739, soon after he landed at Philadelphia, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. She was the mother of the whole American Roush family — ten children, seven of them sons, and every one of the sons went into the Revolution. Very little else of her life was ever written down. After his death the church’s list of communicants carries “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, and then never again.

She died at or about 1796, ten years after her husband, and was buried beside him at the Old Pine Church. “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked,” the family’s historian wrote, “but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When the old stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with them, because there was nothing to move. Alec descends from her three times over, her grandson John and her granddaughter Regina having married each other. This is the second of the three, through her youngest son Jonas, who stood at Yorktown.

John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #1000 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there. The tradition carried down into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County — later Dunmore, then Shenandoah — and farmed it; the family says he kept a mill and a tannery as well, and more than thirty land transactions stand in his name or his sons’. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah.” By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: the sons Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas, and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family has always said that nine sons went out of that house.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: he furnished supplies to the army between 1780 and 1783 and took a Shenandoah County claim certificate, number 2453, for the property impressed from him. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He was a Lutheran, and the family were tanners and builders of churches. He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. When the old burying ground’s stones were moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868 his went with them, and it is kept upright there under glass with a Sons of the American Revolution patriot star beside it. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, two hundred years after his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

Alec’s descent reaches him three times over, because two of his grandchildren married each other. This is the third of the three paths, and it runs through his son Henry Sr., the Church Builder who lies at Plants Cemetery in Ohio — a son by the family’s own settled account rather than by the word of any surviving record.
