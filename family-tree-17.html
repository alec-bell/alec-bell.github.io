Dea. Timothy Tuttle (~1682 New Haven – 1756 Wallingford)

Ahnentafel #1536 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Timothy Tuttle was born about 1682, probably at New Haven, the son of Simon Tuttle, a cooper, and his wife Abigail. He was roughly three when the family removed to Wallingford about 1685, after his grandmother’s death. He grew up there, and as a young man he went out west of the river to look for land, chose a spot, and cleared it with his own hands — staying out through the week because it was too far to walk home each night, and finding his way back on Saturdays along a line of trees he had blazed through the woods. The place was about a mile east of what became the centre of Cheshire.

He put up a house there about 1706, the same season he married: on 2 November 1706 he took Thankful Doolittle to wife at Wallingford. Family tradition made it the first frame house in Cheshire, and the town’s own historian thought it was more likely the second; under its roof, he wrote, were born the builder’s children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren and great-great-grandchildren. It stood empty until, not many years before 1883, it was burned by an incendiary.

His children by Thankful were Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simon in 1721 and Moses in 1723. His father gave him nine and a half acres on 10 July 1711. He turns up all through the west-side records: in 1715 the school district runs “as high as Timothy tuttle & timothy Beach’s,” and he signed that December’s petition of the farmers on the west side of the river. He was moderator of the very first meeting of the New Cheshire society on 16 September 1723, served on the committee to manage the ordination, was “Sergeant Tuttle” on the school-house committee in 1729 and society moderator in 1732, and by 1 December 1741 he is “decon Timothy Tuttle” on the committee to petition for town privileges.

Thankful died in November 1728. He married again in June 1729, to Mary, widow of Stephen Rowe, and for the last time on 28 June 1749, to Sarah (Atwater) Humiston. He had also taken a proprietor’s share in one of the new townships granted up in Hampshire County, Massachusetts, and in 1736 he signed the whole of that right over to his second son — “for divers good causes and considerations and perticularly for love, good will and fatherly affection that I have and [bear] to my son Gershom Tuttle.” It is the deed that sent the next generation north.

He died on 15 April 1756 and lies in Hillside Cemetery at Cheshire, under a brownstone carved with a winged soul effigy: “Here Lies ye Body of Deacon Timothy Tuttle he died April 15th 1756 in ye 75th year of his Age.”

Thankful Doolittle (1688 Wallingford – 1728)

Ahnentafel #1537 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Thankful Doolittle was born at Wallingford, Connecticut, on 5 April 1688, a daughter of John Doolittle and Mary Peck — a granddaughter, on her father’s side, of Abraham Doolittle, one of the men who founded the town. She married young. The Wallingford register, printed in the town history of Cheshire, has the entry plainly: “Timothy Tuttle m. Thankfull Doolittle November 2, 1706.” She was eighteen, and her husband built his house out west of the river that same season.

Her children came across seventeen years: Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simon in 1721, Moses in 1723. In March 1725 the new Cheshire church admitted her — the roll records her simply as “wife of Timothy Tuttle” — three years before she died. Her husband was not admitted until January 1738/9, ten years after her death.

She died on 23 November 1728, aged forty, with her youngest child five years old. Where she is buried is not recorded. Her husband married again thirteen months later.

Her brother was the Reverend Benjamin Doolittle, Yale 1716, settled as minister at Northfield on the Massachusetts frontier from 1718. When her son Gershom went north as a young man to take up his father’s land above Northfield, it was his uncle’s house he stayed in — and the compiler who recorded that called Benjamin Doolittle his uncle, which is how her motherhood is attested from outside her own family’s records.

Two things about her still rest on compiled authorities rather than on a record read at the page: her birth of 5 April 1688 and her death of 23 November 1728. Jacobus, the 1883 Tuttle genealogy and Beach’s Cheshire all agree on them, and the dates sit exactly in the gap this family’s own chronology predicted; but the Wallingford entries themselves have not yet been opened.

William Allis (1680 Hatfield – 1763 Montague)

Ahnentafel #1538 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 19 October 1680, the third child of Samuel Allis, the carpenter and builder of Hatfield street, and his wife Alice. His father died when he was ten; his mother remarried and moved six miles up the road to Deerfield, and on the morning of 29 February 1704 she was killed there in the French and Indian raid, along with his brother Samuel. His sister Mary was carried off toward Canada and came home again. William was twenty-three, and he was not in Deerfield that night.

He had married Elizabeth Davis of Northampton in 1703 or 1704. Their children were Mary, born 18 February 1705, and Lois, born 13 January 1707/8, both at Hatfield; Eliphalet in 1710, also at Hatfield; then Zebediah in 1713 and Elizabeth in 1716, both at Sunderland — so the household moved upriver between the end of 1710 and the autumn of 1713. At Sunderland he appraised a neighbour’s estate in 1739 and deeded land to his son Zebadiah. Later the family moved again, to Montague on the other side of the river, where the town register calls him “Mr. William Allis” — a title of standing — when it recorded his wife’s death on 1 May 1758. The same register shows how hard that place could be: in January 1759 his son Eliphalet and his wife Mary buried four of their children in nine days.

William Allis died at Montague on 20 February 1763, aged eighty-two. He stands in this family’s pedigree twice over. His daughter Lois married Gershom Tuttle in 1737; his daughter Mary married Joseph Mitchell in 1726; and in 1757 Mary’s daughter married Lois’s son, so two of his children’s lines run down to the same descendants.

Elizabeth Davis (m. 1703/4 – 1758 Montague Mass.)

Elizabeth was of Northampton, Massachusetts, and married William Allis of Hatfield in 1703 or 1704. She is named as the mother on all five of their children’s records — Mary 1705, Lois 1707/8 and Eliphalet 1710 at Hatfield, then Zebediah 1713 and Elizabeth 1716 after the family moved upriver to Sunderland. Simon Tuttle was baptised at New Haven on 28 March 1647, the ninth child of William Tuttle, the immigrant, and his wife Elizabeth. He was bred a cooper. Samuel Allis (1647 Braintree – 1691 Hatfield)

Ahnentafel #3076 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Samuel Allis was born at Braintree, Massachusetts, on 24 February 1647. The town register enters him under the year 1646, because the year then began in March and February was reckoned the twelfth month: “Samuel, sonne of William Alyes & Mary his wife was Borne the 24th. of ye 12 month.” In the Old Style of the time the date is written 24 February 1646/7. His father, William Allis, was a Braintree freeman of 1640 who removed about 1661 up the Connecticut River to the new town of Hatfield.

Samuel followed him there and took his own home lot as a young man of twenty-two. On 4 February 1669 the town granted him a house lot sixteen rods wide next to Samuel Marsh’s, with land in the Plain above the great meadow and ten acres in the swamp by the Mill river — “provided he build a sufficient dwelling house upon his lot by or before the last of May in the year 1671, otherwise to forfeit his land.” Daniel Belden had the lot on the other side. He built, and he stayed: a carpenter and builder, living on the east side of Hatfield street.

About 1675–6 he married a woman named Alice, whose surname no record has ever given. Seven children followed: Mehitable on 2 July 1677; Samuel on 20 February 1679; William on 19 October 1680, from whom this family descends; Mary on 6 July 1682; Thomas on 12 March 1684; Sarah about 1685; and Rebecca on 29 November 1687.

Samuel Allis died at Hatfield on 9 March 1691, aged forty-four, leaving Alice with seven children all under fifteen. She remarried in 1696 and was killed in the raid on Deerfield of 29 February 1704; their son Samuel was killed in the same attack, and their daughter Mary was carried to Canada and survived to come home.
