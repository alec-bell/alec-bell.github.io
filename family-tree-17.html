Joseph Mitchell (1705 Deerfield – 1794 Ashfield)

Ahnentafel #770 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Mitchell was born in 1705, the third son of Michael Mitchell and Sarah Catlin, into a household that had come through the Deerfield raid of the year before unharmed while his mother’s family was all but destroyed. He was bred a weaver, not a farmer.

On 2 November 1726 he married Mary Allis, daughter of William Allis of Sunderland; their first child, a son Joseph, was born at Sunderland four months later. Then came daughters — Hannah, who married Zadock King in 1751; Mary, married in 1753; Thankful; Lydia; and, on 9 February 1736/7, Tabitha, who in 1757 married Gershom Tuttle of Farmington and so carried this line forward. There was a son Abner as well. The family moved from Sunderland to Deerfield, and after that to Ashfield, up in the hills to the west.

At Ashfield he seems to have kept the first public house in the town. From about 1763 a Joseph Mitchell — this man or his son of the same name — kept an inn on the east side of Bellows Hill; it was to “the house of Joseph Mitchell, innholder” that the voters were summoned for Ashfield’s first legal town meeting on 6 January 1766, where Joseph Mitchell was chosen constable, and the town went on meeting in his tavern until the meeting house was finished in January 1771. Joseph Mitchell was also one of the handful of men who signed the covenant when the church there was gathered. His daughter Mary’s husband, Samuel Belding, was the town’s first selectman and the man who served the warrant.

Mary died on 8 November 1783. Nine years later, on 14 April 1792, describing himself as of Deerfield, Joseph made his will. He gave twelve pounds each to his daughters Mary, wife of Samuel Belding, Hannah Bigelow, and “my Daughter Tabitha Wife of Gersham Tuttle”; eight shillings each to five grandchildren, the children of “my late Son Joseph”; and the land and the residue to his son Abner. He died on 16 September 1794, at Ashfield, and the will was proved at Northampton on 25 November.

Mary Allis (1705 Hatfield – 1783)

Ahnentafel #771 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Mary Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 18 February 1705, the eldest child of William Allis and Elizabeth his wife, and three years older than her sister Lois. The family moved upriver to Sunderland while the girls were small.

On 2 November 1726 she married Joseph Mitchell, a weaver of the Deerfield Mitchells; their first son was born at Sunderland four months after the wedding. Six more children followed — Hannah, Mary, Thankful, Lydia, Abner, and Tabitha, born 9 February 1736/7 — and the household moved from Sunderland to Deerfield and in the end to Ashfield. She died on 8 November 1783; Sheldon’s Deerfield genealogies, which give the date, call her seventy-seven, though by her own birth entry she was seventy-eight.

Her life joins this family’s tree twice. In 1757 her daughter Tabitha married Gershom Tuttle, the son of her sister Lois — first cousins marrying — so William Allis and Elizabeth Davis stand in two places on the same pedigree, and Mary is an ancestor of this family alongside her own sister.

John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #960 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there; the tradition the family carried into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County, later Dunmore and then Shenandoah, and farmed it; the family says he also kept a mill and a tannery. His wife was Susannah. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah” — the one period land record that names them both. By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: seven sons who can be documented one by one — Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas — and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family’s own voice says nine sons, and its own table says seven; the seven are the ones the wills and the pension files agree on.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: the Library of Virginia holds the Shenandoah County impressed-property claim certificate, number 2453, for supplies he furnished the army between 1780 and 1783. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear — and it is now kept upright under glass at St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson, where the old burying ground’s stones were moved in 1868. It reads: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, on the two hundredth anniversary of his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

It calls the family tanners and builders of churches.

This is the line that reaches him through his son George, the Revolutionary soldier of Weldon Cemetery — the first of three paths on Alec’s chart that end at this one man. That George was his son is the settled family account, stated in four separate places in the family’s own history, and the brothers’ wills and pension files document the sibling group around him; no surviving document names the father in his own words. His 1786 Shenandoah County probate, if it exists, would do it in a stroke.

Susannah Roush (d. ~1796 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #961 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah was the wife of John Adam Roush, the immigrant, and the mother of the whole American Roush family. Her maiden name has not been recovered; the surname Sehler, or Schlern, circulates on family trees and on her gravestone-era monument, but no source for it has ever been produced, so this project does not carry it.

She married John Adam in about 1739, soon after he landed, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. Her documentary life is four moments long. In 1773 the recital of a four-hundred-acre grant on Mill Creek names the land as made “to him and his wife Susannah.” In 1770, and again on 5 January 1786 — nine months before her husband died — she stood as a baptismal sponsor at the Old Pine Church, the second time as a grandmother. And then the church’s own list of communicants records “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, after which she never appears again.

She died, then, at or near 1796, about ten years after her husband, and the family’s historian put it plainly: “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked, but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When his stone was moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with it, because there was nothing. In 1936 the Roush and Allied Families Association cut her name into the bicentennial monument there — “1713 – HIS WIFE, SUSANNAH – 1796” — which is the only stone that carries it.

Alec descends from her three times over. This is the first of the three, through her son George.

Michael Zerckel (Zerkel/Zirkle) (Shenandoah Co., Virginia)

Ahnentafel #962 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Michael Zerckel — the name is written Zirkle, Zerkel, Zerchel, Sircle, Circle and Zickel in the records, and twice differently on his own family’s gravestones — came south with the Zirkle brothers from Pennsylvania into the Shenandoah Valley. His parents were Ludwig Zirkle and Eve; a deed of 1776 by which he and his wife Catherine sold land in Dunmore County describes fifty-four acres “patented to my mother, Eve Sircle, now deceased … and descended to Michael as eldest son and heir of Eve.”

He settled near Forestville, on Holman’s Creek, in the country that became Shenandoah County, in a neighbourhood where the Zirkles, the Neases and the Roushes lived within a few miles of each other and married one another for a century. His brother Andrew built the stone mill at Quicksburg that still stands. In 1776 Michael’s own name appears on a list of the Dunmore County militia under Captain Jacob Holman — the same captain under whom Henry Roush had served the year before.

He married Catharine, and they had fourteen children. The eldest, Catharine, married George Roush; another daughter, Mary, born 20 June 1770, married Henry Nease in June 1791, and the marriage papers carry her father’s consent signed “Michel Corckel”; his son Henry followed the Roushes and the Neases down the Ohio to the Graham District of Mason County and founded the Zirkle family there. His children’s marriages are a large part of why the Roush, Nease and Zirkle families are braided together in Alec’s tree in three separate places.

A cracked slab in the Zerchel–Nehs burying ground, on the east bank of Holman’s Creek northeast of Forestville, is very probably his — though it should be said in the same breath that the photographs of it are photographs of older prints rather than of the stone itself, that the memorial which carries them is openly unsure which of two graveyards holds him, and that the woman buried beside him raises a problem nobody has yet settled: her stone says she was born in July 1747, which would make her thirteen to fifteen when Michael’s eldest daughter was born on the reconstruction this project adopted, and sixteen on the 1928 book’s printed date of 14 August 1763 — so either the book’s date is right and she was a very young mother, or the alternative nobody has excluded is true and Cadarina was Michael’s second wife, not the mother of the Catharine who married George Roush. The slab is cut in German, with a Pennsylvania-German compass star at its head, and it reads HIR RUET MICHAEL ZICKEL IST GEBOREN DEN 29 JANER 1735 UND GESTORBEN DEN 2 OCTOBER 1811 UND SEIN ALTER WAR 76 JAR 8 MO 3 DA — born 29 January 1735, died 2 October 1811, aged seventy-six years, eight months and three days. What identifies it as his is the name, the Zirkle family’s own graveyard in his own neighbourhood, and the matching stone beside it. The ground has long been unfenced. “The old graveyard used to have a good many tombstones in it,” a Forestville man wrote shortly before his own death, “but for many years it has been unfenced. John Adam Roush (1711 Palatinate – 1786)

Ahnentafel #964 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot · Immigrant

John Adam Roush — Johannes Adam Rausch — was born in 1711 in the Palatinate, and the family has always said Darmstadt. He crossed to Philadelphia in the closing months of 1736 and took the oath of fidelity there; the tradition the family carried into the twentieth century was that he and two companions, forbidden to emigrate, were hidden inside hogsheads by a friendly cooper, loaded aboard, and let out at sea. He landed too poor to pay his passage, hired himself out near Philadelphia, stayed on past his term because he was paid unusually well, and then married and went down the Shenandoah Valley.

He settled on Mill Creek in what was then Frederick County, later Dunmore and then Shenandoah, and farmed it; the family says he also kept a mill and a tannery. His wife was Susannah. In 1773 a grant of four hundred acres on Mill Creek was made “to him and his wife Susannah” — the one period land record that names them both. By then he had been in the valley more than thirty years and had raised a large family: seven sons who can be documented one by one — Philip, John, Jacob, Henry, Daniel, George and Jonas — and the daughters Mary Magdalene, Eleanor and Elizabeth. The family’s own voice says nine sons, and its own table says seven; the seven are the ones the wills and the pension files agree on.

Every one of those sons went into the Revolution, and so, in his sixties, did he: the Library of Virginia holds the Shenandoah County impressed-property claim certificate, number 2453, for supplies he furnished the army between 1780 and 1783. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him for it as Patriot Ancestor A098982. One son was a captain with two of his brothers in the ranks; two of them substituted for each other; one of them witnessed another’s pension application fifty years later.

He died on 19 October 1786 near New Market in Shenandoah County and was buried at the Old Pine Church. His gravestone survives — a rough slab cut in German by someone who spelled by ear — and it is now kept upright under glass at St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson, where the old burying ground’s stones were moved in 1868. It reads: ANO 1711 GEBOHREN JOHANNES RAUSCH GESTORBEN DEN 19 OCTOBER 1786. The Adam had already dropped out of his name. Susannah outlived him by ten years and lies beside him in a grave that was never marked. In 1936, on the two hundredth anniversary of his crossing, the Roush and Allied Families Association raised a monument over the two of them that names their children one by one and reckons their descendants, living and dead, at about forty thousand American citizens.

This is the line that reaches him through his youngest son Jonas, who stood at Yorktown — the second of three paths on Alec’s chart that end at this one man, because two of his grandchildren married each other. That Jonas was his son is the settled family account and is stated in four separate places in the family’s own history, including a Lutheran baptismal entry of 5 January 1786 in which John and Susannah Roush stand as grandparents; no surviving document names the father in his own words.

Susannah was the wife of John Adam Roush, the immigrant, and the mother of the whole American Roush family. Her maiden name has not been recovered; the surname Sehler, or Schlern, circulates on family trees and on her gravestone-era monument, but no source for it has ever been produced, so this project does not carry it.

She married John Adam in about 1739, soon after he landed, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. Her documentary life is four moments long. In 1773 the recital of a four-hundred-acre grant on Mill Creek names the land as made “to him and his wife Susannah.” In 1770, and again on 5 January 1786 — nine months before her husband died — she stood as a baptismal sponsor at the Old Pine Church, the second time as a grandmother. And then the church’s own list of communicants records “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, after which she never appears again.

She died, then, at or near 1796, about ten years after her husband, and the family’s historian put it plainly: “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked, but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When his stone was moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with it, because there was nothing. In 1936 the Roush and Allied Families Association cut her name into the bicentennial monument there — “1713 – HIS WIFE, SUSANNAH – 1796” — which is the only stone that carries it.

This is the second of three paths back to her on Alec’s chart, through her youngest son Jonas; her grandson John and her granddaughter Regina married each other, which is why she appears more than once.

This is the third of the paths on Alec’s chart that end at this one man, and it comes through his son Henry Roush Sr., the Church Builder of Plants Cemetery. The family’s history introduces Henry as “one of the oldest of the children of John and Susannah Roush,” and the 1936 monument carves his name among the children; as with George and Jonas, the link is as well attested as a single family tradition can make it, and awaits his father’s 1786 Shenandoah County probate to become a record.

Susannah Roush (d. ~1796 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #1001 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah was the wife of John Adam Roush, the immigrant, and the mother of the whole American Roush family. Her maiden name has not been recovered; the surname Sehler, or Schlern, circulates on family trees and on her gravestone-era monument, but no source for it has ever been produced, so this project does not carry it.

She married John Adam in about 1739, soon after he landed, and went with him down into the Shenandoah Valley to the farm on Mill Creek. Her documentary life is four moments long. In 1773 the recital of a four-hundred-acre grant on Mill Creek names the land as made “to him and his wife Susannah.” In 1770, and again on 5 January 1786 — nine months before her husband died — she stood as a baptismal sponsor at the Old Pine Church, the second time as a grandmother. And then the church’s own list of communicants records “Susannah Roush, widow” in 1788, 1790, 1792, 1795 and 1796, after which she never appears again.

She died, then, at or near 1796, about ten years after her husband, and the family’s historian put it plainly: “The grave of the wife, Susannah, is not marked, but there is no reason to doubt that she lies by his side.” When his stone was moved to St Mary’s Pine Church Cemetery at Mount Jackson in 1868, nothing of hers went with it, because there was nothing. In 1936 the Roush and Allied Families Association cut her name into the bicentennial monument there — “1713 – HIS WIFE, SUSANNAH – 1796” — which is the only stone that carries it.

This is the third of the three paths back to her, through her son Henry Roush Sr., the Church Builder.
