Thankful Doolittle (1688 Wallingford – 1728)

Ahnentafel #1537 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Thankful Doolittle was born at Wallingford, Connecticut, on 5 April 1688, a daughter of John Doolittle and Mary Peck — a granddaughter, on her father’s side, of Abraham Doolittle, one of the men who founded the town. She married young. The Wallingford register, printed in the town history of Cheshire, has the entry plainly: “Timothy Tuttle m. Thankfull Doolittle November 2, 1706.” She was eighteen, and her husband built his house out west of the river that same season.

Her children came across seventeen years: Ephraim in 1710, Mary in 1712, Gershom in 1714, Timothy in 1716, Simon in 1721, Moses in 1723. In March 1725 the new Cheshire church admitted her — the roll records her simply as “wife of Timothy Tuttle” — three years before she died. Her husband was not admitted until January 1738/9, ten years after her death.

She died on 23 November 1728, aged forty, with her youngest child five years old. Where she is buried is not recorded. Her husband married again thirteen months later.

Her brother was the Reverend Benjamin Doolittle, Yale 1716, settled as minister at Northfield on the Massachusetts frontier from 1718. When her son Gershom went north as a young man to take up his father’s land above Northfield, it was his uncle’s house he stayed in — and the compiler who recorded that called Benjamin Doolittle his uncle, which is how her motherhood is attested from outside her own family’s records.

Two things about her still rest on compiled authorities rather than on a record read at the page: her birth of 5 April 1688 and her death of 23 November 1728. Jacobus, the 1883 Tuttle genealogy and Beach’s Cheshire all agree on them, and the dates sit exactly in the gap this family’s own chronology predicted; but the Wallingford entries themselves have not yet been opened.

William Allis (1680 Hatfield – 1763 Montague)

Ahnentafel #1538 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 19 October 1680, the third child of Samuel Allis, the carpenter and builder of Hatfield street, and his wife Alice. His father died when he was ten; his mother remarried and moved six miles up the road to Deerfield, and on the morning of 29 February 1704 she was killed there in the French and Indian raid, along with his brother Samuel. His sister Mary was carried off toward Canada and came home again. William was twenty-three, and he was not in Deerfield that night.

He had married Elizabeth Davis of Northampton in 1703 or 1704. Their children were Mary, born 18 February 1705, and Lois, born 13 January 1707/8, both at Hatfield; Eliphalet in 1710, also at Hatfield; then Zebediah in 1713 and Elizabeth in 1716, both at Sunderland — so the household moved upriver between the end of 1710 and the autumn of 1713. At Sunderland he appraised a neighbour’s estate in 1739 and deeded land to his son Zebadiah. Later the family moved again, to Montague on the other side of the river, where the town register calls him “Mr. William Allis” — a title of standing — when it recorded his wife’s death on 1 May 1758. The same register shows how hard that place could be: in January 1759 his son Eliphalet and his wife Mary buried four of their children in nine days.

William Allis died at Montague on 20 February 1763, aged eighty-two. He stands in this family’s pedigree twice over. His daughter Lois married Gershom Tuttle in 1737; his daughter Mary married Joseph Mitchell in 1726; and in 1757 Mary’s daughter married Lois’s son, so two of his children’s lines run down to the same descendants.

Michael Mitchell (Wallingford Conn. → Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #1540 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Michael Mitchell is first found at Wallingford, Connecticut, in 1695–6, where he held a proprietor’s standing and cast lots with the other townsmen, and he was there again in 1706. Where he came from before that nobody has been able to say: Sheldon’s Deerfield genealogies begin with him, Jacobus gives him four words, and Savage is silent. He married, about 1694, Sarah, daughter of John Catlin of Deerfield.

He moved his family to Deerfield, the most exposed town on the Massachusetts frontier. On the morning of 29 February 1704, when the French and their allies came over the stockade, his whole family escaped unharmed — a sentence Sheldon only had to write because so many of their neighbours did not. His wife’s father and brother were burned in their own house a few hundred yards away.

His children were William, born 1697; Michael, born 8 March 1700/1, afterwards a soldier in Father Rasle’s war and later of Wethersfield, Connecticut; Joseph, born 1705, the weaver, who is this family’s line; and Samuel, born 7 March 1712, very probably the Samuel Mitchell granted land at Sunderland in 1737 on condition he stayed five years. Sarah died on 30 December 1736.

His Wallingford years matter more than they look. The Tuttles were farming west of the river at Wallingford in exactly those years, and Gershom Tuttle was born there in 1714 — so when Michael’s granddaughter Tabitha married Gershom’s son at Deerfield in 1757, it was not a chance meeting a hundred miles from home. It was two Wallingford families who had both gone north, marrying each other.

Sarah Catlin (m. ~1694 – d. 1736)

Ahnentafel #1541 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (10 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sarah Catlin was a daughter of John Catlin — a Wethersfield man who taught school at Newark in New Jersey, served as that town’s attorney and five years its selectman, and then brought his family to Deerfield about 1683, where he was dignified with the title of “Mr.” — and of his wife Mary Baldwin. About 1694 Sarah married Michael Mitchell, and her eldest child was born in 1697.

That marriage is probably why she lived. On 29 February 1704, when the raid came, she was under her husband’s roof and the Mitchell household came through untouched. Her father’s did not. His buildings were all burned and he and his son Jonathan were consumed with them; another son was killed in the Meadow Fight later the same morning; a son and three daughters were carried off toward Canada. Two of those daughters were her sisters Elizabeth, wife of James Corse, and Mary, wife of Thomas Wells, and both were killed on the march. Sheldon, writing of a town where a hundred and twelve people were marched away in a morning, says of her father that “no family suffered more than his.”

She bore at least four sons — William, Michael, Joseph and Samuel — and died on 30 December 1736, about two months before the birth of her granddaughter Tabitha. She is said to lie in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, where the grave can no longer be picked out.

Simon Tuttle was baptised at New Haven on 28 March 1647, the ninth child of William Tuttle, the immigrant, and his wife Elizabeth. He was bred a cooper. Alice (m. 1675/6 – killed 1704 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #3077 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (11 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice married Samuel Allis, the Hatfield carpenter, about 1675–6, and bore him seven children between 1677 and 1687 — Mehitable, Samuel, William, Mary, Thomas, Sarah and Rebecca. When he died on 9 March 1691 she was left with all of them under fifteen.

On 20 November 1696 she married again, to Sergeant John Hawks of Deerfield: a soldier of King Philip’s war who had been in the Falls Fight and was wounded going to the relief of Hatfield on 30 May 1676, and who had become a permanent settler at Deerfield in 1683. She had a daughter by him, Elizabeth, born about 1697.

On the morning of 29 February 1704 the French and their allies came over the Deerfield stockade. Alice was killed in the village. Her son Samuel Allis, twenty-five years old, was killed in the meadow. Her little daughter Elizabeth Hawks, six or seven, was taken and died on the march to Canada. Her daughter Mary Allis, twenty-one, was also carried off, survived the march, was redeemed, and came home to marry another survivor of the same raid, Nathaniel Brooks, in 1710. Her husband lived, but as Sheldon wrote of him, all his children and grandchildren of the name were lost that day; he ended his life at Waterbury, Connecticut, with the one daughter he had left. This family’s line runs through her third child, William, who was at Hatfield and not in Deerfield that night.

She lies with the rest of the dead of that morning in the Old Burying Ground at Deerfield, under a stone that reads: “The grave of 48 men women and children, victims of the French and Indian raid on Deerfield, February 29, 1704.” Her own family name was never written down. The 1919 Allis genealogy leaves a literal blank where the surname should go; Boltwood and Sheldon, both working from the Hampshire County records, call her only “Alice, widow of Samuel Allis.” She is remembered by her first name alone.

Samuel Davis (~1645 Roxbury – 1690 Northampton)

Ahnentafel #3078 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (11 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Samuel Davis was born about 1645, probably at Roxbury, Massachusetts — Savage calls him “perhaps son of William of the same” — and a Roxbury church record of 1649 lists a Samuel Davis among William Davis’s children. He removed up to Northampton in 1668 and was made a freeman there in 1676.

He kept choosing the exposed edge. Besides his Northampton land he held shares in the valley’s silver and lead mine venture, which he sold to Medad Pomeroy, and he put his name down as a subscriber to Harvard College. In 1682 he was a grantee of Northfield — a town that had already been burned twice — and in 1685 he was among the men who went back to rebuild it. In March 1676 his wife was one of a long list of Northampton women fined for wearing silk “in a flaunting manner.”

He married twice. His first wife is named in Northfield’s town history as Hannah Edwards, married 4 March 1668 and dead on 11 September 1680; his second was Elizabeth, daughter of William Smead, whom he married in 1681. Savage records two sons — Samuel, born 1672, and John, born 1677 — and three daughters whose names he does not give. Samuel Davis died at Northampton on 26 July 1690, in the epidemic that swept the town that summer.

He is on this chart as the father of Elizabeth Davis, who married William Allis of Hatfield in 1703–4. That link is probable rather than proved: he is the only Davis household of his generation in Northampton, which is where the Allis genealogy says Elizabeth came from, but no record yet names her as his daughter.
