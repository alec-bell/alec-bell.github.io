Jonas Roush (1763 Frederick VA – 1850 Mason VA)

Ahnentafel #482 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Jonas Roush was born in September 1763 at Holman’s Fort in Frederick County, Virginia — soon to be Shenandoah County — the youngest of the sons of John Adam and Susannah Roush. He was seventeen when his turn came in the Revolution, and he told the story himself, on oath, before the court at Mason County, Virginia, on 6 November 1832.

He “entered the service as a substitute for his Brother Henry Roush who was drafted for a tour of three months in the summer of 1781,” served under Captain All in Colonel Bird’s regiment at Falmouth near Fredericksburg, and went out again on a second three-month tour. That tour took him to the siege of Yorktown, and he was there on 19 October 1781 when Cornwallis’s army surrendered. He fell ill near Fredericksburg while escorting prisoners. His pension was allowed at twenty dollars a year from 4 March 1831 as claim S4785, and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as Patriot Ancestor A098985.

He married Barbara Fox, of a Shenandoah County family settled near Forestville. Their daughter Regina, born 30 November 1788, married her cousin John Roush in 1808 — the marriage that doubles back through Alec’s tree, so that Jonas and his brother George both stand as his ancestors. Their other children were Abraham, John, Adam and Philip.

Like his brothers he came down the Ohio, and he was living in Mason County, Virginia when he applied for his pension. That same year, 1832, his brother Daniel made him executor of a will leaving the residue of the estate “equally divided among the children of my brothers Jonas, George, Henry, Philip and Jacob.” Barbara died in the mid-1830s, and in 1837 he gave up his own household, moved his pension across to Meigs County, Ohio, and spent his last years in his daughter Regina’s house in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township. Whether he died on the Ohio bank or back on the Virginia one has never been settled; the two places are opposite sides of the same water.

He died on 14 December 1850, aged eighty-seven, and lies in Gilmore Cemetery at Minersville, Meigs County, Ohio, the burying ground of that settlement. John and Regina were laid beside him in graves that were never marked. His own stone is still there, worn but standing, and two later Revolutionary War markers have been set by it: an upright government headstone, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT / VA TROOPS / REV WAR / 1763 1850,” and a flat bronze, “JONAS ROUSH / PVT VA REGIMENT / REVOLUTIONARY WAR.”

Barbara Fox was born about 1766 into a Fox family — Fuchs, in the older spelling — settled near Forestville in Shenandoah County, Virginia, prominent enough in the neighbourhood to stand among the leading names of the district in the first federal census. She married Jonas Roush, probably in Shenandoah County, in the 1780s.

Her daughter Regina, born 30 November 1788, married her cousin John Roush in 1808, and is Alec’s ancestor twice over; there were other children — Abraham, John, Adam and Philip — and the family moved down the Ohio with the rest of the Roush connection into Mason County, Virginia.

She died on 21 October 1836, aged seventy, by the stone that stands over her in Gilmore Cemetery at Minersville, Meigs County, Ohio: “BARBARA / WIFE OF / JONAS ROUSH / DIED / OCT 21 1836 / AGED / 70 YEARS.” It is very probably hers, though it carries no maiden name. Her husband’s pension papers put her death a few months later, in February 1837, and nobody has reconciled the two. Either way she was dead before Jonas gave up his household and crossed the river to live with Regina, and he was buried in the same ground fourteen years after her.

Henry Roush Sr. (1752 – 1831 Plants Cemetery)

Ahnentafel #500 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

Henry Roush was born in 1752 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, one of the oldest of the children of John Adam and Susannah Roush.

He was twenty-three when the war began, and he was in it from the first season: a private in Captain John Tipton’s company, eighty-eight days’ service, paid off at Romney on a receipt dated 25 October 1775 for £6 12s. In the summer of 1781 he was drafted again and his younger brother Jonas went in his place — Jonas swore to it half a century later, applying for his own pension. The Daughters of the American Revolution carry Henry as Patriot Ancestor A098977.

He married Dorothea about 1777 and they raised ten children in Shenandoah County near Solomon’s Church — Michael, Christina, Henry Jr., Elizabeth, Susannah, Anthony, Dorothy, Eva and Balser among them. Anthony, born 23 January 1792, is the son through whom this family descends. Henry gave the two and a half acres the church and its graveyard stand on, and in 1793 he put his hand to the Solomonburg deed, signing “HENRICH ROUSH” in German on the same page where his wife made her mark. It is why the descendants who later marked his grave called him the Church Builder.

He followed the Roush migration down the Ohio — to Mason County in 1798, and on to Letart Falls in Meigs County, Ohio, about 1800 — and he settled his children on land as he went: a deed to Henry Jr. in October 1805, seventy-five acres to Anthony for three hundred dollars in July 1812, and in June 1817 land to his son Balser and to his daughters Susanna Linscott and Catharine “for natural love and affection.”

He died on 26 October 1831, aged seventy-nine, and lies in Plants Cemetery above the river at Plants, Meigs County. The little stone set over him then — “Henry Roush died October 26, 1831, aged 79 years” — has been joined by a granite block carrying a plaque from his descendants: “HENRY ROUSH / 1752-1831 / ‘THE CHURCH BUILDER’ / ONE OF NINE BROTHERS / WHO FOUGHT IN THE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / ERECTED BY HIS DESCENDANTS.” A bronze tablet at its foot reads “HENRY ROUSH / PVT CAPT TIPTON’S CO / VA STATE TRP / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / 1752 † 1831,” naming the very company he had served in fifty-six years before he died. The markers were dedicated by the Sons of the American Revolution at the Roush family’s annual meeting on 19 August 1933, the same afternoon as his brother George’s, a few miles down the Ohio.

Dorothea Roush (fl. 1793)

Ahnentafel #501 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Dorothea — Dorothy to the neighbours who wrote her name down, Dolly in the family — married Henry Roush Sr. in Shenandoah County about 1777. Her maiden name has never been recovered; the family thought she was a Nease, Nehs as that family spelled itself in German, and there were Neases standing as baptismal sponsors for her children.

She bore ten children in the neighbourhood of Solomon’s Church — Michael, Christina, Henry, Elizabeth, Susannah, Anthony, Dorothy, Eva and Balser among them. Anthony, born in January 1792, carries this line. In 1793 she witnessed the Solomonburg church deed: her husband signed his name in German, and she signed “DOROTHY ROUSH (her mark)” on the same page. In 1796 a John Nease was serving as a church trustee beside Henry and Dorothea.

She went with the family down the Ohio to Letart Falls in Meigs County, Ohio, and she died about the same time as Henry, in 1831 or near it. She lies beside him in Plants Cemetery. Her stone was already past reading when the family went looking for it a century later — the “inscription … is now too far gone to be recovered, but it seems undoubtedly to be his wife Dorothy” — and a broken, wholly illegible stone still stands beside his monument today. Her grave counts as unmarked.

John Bell (~1710/30 – 1786 Camden SC)

Ahnentafel #512 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Whether this John Bell belongs to Alec’s family at all is unproved, and the whole line above him waits on it — but a life can be made out of him all the same.

He sat on Bear Swamp in Duplin County, North Carolina, before the Revolution, seated there by 1762 on good swamp-edge land among a kin-group of Bells, Kings, Snells and Herrings who had come down together out of Bertie County in the late 1740s. In 1765 he sold his plantation to his eldest son Frederick for a hundred pounds and went on living on it; he was still “Sr” in the Duplin papers in January 1773, and then he is gone from North Carolina altogether. Somewhere after that he made the four hundred miles south into the Black River country of South Carolina, where his son Frederick was settled by May 1777, and on 18 October 1786 he made his will in Camden District.

The will names his wife Mary, his eldest son Frederick, and his eldest daughter Penelope Stuckey, and it hands money to two more men, a John Bell and William Rasor Bell, without saying what either was to him. Frederick administered the estate.

His daughter had married John Stuckey of Duplin, and her life did not go easily: Stuckey and three others lost a judgment to the administrators of William Kenan for £1,658 11s 0½d, and the sheriff sold his plantation on the west side of Bear Swamp out from under him. A Duplin deed of 1822 was still tracking the ground as land “formerly owned by John Stuckey, which said land was sold by virtue of an execution.” But his grandson Lewis Stuckey had something of the old man’s: in a deed of 1792 Lewis says his property “descended to me from my Grandfather John Bell,” and by 1794 Lewis is “now of the County of Salem, South Carolina” — the family having followed the same road.

If he is who the Bertie County papers suggest — a son of Robert Bell of Chowan and Bertie, who died intestate in 1738 — then his own father could not write. Robert signed with a big interlocked R B, while John and his brothers all signed their names.

Gershom Tuttle I (1714 Wallingford – 1777 Claremont)

Ahnentafel #768 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gershom Tuttle was born at Wallingford, Connecticut, on 11 August 1714, the fifth child of Timothy Tuttle — the deacon who had cleared his own farm west of the river — and Thankful Doolittle. He grew up in the Cheshire parish, and as a young man he left Connecticut altogether for the Massachusetts frontier above Northfield, where his father held a proprietor’s right in one of the new townships. He lodged for a time at Northfield with his mother’s brother, the Reverend Benjamin Doolittle, who was minister there. In 1736, when he was already settled up in Hampshire County, his father signed the whole of that right over to him — given, the deed said, for “love, good will and fatherly affection.”

A year later, on 17 October 1737, he married Lois Allis in her own family’s town of Sunderland. By 1739–40 he was buying land back at Farmington, Connecticut. He had gone home with a whole colony of Cheshire people who settled on Chippen’s Hill in the remote parish of New Cambridge — the place that is now Bristol — and there he was prominent in church and town affairs. In October 1742 he put his name to the petition of the inhabitants of New Cambridge asking for a winter preacher; it went down as “Gerfhum Tuttle.” His children came in a steady file: Gershom in 1738, Oliver in 1739, Eunice in 1743, Hannah in 1745, Samuel in 1747, Huldah in 1749, and a daughter Lois about 1751.

He served in the French and Indian Wars, and the rank he carried out of them stayed with him for the rest of his life: he was Ensign Gershom Tuttle at Bristol in 1761 and still Ensign in 1770, and that is how his daughter Lois’s gravestone names him when she died on 5 January 1769 in her eighteenth year — “Lois Daugtr of En[s]n Garshum & Mrs Lois Tutle.”

After that he moved the family north a second time, to Claremont, New Hampshire, and this time he stayed. The spring of 1777 took his small grandson Daniel, Oliver’s boy, buried at four years old on 2 March. On 9 May Gershom made his will. He left his “wellbeloved Wife Lois” the use of a third of his estate, half his household goods to his daughter Eunice, wife of Abner Matthews, and the other half to his “Four motherless Grandchildren Hannah Gurdon Asa and Louisa Johnson,” and divided everything else between his three sons, Gershom, Oliver and Samuel, with his son Gershom as sole executor. He died on 13 August 1777, two days after his sixty-third birthday, in the second summer of the Revolution, while that son was away conducting provision teams for the Connecticut Assembly; the will was proved at Farmington on 27 September. He lies in Union Cemetery at Claremont, section B, lot 106, under a slate stone cut with a winged soul effigy: “In Memory of En[sig]n Gershom Tuttle … A Man of Sober Life, Who Lived beloved & Lamented died.”

Lois Allis (b. 1707/8 Hatfield; m. 1737)

Ahnentafel #769 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (9 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Lois Allis was born at Hatfield, Massachusetts, on 13 January 1707/8, the second daughter of William Allis and his wife Elizabeth. While she was small the family moved up the Connecticut River to Sunderland, and it was there, in her father’s own parish, that she married on 17 October 1737. She was twenty-nine. Her husband, Gershom Tuttle, was a Connecticut man who had come north to take up land his father held in one of the new frontier townships.

They went back to Connecticut together, to the New Cambridge parish of Farmington on Chippen’s Hill, and the next twelve years were given over to children: Gershom in 1738, Oliver in 1739, Eunice in 1743, Hannah in 1745, Samuel in 1747, Huldah in 1749. A daughter born about 1751 was given her mother’s name. That younger Lois died on 5 January 1769, in her eighteenth year, and the stone raised over her at Bristol names both her parents — “Lois Daugtr of En[s]n Garshum & Mrs Lois Tutle.”

The Allis tie held across two generations. Her elder sister Mary had married Joseph Mitchell of the same valley, and in 1757 Mary’s daughter Tabitha married Lois’s son Gershom — the cousins married, and the same Massachusetts grandparents stand twice in this family.

She was still living in May 1777, when her husband made his will at Farmington and left “my wellbeloved Wife Lois” the use of a third of his estate for life. Where she died, and when, is not known. She is not buried beside him at Claremont, New Hampshire, and she is not in the Bristol burying ground that holds her daughter.
