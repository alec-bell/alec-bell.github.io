Elizabeth (Rickard) Roush (m. ~1810 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #251 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Elizabeth Rickard was a sister of the Rickard brothers of Mason County, in what was then Virginia, and about 1810 she married Anthony Roush, a young man of the Shenandoah Valley Roushes whose family had settled in the same neighbourhood on the Ohio. Who her own parents were is not known.

She bore Anthony a long family, among them Nancy, born on 16 February 1832, the eleventh, who married George Nease in 1848 at sixteen. Elizabeth was dead, or the marriage was over, before Anthony married Catherine Hoffman.

No date of birth and no date of death has come down for her, and her grave is not identified — though the burying ground at White Church, where Anthony lies, carries her family’s name. Of her own life nothing survives but that one phrase, “a sister to the Rickard brothers,” and the children she raised.

William Rasor Bell (fl. 1784 – 1803/09 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #256 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

William Rasor Bell appears out of nowhere in 1784, already styled planter, in Claremont County of old Camden District, South Carolina. In June of that year he helped appraise Isaac Higgs’s estate in Kershaw District; in April 1787 he bought Micajah Stokes’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle, for £105 15s — and the very next day began giving it all back to Stokes’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in the language of that century usually means family. Ann was conveyed twice in two days between two households that were probably kin; she is the only person he is documented ever to have held, and he held her for a day. His own 1790 household was ten people and no one in slavery.

He had two surveys in 1785 — a hundred and fifty acres on Bare Branch in April, four hundred on Lynches Creek in June — and the grants on both issued to Samuel Boykin, who had paid the treasury fee, as was the local habit. His land lay on both watersheds at once: a later grant describes eighty acres “on Charleston Road between Black River and Lynches Creek, bounded N.W. by William Rasor Bell and Fred:k Bells Land.” A 1787 grant to Frederick Bell recites its north-east line as “Land Surveyed for Wm Rasor Bell, Frederick Bell, Jonathan Newman and Lewis Mathews” — one line of one document carrying three surnames that stay beside this family for three generations.

There is a single sheet of paper that might make him a Patriot. Book G, stub number 30, in South Carolina’s Revolutionary indents: “Issued the 22d. April 1784 To Mr. William Bell for Eleven pounds Eight Shillings and Seven pence Sterling for two Horses impressed for Militia Use in October 1780.” It is the only William Bell indent in the state that no man has ever been attached to, and no lineage society claims it. Nothing yet ties its claimant to him rather than to one of at least six other South Carolina William Bells, and the original stub, read at Columbia in 2026, carries no district, no auditor and no assignee to help.

On the day after Christmas 1803, “very sick & weak in body,” he made his will. It divides a plain farmer’s world among a wife and eleven children: the plantation and stock to Mary for her life, to be sold at her death or remarriage and divided among nine sons — “John Bell, Archibald Bell, Thomas Bell, William Bell, James Bell, Drury Bell, Jesse Bell, Benjamin Bell, & Robert Bell” — and feather beds to two daughters, Mary and Elizabeth, Elizabeth also getting “One Cow & Calf which is now call’d hers,” the only line in the document about a child’s own particular possession. The witnesses were Lewis Bell, Mary Stuckey and a Douglas.

He was dead by 1809. His executor Moses Gordon never served, and administration with the will annexed went on 15 April 1809 to Charles Williams of Georgetown District. Jonathan Newman — the man Archy Bell had witnessed for in 1805 — appraised the estate. The sale of the land that would have named all nine sons in one instrument has never been found, and the fiduciary volumes that would carry it are unfilmed paper in Columbia.

His own parents are unidentified. The leading frame is the Raccoon Swamp Bell clan of Albemarle Parish in Surry and Sussex counties, Virginia, where the patriarch’s 1746 will names five sons and no William; if he belongs there at all it is as an unnamed grandson. And the name Rasor — almost certainly his mother’s or grandmother’s family — has never been found anywhere.

Mary was the wife of William Rasor Bell of Salem, in Sumter District, South Carolina, and she outlived him. His will of 26 December 1803 left her the plantation and the stock for her lifetime “for the benefit of my children, if she remains single” — the land to be sold at her death or her remarriage and the money divided among their nine sons. Nothing else of her is certain. A “Mary x Bell” signs by mark on a Darlington-proved family deed of 1794 beside William Rasor, John and Frederick Bell, and that may be her or may be Frederick’s wife. William Pigford Sr. (d. 1803 New Hanover Co. NC)

Ahnentafel #290 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

He was, if his family's own story is true, a small boy found alive on the North Carolina coast sometime between 1735 and 1745 after a shipwreck. He could tell them only that his name was Billie Pigford and that he had sailed from England with his parents, who were never found. A Dutch family took him in. In March 1740 or 1741 the county court dealt with him formally: Rebecca Perry petitioned to be allowed to keep the boy herself, and the court bound him instead to Benjamin Fussell. He was called Lost Billie for the rest of his life, and his great-granddaughter was still telling the story in 1896.

Whatever he came from, he ended a landowner on both banks of Sill's Creek, in the Penderlea country where New Hanover County runs up against Duplin — and in 1790 his was the only Pigford household in the whole state. He married a woman named Sarah, remembered in the family only as “a woman of Dutch descent,” and they raised a large family: William, Jacob, Timothy, Solomon, Edward, Martha and Margaret, and probably a daughter Elizabeth, who married an Eakins of Moores Creek.

On 27 March 1801 he had his will written out. It gives Sarah all the household furniture and moveable effects and everything else not otherwise left, “to be by her disposed of amongst her children as she may think proper,” together with the plantation he lived on and all the land east of Sill's Creek for the term of her life. Timothy got the land on the west side of the creek. Solomon got the home place after his mother's death — and in return was bound to help his brother Timothy build “a dwelling house, kitchen, corn crib, smoke house, dairy, and in clearing ten acres of land.” To the rest of the children he left one shilling sterling each. He could not sign it. The paper ends “William X Pigford (his mark),” witnessed by William Jones.

He died before March 1803, when the New Hanover Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions proved the will — and had to do it the slow way, since the witness himself was not there to swear to it: William Bloodworth stood up instead and swore that the signature was in William Jones's own hand. Both the Sons and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as a patriot for signing a 1779 petition from the inhabitants of New Hanover, Duplin and Bladen counties, asking that the county be divided so that militiamen could reach their musters; in 1779, though, there were two William Pigfords in the county, father and son, and which of them put his name to that sheet is not known. He and Sarah lie somewhere in the Penderlea area in unmarked graves.

Gershom Tuttle II (1738 Farmington Conn. – 1818)

Ahnentafel #384 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Revolutionary patriot

Gershom Tuttle was born on 22 August 1738 at Farmington, Connecticut — in the parish of New Cambridge, which later became the town of Bristol. His father was Ensign Gershom Tuttle and his mother was Lois Allis.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield in Massachusetts, he married Tabitha Mitchell, and he married inside his mother’s family: Tabitha’s mother Mary Allis and his own mother Lois were sisters, so bride and groom were first cousins. The wedding was at Deerfield because that was where the bride was at home; the clerk styled him “of New Cambridge”, the outsider of the two.

His father died in 1777, leaving a will made that May which gave the widow Lois a third for life and divided the rest among “my Three Sons Gershom, Oliver and Samuel Tuttle,” and appointed “my Son Gershom Tuttle whole and sole Executor.” The account of “Gershom Tuttel Executor” was allowed in January 1779.

He is a patriot of the Revolution, and his war was logistics rather than battle — which in Connecticut in 1780 mattered as much. He was a conductor of teams: he ran wagon trains of provisions out of Connecticut into New York State, some three thousand barrels, about five hundred tons, averaging a hundred and fifteen miles a haul. On 23 May 1780 the Connecticut General Assembly paid him £285.13.8 in wages and £60.13.11 for forage in advance, and still owed him £200.2.8; he stands first of the nine conductors on the list. The Daughters of the American Revolution honour him as ancestor A131791, rank teamster — “Conductor of teams transporting supplies for the army.”

After the war he moved north to Claremont, New Hampshire, where his father had died, and on 17 December 1789 he signed a town recommendation of Ambrose Cossitt as justice of the peace, with his brother Oliver and his son Solomon; in 1790 he was keeping house there. About 1790 he went on to Clinton in Oneida County, New York, and afterwards joined the settlement at Watertown in Jefferson County. His family’s book made him the first deacon of Watertown’s first church, organised in 1803, although the deaconship of 1808 may really have belonged to his son. He lived to see that son become a colonel of militia there.

He died on 5 January 1818, in his eightieth year. Where he died, and where he lies, nobody now knows.
