William Tuttle (England → New Haven 1635 – d. 1673)

Ahnentafel #6144 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Tuttle came out of England on the ship Planter, which left the port of London in April 1635 and put into Boston about the first of July. He travelled with his wife Elizabeth, their first three children, and — in the same passage — two brothers and their households. The passenger list calls him a husbandman, a man who farmed his own ground; a petition surviving in Boston shows him among the merchant owners of the ketch Zebulon of Ipswich, asking the General Court for two guns to arm her for a voyage to the Indies. The court refused.

Two of his sons were baptized at Charlestown, Jonathan in 1637 and David in 1639, and by June of that year the family had moved to the new colony at Quinnipiac. On 4 June 1639 the planters met in Mr. Newman's barn and signed the church covenant that founded New Haven; William Tuttle's name is on that list for the first time, standing between Benjamin Ling and Ezekiel Cheever, near the head among the principal men. In the first division of land in 1640 he was rated at £100 of estate and seven persons in the family. In 1641 he held the home lot of Edward Hopkins, afterwards governor of Connecticut, and in 1656 he bought from Joshua Atwater a house, home lot and barn on the green. That became the Tuttle homestead, and the family held it for thirty years. Long after, the first building of Yale College was raised on the same ground.

He had land across the water at East Haven as well, enough of it that a stream there is still called Tuttle's Brook and the rise above it Tuttle's Hill. In 1651 he joined Jasper Crane and about fifty others in the New Haven colony's attempt to plant a settlement on the Delaware; the Dutch governor at Manhattan seized the party, held them prisoner and sent them home, and the petition of protest that followed — “the humble petition of Jasper Crane, William Tuttle and many others” — went to the commissioners of the United Colonies. The venture cost him money he did not recover, and after it he turned to farming and to town business.

The colony records catch him at that business constantly: fence viewer for Mr. Davenport's quarter in 1644, laying out allotments and settling boundaries; a juror again and again, his title of “Mr.” always carefully written, which in a New England town record is a rank and not a courtesy; sworn constable in March 1666-7; one of the commissioners who finally settled the line between Branford and New Haven in 1669 and between New Haven, Milford, Branford and Wallingford in 1672. When the meeting house was seated in 1646-7 — the seats graded by descent, public trust, piety and estate — Mr. Tuttle was voted into the first cross seat at the upper end, nearest the pulpit. They catch him in smaller things too. In 1646 he and Jeremy Watts were fined for falling asleep at the watch house. In 1664 a girl caught pilfering tried to drag his fourteen-year-old daughter Mercy into it; the charge collapsed, and before sentence William Tuttle asked the court, “with great affection,” to deal gently with the girl and place her in a pious family.

He died suddenly in the first days of June 1673, leaving no will; his inventory was entered just after that of his neighbour Benjamin Ling, taken on the sixth. A deed he had agreed to was found unsigned. His widow Elizabeth called herself his lawful administratrix, and his Branford division lands were still being parcelled out to his children on 14 February 1705, thirty-two years after he died — his son Simon's deed of that year conveys “all my right in Branford division set to my father, Mr. William Tuttle, now (Feb. 14, 1705) laid out for his children.” The last remainder of the estate was not divided until 1709 — thirty-six years after his death — when it was split into eleven parts, with a grandson appealing the division to Hartford. He lies somewhere under the Old Green at New Haven; the exact spot is not known. Among his descendants is his great-grandson Jonathan Edwards, who studied and taught on the very ground where William Tuttle's house had stood.

Elizabeth Tuttle (~1609 England – 1684 New Haven)

Ahnentafel #6145 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Elizabeth Tuttle was born in England about 1609. She was about twenty-six when she crossed to New England in April 1635 on the ship Planter with her husband William and their first three children — John, Hannah and Thomas — in a party that also held her husband's two brothers and their families and his widowed mother. They landed at Boston about the first of July, and a year later, on 24 July 1636, “Mrs. Elizabeth Tuttell” united with the church in Boston. The same church register records her bringing two sons to be baptized: Jonathan in July 1637 and David in April 1639. Within weeks of that second baptism the family had gone to the new plantation at Quinnipiac, which became New Haven.

She bore twelve children and, in a century when that was rare, every one of them lived to grow up: John, Hannah, Thomas, Jonathan, David, Joseph, Sarah, Elizabeth, Simon — from whom this family descends — Benjamin, Mercy and Nathaniel. Her own standing in the town is legible in the dullest of records. When New Haven seated its meeting house in 1646-7, the seats graded by rank, Mrs. Tuttle was placed in the fifth seat in the middle among the wives of the leading men; in 1655-6 in the fourth long seat; in 1661-2 the clerk simply wrote “Sisters Miles, Peck, Lindon, Tuttle and Gibbard.”

Her husband died suddenly in June 1673, leaving no will. Elizabeth took out the administration herself — at a court in June 1674 she appeared to confirm a sale her husband had made and is entered as “widow and relict and lawful administratrix,” signing with her mark. She stayed on at the homestead on the green, where she had lived since 1656, and kept it for another eleven years; in all she lived there twenty-eight.

The widowhood was hard. In November 1676 her daughter Sarah, married to John Slauson of Stamford, was killed; later writers quoting the New Haven court record say she was struck down by her own younger brother Benjamin after a quarrel, and that Benjamin was hanged at New Haven in June 1677. This project has not seen that court record itself. The family's own 1883 genealogy records only the bare dates — Sarah dead on 17 November 1676, Benjamin unmarried, making over his property to his brother-in-law Richard Edwards on 18 June 1677 — and says nothing of how either of them died.

Elizabeth spent her last years with her youngest son, Nathaniel, and died on the last day of 1684. The will she left was presented to the New Haven court on 28 July 1685 and refused: “the other children objected and the court would not allow it.” Her inventory, taken that February, was thought by a later reader to show “a ladylike refinement in apparel and household appointments.” Her gravestone, cut with the old-style date “Age 76: 31:10:1684 — Elisabeth Tutle” (month ten being December under the old calendar), was carried with the others from the Old Green to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821 and stands today in the row along the north wall. Part of its epitaph has crumbled away, but it was copied before it went: “The blest live as They are: the blest That live at Rest.”

Abraham Doolittle (~1620 – 1690 Wallingford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #6148 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s father (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Immigrant

Abraham Doolittle was born in England about 1620 and was in New England by 1640. He was at Boston in 1640 and at New Haven by 1644 — the monument later raised over him says 1642 — and from there he never left the colony's service: corporal, freeman, marshal of New Haven Colony from 1662 until the colony was swallowed by Connecticut, townsman, sergeant of the first train band, deputy to the General Court for New Haven and later for Wallingford, town treasurer. In the earliest records the name is written “Dowlittle.” On 10 November 1669 he sat as a juror at New Haven on the same panel as William Tuttle and Roger Alling — his own brother-in-law — in a court presided over by the deputy governor.

That same year he was chosen to superintend the affairs of the new settlement inland, and he became one of the founders of Wallingford. The town historian puts him and John Peck on the spot two years before the town was incorporated: “Abraham Doolittle and John Peck were on the ground in 1668.” He was one of four trustees who managed the plantation until Wallingford was incorporated in 1670, one of the committee that gathered its first church, and deputy for it in 1668 and 1672. During King Philip's War his house was one of those fortified, and he served on the town's vigilance committee in 1675.

He married twice. His first wife was Joan Alling, by whom he had the children of the 1640s and 1650s, including John, born 14 June 1655, from whom this family descends; she died about 1661 and was buried at New Haven. On 2 July 1663 he married Abigail Moss, daughter of his fellow Wallingford founder John Moss. In all there were thirteen children, and when he died on 11 August 1690, aged seventy, seven sons and three daughters survived him; his will names his wife, the seven sons and the three daughters. He lies in the Center Street Cemetery at Wallingford, where the town replaced his worn original stone in 1918 with a tall granite monument that sets out his whole public career. Which of his children belonged to which wife is a question later genealogists have had to reason out; the identification of John as a child of the first marriage rests on their reading rather than on a surviving record.

Joan (Alling/Allen) (bap. 1617 Kempston, Beds.)

Ahnentafel #6149 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father’s mother (12 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Joan Alling was baptized at Kempston in Bedfordshire on 12 October 1617, a daughter of James Alling. Her brother was Roger Alling, who became treasurer of the New Haven Colony and a deacon of its church — so when she crossed the ocean she was coming to a family already planted near the top of the colony's affairs.

She was the first wife of Abraham Doolittle of New Haven, and the mother of the children born in the 1640s and 1650s: Abraham, Elizabeth born 12 April 1652, Mary, John born 14 June 1655 — the son from whom this family descends — and Sarah. She died about 1661, before her husband's second marriage in July 1663, and she was buried at New Haven; where, is not known. The town of Wallingford remembered her all the same. When it raised a new monument over Abraham Doolittle in 1918 it cut a line for her near the top: “First wife Joane Alling is buried in New Haven.”

Her place in the family is reasoned rather than recorded. That the children born before 1663 belonged to a first wife and not to Abigail Moss is settled several ways over — Abigail was only about fourteen when John was born — but the naming of that first wife as this Kempston-baptized Joan rests on one genealogist's work. No marriage record for Abraham and Joan survives anywhere.

John Peck (~1638 – 1724 Wallingford Conn.)

Ahnentafel #6150 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father (12 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Peck was born about 1638, the son of Deacon William Peck of New Haven, the London merchant who had come over on the Hector. He grew up in the colony his father helped found, and became a freeman there in 1669. On 3 November 1664 he married Mary Moss, daughter of John Moss, in New Haven.

In 1668, two years before Wallingford was incorporated, he and Abraham Doolittle were already on the ground there — the town historian puts them in the same sentence — and John Peck settled in the new inland town and stayed. The first of his ten children were born at New Haven and the last six at Wallingford; the eldest, Mary, born 4 March 1666, married John Doolittle, so that the two men who had broken the ground together became grandfathers to the same children.

John Peck died at Wallingford in 1724, about eighty-six years old; Mary outlived him by a year, dying 16 November 1725 aged seventy-eight. They lie in the Center Street Cemetery, in a plot that is now the ninth stop on the town's walking tour, but no stone for either of them survives — none was found when the burying ground was transcribed in 1934, and the two markers standing in the plot today belong to a later John Peck and his wife.

On 20 December 1680 he made his will, and its first words say exactly where he was and how he felt: “In the Name of God Amen the twentieth day of december, One thousand Six hundred and eighty, I Joseph Baldwin Senr of Hadley in the Countie of Hampshire within the Colony of the Massachusetts: being together with old age and of life now Sick and weake of body but of good and perfect Memory, thanks and praise be to Almighty God for it.” He left his wife the use of his house and land at Hadley for her life, excepting the half of his lands he had already made over to his son Joseph; the other half, with the houses and the moveables, went to that son. Then he went through the daughters one by one — five pounds to his “Loveing Daughter Hanah Hull” and five pounds to her daughter Mary Hull when she should reach eighteen; forty shillings to “my Daughter Mary Cattlin”; a legacy to Elizabeth Warriner; something to the children of his daughter Sarah Bartlett, already dead; five pounds each to Martha Hawks and Hannah Hawks — and confirmed to his three sons Benjamin, Jonathan and David the gifts he had made them out of his Milford lands. He signed it before two witnesses, Chiliab Smith, who made his mark, and Samuel Partrigg.

Nearly four years later, on 30 October 1684, he had the will read over to him again and ratified it with some further provisos, appointing his grandson Joseph Baldwin junior his sole executor in place of the son, who had died. He lived only a few days more. The probate clerk headed the inventory “An Inventory of the Estate of Joseph Baldwin Senr who deceased November 3d, 1684, taken this Novembr 8th 1684 by Lt Smith, Chilliab Smith and S. Partrigg” — a day later than the 2 November that the printed genealogies give him. It came to £165 8s 7d, of which his house, barn and home lot at Hadley were valued at £50 and ten acres of land at £45, with debts of £10 14s 3d, leaving a net estate of £154 14s 4d. In the town he had helped to found at the other end of the colony, Milford set a granite boulder by the Wepawaug bridge among its founders' memorials: JOSEPH BALDWIN, OBIT 1690, HANNAH HIS WIFE. The date on it is wrong by six years, and his own probate file says so.

Deacon William Peck (1601 London – 1694 Lyme Conn.)

Ahnentafel #12300 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (13 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

William Peck was born in 1601 and was a merchant in London. In 1637 he sailed for New England on the Hector with his wife Elizabeth and their young son Jeremiah, in the company of Theophilus Eaton, Edward Hopkins and the Reverend John Davenport; the ship reached Boston on 26 June. He went with that company to Quinnipiac, and in June 1639 he signed the Fundamental Agreement that founded New Haven. He was made a freeman there on 29 October 1640.

His home lot was on George Street and his shop fronted on Church Street. He was never a rich man — his estate shows it — but he was a trusted one. He was one of three men who inventoried the estate of Sergeant Thomas Jeffrie in 1661. In December 1662 he stood up at a New Haven town meeting to tell it that the town's elders were not getting enough to eat: there was not wheat and malt enough to go round. With Roger Alling he was appointed a trustee of the fund, £918 in all, that paid for the colony's collegiate grammar school. From 1659 until his death — thirty-five years — he was deacon of the First Church of New Haven, and that is the name he kept.

Elizabeth died in December 1683, and about a year later he married Sarah, the widow of William Holt; his will, drawn 9 March 1689, mentions the second wife and his four children, all of them Elizabeth's. He died in October 1694 while staying at the house of his son Joseph at Lyme, more than forty miles from home. His stone was moved with the rest from the Old Green to Grove Street Cemetery in 1821 and still stands against the north wall there. It settles, in its own worn letters, a date that the books had muddled: “Deacon William Peck, aged 93, deceased Octobr 14, 1694.”
