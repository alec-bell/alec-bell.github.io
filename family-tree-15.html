Susannah Bish (1818 Fairfield OH – 1908 Illinois)

Susannah Bish was born on 17 November 1818 in Fairfield County, Ohio, about eighteen miles east of Columbus, a daughter of John and Ann Elizabeth Bish. She was brought up among Lutherans, of Dutch or German descent, had what her family called “a very limited education in the old fashioned ‘deestrict schools’,” and learned “the various mysteries of house keeping.” In 1836 she came with her parents to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township.

On 23 March 1837, four months past her eighteenth birthday, she married Elias Lamb at her father’s house. Her dowry to the “joint stock company” was about twenty-five dollars’ worth of goods, including a heifer calf. They kept house first on his father’s farm, then on farms of their own three-quarters of a mile away, for thirty-one years. Ten children were born to her; one, an infant son, died in June 1844. In 1841 she and Elias left the Society of Friends — his people’s church, not hers — and joined the United Brethren by letter.

Her own family stayed close by. Her sister Mary Ann, born near Dayton, Ohio, in July 1823, married first William Petty and then Jesse Sykes and lived until 1915; her brother John married Sintha Johnson on 8 October 1842, a wedding written into the marriage page of the Bish family Bible, which a generation later recorded a second Lamb–Bish match, William D. Lamb to Pally A. Bish.

The family moved to Vermillion County in November 1870, and in 1887 into a house in Newport. Four years before that, on the night of 12 October 1883, burglars broke in on them; Elias was badly beaten and a bullet grazed Susannah’s head and split her ear. Their golden wedding, on 23 March 1887, drew fifty-eight relatives and friends, two ministers to perform the ceremony over again, and a table of presents — a tidy and a handkerchief from Mrs. Petty, three towels from Mary J. Sykes. They counted forty-eight descendants, most of them present.

She outlived Elias by eighteen years. In her last years she made her home with her son-in-law Levi S. Clendenning near Sugar Grove, Illinois, and she died there at three o’clock on the morning of Wednesday 8 April 1908, at ninety. Eight of her children survived her. The funeral was held in the church at Sugar Grove the next morning, and she was brought back to Indiana and buried beside Elias in the Thomas burying ground at Newport, under a stone that says only SUSANNAH, wife of E. LAMB.

John Bailey (~1793 Virginia – 1863 Wayne Co.)

Ahnentafel #110 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Bailey was a Virginian, born about 1793. A Wayne County history of 1872 tells his road west: out of Kentucky to Richmond, Indiana, about 1810, then to Perry Township, and in 1859 to Clay Township, where his son Milo was already farming. By 1850 he and his wife Martha were in Green Township with seven children at home, from Harriet, twenty, down to Malissa, seven.

The Baileys farmed among the Lambs. Thomas Lamb lived two doors away, and in a single winter two of the girls of his house married two Lamb brothers: Sarah Ann married Azel E. Lamb in December 1859, and Malissa married Irvin R. Lamb in March 1860, at fifteen. The two families went on doing each other’s business for years; in 1872 Milo Bailey and Irvin’s father stood surety together for Irvin in the county court.

John Bailey died on 5 August 1863, without a will, leaving his widow Martha, twelve grown children and the children of a daughter, Julia McPherson, who had died before him. For months his family simply got on with it: the widow and all the heirs but one, who was “out of the country”, agreed to let two of the sons, John A. and Milo, settle the estate without the court. In February 1864 the two brothers took out letters anyway, on a $22,000 bond with Joseph Lamb as surety, signed by the county clerk Solomon Meredith, lately a general at Gettysburg. The farm and stock came to more than $11,000 in personal property and $5,000 in land; it took five years, four settlements and a lawyer before, in January 1869, the widow received her third and each of the thirteen heirs $582.85 — among them his daughters Sarah A. Lamb and Mallissa Lamb.

Neither of them could write; both signed with a mark, and so did one of the witnesses, Richard Duckworth.

Three children were born to them in England — Mary about 1842, William about 1844, Adam in March 1847 — and the family spoke of themselves ever after as coming from Manchester, nine miles south of Bury and the name a Lancashire family abroad would naturally use. They crossed the Atlantic about 1850 or 1851: Sarah was born in Pennsylvania in 1851, Ralph there about 1856, and by about 1859, when Richard came, they had moved on to Ohio. It is the classic path of an English mining family — the Pennsylvania coalfield first, then the Ohio Valley pits.

At Syracuse, Meigs County, in 1860 he was forty-seven with Ann and six children in the house, the name written down as “Deckworth,” and he was still the head of his own household at fifty-six in 1870. Sarah married Moses Roush that September.

On 24 April 1874 the Meigs County probate court heard a case headed “In the Matter of the Insanity of James Duckworth.” James was present. On the sworn testimony of Wm Duckworth — almost certainly his own son William — and of Henry and August Bartels and David Thomas, the court found him insane, of less than two years’ duration, with a legal settlement in Sutton Township, and entitled to admission to the Lunatic Asylum of the State of Ohio at Athens, which had opened only three months before. Dr. Isaac Irwin was ordered to file his medical certificate; in the meantime the court directed that the patient be held in the county jail “separate and apart from those who are charged with crime.”

He was dead by June 1880, when Ann was a widow. Where he died, and where he lies, nobody knows; a man sent to a state asylum leaves behind him no will and no inventory.

Ann Collins was born in Lancashire about 1818, the daughter of Adam Collins of Freetown, in Bury. She was still under twenty-one, and needed her father’s consent, when she married James Duckworth, a collier of Wood Head Hill, at St Mary’s, Bury, on 21 January 1839. Neither of them could write; both made their mark.

Three children came in England — Mary about 1842, William about 1844 and Adam in March 1847, named for her father. Then the family crossed to America, most likely in 1850 or 1851, and Sarah was born in Pennsylvania, Ralph about 1856, and Richard in Ohio about 1859.

In 1860 she was forty-three and keeping a house of eight at Syracuse, Meigs County, in the Ohio Valley coalfield. Her children married into the English and Cornish mining families around them: Mary to John W. Treadwell, himself a Manchester man; William to Sarah Pascoe, a Cornish name; Sarah to Moses Roush, and Adam eight days after Sarah, in the same township, in September 1870.

In April 1874 her husband was committed to the state asylum at Athens, and by June 1880 he was dead. Ann, sixty-two and widowed, kept her own house in Sutton Township, five households from her daughter Sarah, who had four children under ten. Sarah died the following March, and her mother was right there when it happened. After that nothing more is known of her — when and where she died, and where she is buried, are lost.

George Nease (1819 – 1899 Mason Co. WV)

George Nease was born in Virginia on 10 December 1819 — his own death record says 21 November 1820 — into one of the Nease families of Mason County who had come down from the Shenandoah Valley with the Roushes and settled both banks of the Ohio.

On 12 March 1849 he took out a licence to marry Nancy Roush: she was seventeen, he was twenty-nine. He and Henry Roush signed the bond, and with it the clerk filed a note from an Anthony Roush, made with his mark: “let George Nease have lisons to git maried to nancy Roush.” Their eldest, Susan M., was an infant in the house in 1850, when he was thirty-one and Nancy eighteen, and a six-year-old Lewis J. was there in 1860, in a household the enumerator spelled “Neoce.” He farmed in Graham District, and he and Nancy raised their family there; their son Lewis married Martha Love in 1877 and twenty years later gave his own daughter in marriage to a Roush.

George Nease died on 21 November 1899 in Mason County, and Nancy gave the county the particulars — she is entered as “Nancie Nease, wife.” It was one of the few years the register asked for the dead man’s parents, and the clerk wrote “Samuel and Catherine Nease,” a first name that does not match the Emanuel and Catharine Nease who were the only Nease couple of that generation in the county. A deed he signed himself in 1865 answers it. With his brothers and sisters he sold his share of the home farm as one of the “Heirs at law of Emanuel Nease deceased”.

He lies at Broad Run. His marker is a small modern block cut GEORGE NEASE 1818 - 1898 — a year adrift at each end from everything else that records him.
