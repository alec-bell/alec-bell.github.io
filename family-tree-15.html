Sarah Pigford (fl. 1801 New Hanover Co. NC)

Ahnentafel #291 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Sarah was the wife of William “Lost Billie” Pigford of Sill's Creek, in the Penderlea country of New Hanover County, North Carolina. Family tradition, set down about 1896, says only that Lost Billie “married a woman of Dutch descent.” Her own surname was never written down anywhere that survives — the family's historian hunted through the marriage records of both New Hanover and Duplin counties for it and wrote in 1967 that “her family name continues to be beyond the reach of this recorder.”

They had a large family: William, Jacob, Timothy, Solomon, Edward, Martha, Margaret, and a daughter Elizabeth, who married an Eakins.

Her husband's will of 27 March 1801 begins with her, and it trusts her with everything: “First; I give and bequeath to Sarah, my dearly beloved wife, all my household furniture, moveable effects and all other of my estate… to be by her disposed of amongst her children as she may think proper; and also the plantation whereon I now live, with all the improvements thereon; and all the land I possess adjoining the east side of Sill's Creek, for the term of her life.” She was named executrix. Her son Solomon was to have the home place only after her death, and until then was under orders to help his brother Timothy put up a house. She is believed to lie in an unmarked grave in the Penderlea area, near the old Pigford burying ground.

Mary Thorn / Thorne (d. 1844 Greene Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #321 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Mary Thorn died in Greene County, Alabama, shortly before 23 January 1844. She was the widow of James Thorn, who had made his will on 12 June 1837 and whose estate was proved there in October 1839. His will names his own children but no wife at all, so she was probably his second wife and not the mother of his family; when his estate was finally settled on 14 October 1844 his sons Jesse W. and William H. Thorn released every claim against hers.

Her own next of kin were Stephenses. The court that opened her estate on 23 January 1844 cited Moore, James, Lewis and William Stephens, Esther Hitt, Lucinda Ball and Elizabeth Bevers as her nearest relations; James Stephens was appointed administrator; and when the estate was divided on 12 October 1846 it went in ten equal shares to that same set of brothers and married sisters, with the share of Esther Hitt — by then dead — going to her “Representatives,” the way a court treats a predeceased child. In all likelihood Stephens was her first married name and Thorn her second, which would make those ten her children; nothing says so outright, and she may equally have been their sister or their aunt.

Her inventory, taken on 27 April 1844, catalogues her entire world down to an eighteen-cent umbrella and a woman’s saddle — and then one line worth more than all the rest together: “1 Negro woman Jane — 475.00.” Jane had very likely come to her as part of her widow’s share of James Thorn’s estate. She had been hired out for seventeen months, and she was sold at the estate sale on 29 April 1844 to the administrator, James Stephens, for $700. The whole sale raised $1,075.86, so Jane was sixty-five per cent of everything Mary Thorn owned, and roughly $70 of Moore Stephens’s $107 share was her price.

Mary had also been lending money to the men around her: her papers include promissory notes on Charles Stephens for $15.13 and $32.40 and on Lewis Stephens for $100 — she was banking her sons. Her land was sold at the ordinary sale to Andrew Norwood for $127.09.

Gershom Tuttle II (1738 Farmington Conn. – 1818)

Ahnentafel #384 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Revolutionary patriot

Gershom Tuttle was born on 22 August 1738 at Farmington, Connecticut — in the parish of New Cambridge, which later became the town of Bristol. His father was Ensign Gershom Tuttle and his mother was Lois Allis.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield in Massachusetts, he married Tabitha Mitchell. The Deerfield clerk styled him “of New Cambridge” and gave the bride no parish, which is what a clerk does for a local bride — and she was local in more than one sense: her mother Mary Allis and his mother Lois Allis were sisters. He married his own first cousin.

His father died in 1777, leaving a will made that May which gave the widow Lois a third for life and divided the rest among “my Three Sons Gershom, Oliver and Samuel Tuttle,” and appointed “my Son Gershom Tuttle whole and sole Executor.” The executor's account of “Gershom Tuttel Executor” was allowed in January 1779.

He is a patriot of the Revolution, and the service is logistics rather than battle — which in Connecticut in 1780 mattered as much. He was a conductor of teams, which meant he ran wagon trains of provisions out of Connecticut into New York State: some three thousand barrels, about five hundred tons, averaging a hundred and fifteen miles a haul. On 23 May 1780 the Connecticut General Assembly paid him £285.13.8 in wages and £60.13.11 for forage in advance, and still owed him £200.2.8. He is listed first of the nine conductors. The Daughters of the American Revolution recognise him as ancestor A131791, rank teamster, “Conductor of teams transporting supplies for the army.”

After the war he moved north. At Claremont, New Hampshire — where his father had died — he signed a town recommendation of Ambrose Cossitt as justice of the peace on 17 December 1789, with his brother Oliver and his son Solomon, and in 1790 he headed the only Gershom Tuttle household in the census of the whole country. About 1790 he went on to Clinton in Oneida County, New York, and afterwards joined the settlement at Watertown in Jefferson County. He was alive to see his son become a colonel of militia there.

He died on 5 January 1818, in his eightieth year. Where, and where he lies, is not documented by any record; the family genealogy gives no place and the DAR's “Watertown” carries no source. No Gershom Tuttle appears in Jefferson County in the 1820 census, four county histories record no death, and neither Rutland's nor Watertown's cemetery transcriptions hold him.

The Tuttle family genealogy of 1883 makes him the first deacon of Watertown’s first church, organised in 1803. The church’s own historian, who read its minutes, dates the Tuttle deaconship to 1808 and gives it to his son the colonel, so the honour may belong to the son.

Tabitha Mitchell (b. 1736/7 – m. 1757 Deerfield)

Ahnentafel #385 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Tabitha Mitchell was born on 9 February 1736/7, the daughter of Joseph Mitchell and Mary Allis. The family was at Sunderland, in the Connecticut valley of Massachusetts, where her brother Joseph had been born in 1726/7, and afterwards at Deerfield.

On 22 December 1757, at Deerfield, she married Gershom Tuttle of New Cambridge in Connecticut — and the match was not the mystery it looked like for years. Her mother Mary Allis and the groom's mother Lois Allis were sisters: Lois had married the elder Gershom Tuttle in 1737, and twenty years later Lois's son married Mary's daughter. Tabitha and her husband were first cousins, and the Deerfield venue needs no explaining — the bride was at home.

She went with him through Connecticut, the Revolution, New Hampshire and on into New York. Her father's will, made on 14 April 1792 and proved in November 1794, gives “to my Daughter Tabitha Wife of Gersham Tuttle Twelve Pounds,” beside her sisters Mary Belding and Hannah Bigelow and the children of “my late Son Joseph.” That one clause does three things: it documents her parentage from a record, it documents her marriage, and it shows her alive in the spring of 1792 — which refutes the unsourced “1737–1782” that followed her around.

On the Daughters of the American Revolution's own file for her husband she is recorded as “X Mitchel” — surname and nothing else. This family knows her first name, her birthday, her parents and her grandfather, all from records, and the national society that honours her husband does not.

George Roush (1761 Frederick VA – 1845 Meigs OH)

Ahnentafel #480 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict · Revolutionary patriot

George Roush was born in July 1761 in Frederick County, Virginia — the part of it that became Shenandoah County eleven years later — one of the younger sons of the German immigrant John Adam Roush and his wife Susannah. He grew up on Mill Creek in a house where the Bible was printed in German, and he was eighteen when the Revolution reached him.

He enlisted in the autumn of 1779 and served two months under his own brother, Captain John Roush, “both of Shenandoah County Virginia.” He was drafted again in the spring of 1780 under Captain Joseph Pugh, and a third time in 1781 for three months — that tour, his brother Jonas later swore, as a substitute for another brother, Jacob. The family always told that he was at Yorktown and saw Cornwallis hand over his sword; his brother Jonas certainly was. He applied for a pension from Ohio in 1832 and it was allowed as claim S18579, and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as Patriot Ancestor A098975.

About 1781, in Shenandoah County, he married Catharine Zerkel, the eldest daughter of Michael and Catharine Zerckel of Forestville. Their children, born over more than twenty years, included Michael, Magdalena, John — who in 1808 married his cousin Regina, Jonas Roush’s daughter — Daniel, Lewis, Catherine, Samuel, Susannah, Jacob, Mary and a second Jonas.

He went down the Ohio ahead of his family about 1798, taking up land in Mason County with his brother Captain John, in whose name the purchase was made; his son Jacob was still born and baptised back in Shenandoah County in 1799. In 1807 he crossed to Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, bought a level tract from Edward W. Tupper west of the hamlet of Dorcas, near Racine, and lived on it for the rest of his life. He was a mechanic by trade — “a very good one, we are told,” the family said — and a farmer, and when Meigs County organised itself in 1819 he sat among its first jurors. He had been a citizen of three counties at the moment each was created.

Catharine died in Virginia, after a journey home she had pined for; her burial place was never written down. On 13 August 1815, at Gallipolis, George married a widow, Catherine “Kitty” Wolfe, and they had one daughter, Hannah.

One Sunday morning a schoolmaster boarding in the Roush house — one of the Henkels, the Lutheran printing family of New Market, the family believed — asked to sketch him. After “considerable reluctance” George gave in, put on the coat of his uniform, and sat. The small sketch stayed in the family at Racine for a century, kept by Elizabeth Wilcoxin and then by James L. Wilcoxin, and was carried out to Meigs County pioneer meetings to be looked at. Of the nine Roush brothers who went to that war, he is the only one whose face is known.

In his last illness he talked about charging the British, and told his comrades to get ready, they were coming over the hill. He died on 31 May 1845 and lies in Weldon Cemetery, on the third terrace above the Ohio at Racine, Meigs County. His small marble stone is still legible: “GEORGE ROUSH … DIED May 31, 1845 AGED 84 years.” On 19 August 1933, before about a thousand descendants, the Sons of the American Revolution dedicated a grave marker there; the flat marker lying at the foot of his headstone today reads “GEORGE ROUSH / CONTINENTAL LINE / REVOLUTIONARY WAR / JUL 1761 – MAY 1845,” and it gets his war wrong — all three of his terms were Virginia militia, not the Continental Line. His German Bible, said to have come from his father out of Germany, was still in the family in 1928, in the hands of a great-granddaughter at Racine.

⭐ 27 Oct 2026 — content round 2 (R2_likenesses). The likeness of George Roush printed at p.418 of the 1928 Roush history is a 1920s REDRAWING, not a reproduction of the original. The original was a small sketch taken from life, with the sitter wearing his uniform coat, by an unnamed schoolteacher boarding in the Roush home whom the compiler believed to be a Henkel. [ROUSH-130] As of 1928 the ORIGINAL small sketch of George Roush survived in private hands at Racine, Meigs County, Ohio, in the Lafayette Wilcoxin house, and had repeatedly been exhibited at Meigs County pioneer meetings. A recovery path for the original therefore exists. [ROUSH-131] The artist's monogram at the lower right of the 1928 plate was read at full resolution for the first time: a light cursive cipher, a rounded C-or-G conjoined with a descending stroke crossed by a short bar, with a full stop after it. It is painted light-on-dark INSIDE the picture area, i.e. signed on the artwork. Given the text at p.420 this is most likely the 1920s copyist's mark rather than the original sketcher's. [ROUSH-132] The Roush Family Association had its own portrait copyist. Its 1937 booklet directs members with old pictures they want 'redone' to John Rausch, Portrait Studies, 2515 Maplewood Drive, Minerva Park, Columbus, Ohio. He is a candidate — a LEAD, nothing more — for the hand that redrew the George Roush sketch for the 1928 history and left the monogram on it. [ROUSH-133] ⚠ Whether the 1928 plate reproduces the original likeness or is a later copy drawing — History of the Roush Family in America vol. 1 (1928), printed p.420: "From it we were able to sketch again with great difficulty the likeness of this man"
