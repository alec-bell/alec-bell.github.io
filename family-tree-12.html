William H. Ward (~1806 SC – 1869 Henry Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #178 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

William H. Ward was born about 1806 in South Carolina and came west to the south-eastern corner of Alabama. The 1850 census of Henry County finds him at family 646 — “W. H. WARD, 44, m, Farmer, $400, S.C.” — with his wife Syntha, thirty-five and also South Carolina-born, and eight children, all of them born in Alabama. He was invisible to the indexers for a century and a half because the enumerator wrote the dwelling and family numbers one line low, onto his wife's line, so the machines made Syntha the head of the house and pushed him up into somebody else's household.

On 28 October 1854 he put his name to one of the better documents in this family's record. His daughter Emily Caroline was seventeen and wanted to marry John Truett, and Alabama required a parent's consent for a bride under twenty-one. The clerk copied his words straight into the marriage book: “I give my free and unqualified consent that license should issue for the marriage of John Truett with my daughter Emily C.” It is signed Wm. H. Ward in a firm, practised, unmistakably literate hand — which is worth noticing, because the bridegroom and the bride both signed their own papers with an X for the rest of their lives.

By 1860 he was at Rowville, Beat 5, aged fifty-three, with Cyntha and the younger children. Then he died, intestate, somewhere between 1860 and September 1869 — and probably in 1868 or 1869, because by 18 September 1869 his administrators, his son-in-law C. J. Hutto and his eldest son John E. Ward, were already in the Probate Court of Henry County applying for the widow's dower, with notice ordered to be published for thirty days in the Henry County Register.

He died on 6 February 1869 -- his son and his son-in-law swore to the date six weeks later when they asked the Probate Court for letters. He left about a thousand dollars of land and twenty-five hundred dollars of personal estate, most of it other men's promises: eleven notes, from twenty-four dollars to three hundred and thirty, several of them marked 'doubtfull'. The family's lawyers were Oates & Cowan of Abbeville, and the receipt for their hundred and five dollars is signed by W. C. Oates himself.

His land could not be divided, so it was sold. The petition of 28 September 1870 names every heir: the widow Cynthia; Ann Elizabeth Hutto; John E. Ward; Mary Nichols; Martha Driggers; William H. Ward, named for his father; and the minors George R., Eliza and Isaiah C. Ward, each “over fourteen and under twenty-one.” One name was left off — the daughter who had gone away west — and six weeks later a neighbour, Abijah Wiggins, put her back in under oath: among the heirs of full age, he swore, was “the wife of JOHN TRUETT.” His daughter said in 1920 that he had died in Clarke County; his widow was still at Rowville in 1880. Both are recorded, neither is proved.

Syntha / Cyntha Ward (~1813 SC – 1880/1900)

Ahnentafel #179 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Syntha Ward — the clerks also wrote Cyntha, Cynthia and Cintha — was born in South Carolina about 1813, and both of her own parents were born in North Carolina. She married William H. Ward about 1833 or 1834, probably in South Carolina, since no Alabama marriage of a Ward to a Cynthia is indexed in those years. They had at least ten children.

She is on the 1850 schedule of Henry County, Alabama as “Syntha, 35”, next to her husband and eight children, and on the 1860 schedule at Rowville Beat 5 as “Cyntha, 47”. Her youngest son was given the unusual name Caraway — or Conway; the records read it both ways — which is exactly the way a mother's maiden surname usually travels, and it is the best clue anyone has to hers.

Her husband died intestate before September 1869, and on the 18th of that month the court appointed a day to hear the application for her dower, with notice to be published in the Henry County Register and J. H. Simonton named guardian ad litem for the minor heirs. When the estate's land was sold the following year she is “Cynthia Ward, widow of decedent”; when the neighbour Abijah Wiggins swore his deposition at Abbeville in November 1870 she is “Mrs Cyntha Ward the widow of deceased.” In 1880 she was still at Rowville, sixty-six, widowed, keeping house, entered as MOTHER in the household of her youngest son Isaiah. She does not appear in the 1900 census of Alabama, so she died in those twenty years — before the state began registering deaths, which is why there is no certificate for her.

Her dower was eighty acres, measured off by five freeholders on 23 November 1869 and chosen, the writ said, so that none of them was connected to any of the parties by blood or by marriage. She signed everything with a mark, and the clerks spelled her Cynthia, Cyntha and Syntha on the same file.

Four documents in her husband's estate name her, and every one of them calls her only Mrs Cynthia Ward, widow of the deceased. Her maiden name has not survived in anything this project has read.

Colonel Gershom Tuttle III (1769 – 1823)

Ahnentafel #192 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Gershom Tuttle was born on 11 June 1769 at Bristol, Connecticut. He is the third Gershom Tuttle in a row in this family, and he is almost certainly the son of the second — the Revolutionary teamster — though that particular link is stated by the family genealogy of 1883 and by the DAR rather than by any record that says so in words, and this project has not been able to close it.

He married Pamelia Strong Clark at Farmington on 8 July 1788, when he was nineteen. In 1791 he and Joseph Tuttle, of Wardsborough, Vermont, bought a fifty-acre lot at Wilmington in that state; by the mid-nineties they had moved west to Paris, in Oneida County, New York, where the 1800 enumeration carries “Gershom Tuttle” and “Gershom Tuttle jun” on one sheet. About 1800 he went north again to the new Black River country and opened a farm of 275 acres some five miles from the site of Watertown, in the town of Rutland.

He was there from the beginning. When Rutland held its first town meeting in March 1803, in David Coffeen's house, he was one of the three men chosen highway commissioner — the same meeting that set a ten-dollar bounty on wolves. In 1806, with Seth Bailey, he built a grist mill on the Black River, the first of its kind there. He was a charter member of the Masonic lodge warranted at Watertown on 17 June 1806, where the roll enters him as “Gershom Tuttle, Jr.” — which is why, seventeen years later and eight hundred miles away, a lodge in Indiana buried him. From 1808 he was one of the county's commissioners for loaning the moneys of the state. In 1810 he took five shares at ten dollars in the Black River Navigation Company. He traded, made potash, and kept a tavern on the great highway to the lake country.

The tavern is what his neighbours remembered. About 1803 a hundred men of two townships assembled there, by appointment, to settle a bet about which town could out-wrestle the other, the winners promised all they could eat and drink. The first Congregational church of Watertown, organised in 1803, met in his ball-room when it had nowhere else, and he was an original member. He was fined eight dollars in 1809 for not turning up as a grand juror. The family book calls him “a man of excellent character, genial temper, very fond of a joke, a capital story teller,” and that is the man the records show.

He was commissioned second major of the Jefferson County militia on 5 April 1805, and on 4 April 1808 lieutenant-colonel of the 76th Regiment — the regiment the county histories simply call Tuttle's. In the War of 1812 the Watertown militia belonged to it and were turned out en masse whenever invasion threatened; at the Battle of Sackets Harbor on 29 May 1813 the county history posts the militia “in the edge of the woods, back of the clearing,” under Colonel Gershom Tuttle. (A second and unrelated Colonel Gershom Tuttle, of Massachusetts and of the regular army, died at that same post during the war and lies under the monument there; the militia command is ours.) He resigned in 1814.

In 1816 he took the family to Vigo County, Indiana. A federal land clerk entered him on 22 November 1817 as “Gershom Tuttle, Jeff'n Co N.Y.” and, three weeks later, as “Gershom Tuttle, Fort Harrison.” He became a county commissioner in 1820. The last summer of his life a sheriff was advertising a half-quarter section “the property of Gersham and Chester Tuttle” for sale on execution at the suit of John F. King, and his patent for eighty acres came through under James Monroe's hand on 15 July 1823, seven weeks before he died. On 29 August he made a will leaving everything to Pamelia for life and naming no child at all.

He died on the Friday night of 5 September 1823 in Otter Creek Township, in the fifty-fourth year of his age. Terre Haute's first newspaper reported that “on Sunday last his remains were consigned to the earth, accompanied with Masonic honors by the Terre Haute Lodge of which he was a member.” The estate turned out to be insolvent and was still in court nine years later. His grave has never been located.

Pamelia Strong Clark (1770 Farmington – 1851)

Ahnentafel #193 · his mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Pamelia Strong Clark was born on 4 November 1770 at Farmington, Connecticut. Clark was her surname; Strong was a middle name, and it descended four more generations — Clark Strong Tuttle, Dennis Clark, Richard Clark — long after anyone remembered where it came from. On 8 July 1788, aged seventeen, she married Gershom Tuttle of Bristol.

She spent the next twenty-eight years moving west with him: Wilmington in Vermont, Paris in Oneida County, and about 1800 the new Black River country of Jefferson County, New York, where her husband farmed 275 acres, built a mill and kept the tavern that gave the neighbourhood its ball-room. Her son Chester was born at Sackets Harbor in 1796; Cynthia at Watertown in 1803; Amanda in 1810; Clark Strong in April 1811; Asa in 1813. In 1816 the family went on to Vigo County, Indiana.

Her husband died on 5 September 1823, leaving a will that gave her everything for life, with power to provide “for any of our children for whom she may think necessary to provide.” Three weeks later she gave bond as executrix, with Abraham and Henry Markle as her securities, and the inventory of the whole estate came to a little over six hundred dollars. It was insolvent. Her co-executor Nathaniel Huntington was out of the sheriff's reach by 1829 and dead by 1831.

What followed is the hardest stretch of any life on this chart. The probate court cited her in January 1829, again in November 1829, and summoned her again on 2 November 1830 to show cause “why she has not made a final settlement of the estate of Gursham Tuttle deceased, of which she is the executrix.” In March 1831 she petitioned to be let out of it, through a solicitor, in words that are still painful to read: the estate's business was “long since closed,” but “the papers of said Estate were left in the possession of Nathaniel Huntington Esq. who is dead, and they are now in so confused and mutilated a situation that no accurate statement of the condition of the Estate could be made from them.” On 29 December 1831 the court issued a writ of attachment for her body for contempt. The sheriff executed it on 2 February 1832 and charged eighty-three and a half cents in fees. Bail was set at ten dollars.

She outlived her husband by twenty-eight years and followed one of her children ninety miles south into the hills of Brown County. She died on 22 April 1851, and her stone at Southview Cemetery, Nashville, reads: In Memory of PAMELIA TUTTLE DIED April 22 1851 Aged 80 Y's, 5 M's, 18 D's. Count the age back and it lands, to the day, on 4 November 1770 — her own stone confirming the birthday the family book recorded.

According to the Tuttle family genealogy of 1883, her brother was an officer of the United States Army who died while in charge of the federal arsenal at Troy, New York.

Joseph Lamb (~1782 NC – 1855 Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #216 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Lamb was born in North Carolina about 1782 — the county historian says he died in 1855 “at the age of 73.” The family was living in Randolph County, about sixteen miles north of Asheboro, in 1814, when his son Elias was born there. He married Lydia Adamson — the 1806 bond is made out for “Lidya Addison,” with her brother Samuel Adamson standing bondsman — and they were members of the Society of Friends. Lydia died first — in 1844 by his son Elias’s account, though other records say 1837. Joseph died on 29 July 1855, aged seventy-three, and lies in Sugar Grove Cemetery in Wayne County under a small marble stone, weathered but still readable: JOSEPH LAMB DIED 29th Day of the July of year 1855.

John Roush (1785 – 1861 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #240 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

John Roush was born on 22 November 1785 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, a son of George Roush and Catharine Zerkel, and was baptised into the Lutheran faith on 5 January 1786, with another John Roush and his wife Susannah standing as witnesses.

On 18 May 1808 he married his own cousin, Regina Roush, the daughter of Jonas Roush. The county's oldest marriage book records it on its first page, in the family's older spelling: “John Rausch to Regenna Rausch, May 18, 1808,” by Francis Watkins, Justice of the Peace. Eleven children followed, and the New Haven Lutheran church register is said to have carried the birth and baptism of every one of them.

Benjamin, the fifth, was born at Graham Station on the Virginia side in August 1816; Hannah was born in the Nease Settlement, in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio, in May 1820. Between those two births John and Regina crossed the river for good, into the few square miles where the Roush and Nease families who later intermarried were founding neighbours - the same ground where their great-great-grandson John G. Roush would be born more than fifty years on. He was known in the family as “Blackbird John.”

He could not write. Every deed he made he signed “John his + mark Roush.” In the twenty months between July 1848 and March 1850 he settled land on four Roush men - Abel, James, John Junior and Benjamin. On 9 August 1849 he cut the east strip of Section 25, Town 3, Range 12 in two on a single day before a single justice and two witnesses: seventy acres running north from the south-east corner to John Roush Junior, and sixty-nine acres and twenty poles running south from the north-east corner to Benjamin, each for five hundred dollars. Not one of those deeds says father, or son, or heir. No wife joined in any of them to release her dower, and there was no Regina in his house when the census taker came in 1850.

He was sixty-six then, living four doors from Benjamin in Sutton Township. He died on 21 January 1861. Regina's father Jonas had spent his last years in her home and lies in the same ground. For most of a century John and Regina's graves were unmarked - the 1928 family history says the two graves just north of Jonas's were pointed out to the compilers by people who knew, “altho we have had them definitely pointed out to us.” Today a granite stone at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville carries the name ROUSH, and underneath, JOHN 1785 - 1861 and REGINA 1788 - 18. Whoever ordered it still could not supply her death year, and the cutter left the last two figures off.
