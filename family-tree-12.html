J. T. Swain (1838 Wilcox AL – 1904 Sumter AL)

Ahnentafel #76 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Tolbert Swain was born on 24 May 1838 in Wilcox County, Alabama. By his early twenties he had moved west into Lauderdale County, Mississippi, where on 6 February 1860 he married Nancy E. Jane Phillips; their first child, Joseph Lee, was born there that December.

He enlisted on 8 March 1862 at Lauderdale Station, a private in Company C of the regiment that ended the war as the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry, bringing his own horse. The army described him then: twenty-four, five feet eight, dark complexion, black eyes, black hair, a farmer born in Wilcox County, Alabama. Within five months he was discharged with consumption — “phthisis pulmonalis” — and paid off at Columbus in August 1862, having already signed his claim to the back pay over to another man. In September 1863 he enlisted again, at Enterprise; he was sick in the recruiting camp in the summer of 1864, and in October 1864 he was discharged by order of General Hood. A portrait of him survives — a man in a dark coat and a hard round hat, with a heavy beard down his chest and a direct, unsmiling stare.

When the war was over he did not wait. His two eldest children were born in Mississippi, in 1861 and 1864; every child from 1866 onward was born in Alabama. He moved his wife and two small children across the state line into Sumter County in the first months after the surrender, when he was about twenty-seven, and settled at Cuba. By 1880 the house there held him and Nancy, both forty-two, with seven children at home, from Joseph L. at nineteen down to John M. at six.

On 3 July 1875 he walked into the registrar's office at Cuba, in Precinct No. 18 of Sumter County, and took the registration oath ahead of that August's referendum on a new state constitution. The printed oath he swore made him promise that he would “never countenance or aid in the secession of this State from the United States” and that he “accept[ed] the civil and political equality of all men” — ten years after he had ridden with the 2nd Mississippi Cavalry. The registrar W. H. Lewis wrote his name for him and he made his mark: he could not write. Against his name the registrar put down his living in a word: farmer, near Cuba. Two lines below him on the same page, registering the same day, was B. A. Simmons, the miller and farmer whose daughters his son Joseph Lee would marry nine and ten years later.

Two more babies, Jesse and Hassiel, were born and died in 1876–79. Nine children in all are known, and four of them were still living in 1940: Joseph Lee, John M. Swain of York, Mattie Shamburger and Laura McDonald. Joseph Tolbert Swain died on 14 January 1904, aged sixty-five, in Sumter County; his son William T. followed him four months later. In November 1905 his eldest son, acting “as the administrator of the estate of J. T. Swain, deceased,” cried a hundred and sixty acres of his land for sale at the post-office door at Cuba, to be divided among the heirs. He lies in Clay Memorial Cemetery at Cuba under a marble marker reading JOSEPH TOLBERT SWAIN, PVT CO C 2 MISS CAV, CONFEDERATE STATES ARMY. Someone left a line over him in 2013: “Here rests one who so bravely fought for the land he could not save.”

She died at Cuba on Monday 1 June 1908, about seventy years old, and was buried the next day beside her husband in Clay Memorial Cemetery at Cuba. Her stone does not give her own name. Ben Simmons (1818 GA – 1894 Cuba, Sumter AL)

Ahnentafel #78 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Benjamin Asbury Simmons — B. A. Simmons on his gravestone and Ben on most people's tongues — was born on 26 October 1818 in Georgia, in Troup County by his family's reckoning, and came as a young man with his people into Sumter County, Alabama. His father was very probably Robert Simmons of that county — who was married by 1845 to a wife named Ann S., though whether she was Ben’s mother no record says — who in the fifth item of a will written on 6 March 1845 set down: “I hereby appoint my Son Benjamin Asbury Simmons my executor to this my last will and testament.” The same will shows how that household looked after its women: the daughters' share was granted “to them and the heirs of their bodies; and not to be subject to the debts of their husbands.” The will was proved on 15 November 1855. Ben's brothers and sisters were Mariah Jane, who married a Bustin, William Miles, John Christopher, Robert H., an E. W. and Susan H., who married a Boyd and died young in 1852.

He married Adeline on 11 May 1847, when he was twenty-eight and she was seventeen. Their children's birthplaces trace a family moving back and forth along the state line: Alabama in the late 1840s, Mississippi in the early 1850s, Alabama again by the end of that decade. By 1870 he was at Cuba with Adeline and six children in the house, and he was still there in 1880, with a sixteen-year-old daughter among them whose name the clerk wrote down as “Dellon” — Rebecca Anna Dillon Simmons. Two of his daughters married the same Swain man, twenty-two months apart. Eugenia married J. L. Swain first and died young, leaving a little girl named Lela. Dillon married him next. When the probate court divided Ben's land in 1895 it listed all seven of his heirs by name, and among them was “Dillon Swain, adult wife of J. L. Swain.” Lela, still a minor, took her dead mother's share.

He was a miller as well as a farmer. When he registered to vote at Cuba on 3 July 1875, entry 64 of the Sumter County book, he wrote his own name in a strong, practised hand, where most of the page made marks, and the registrar gave his living in three words: “Mill & Farm.” His was a slaveholding family before the war — in 1855 his brother Lewis was taxed for twenty-nine enslaved people, Robert H. for fifteen and William M. for two.

He ended his life on his own place near Cuba Station, Sumter County, Alabama, with a mill’s tools and an iron safe among his goods, and died on 6 April 1894, aged seventy-five. Adaline took out letters on his estate that August and sold his horse, cows, buggy and shop tools on the premises the Friday before Christmas. He was buried just across the state line at Kewanee, in Lauderdale County, Mississippi. His marble headstone in the churchyard at Salem Baptist, carved with two clasped hands, reads B. A. SIMMONS, HUSBAND OF ADALINE SIMMONS, BORN OCT. 26, 1818, DIED APRIL 6, 1894.

Henry H. Stephens (1822 NC – 1879 Sumter Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #80 · his father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Henry H. Stephens was born on 8 August 1822 in North Carolina, in Orange County by the family’s account, the son of Moore Stephens and Rebecca Moize. He was a small child when the family left for Alabama — they were in Greene County by 1824 — and he grew up on the Alabama side of the Tombigbee country, moving on with his father into Sumter County.

On 4 June 1846, at twenty-three, he married Sarah Ann Jones, born in North Carolina about 1833, whose family lived a few doors from his own. In the summer of 1850 the whole arrangement sat on one page of the census: in the “Intercourse” division of Sumter County, enumerated on 7 June, Henry was twenty-seven and farming at dwelling 1184 with Sarah, seventeen, and a son Calvin, aged one, and a boarder in the house, Redden Marshall, twenty-three and North Carolina-born. Two dwellings along lived Daniel Jones, Sarah’s father; six houses away, at dwelling 1190, lived Henry’s own father Moore. A son, Caswell Massey Stephens, was born on 8 January 1852.

Sarah died young, and nothing records her death. On 21 October 1854 Henry married again, to Elizabeth Jones — almost certainly his first wife’s younger sister — before Jesse B. Thorne, Justice of the Peace. He was a Minister of the Gospel as well as a farmer, and the Sumter County marriage registers have him performing weddings through the 1860s and seventies, his own son Caswell’s among them in October 1871.

By 1860 the family had followed Moore Stephens west into Newton County, Mississippi, where Henry was listed with Elizabeth, twenty-one, and the children Caroline, Caswell, James and William. He was forty when the war reached him, with a second wife and small children at home and no exemption — he was liable to Confederate conscription from September 1862. He served, and the company he most likely marched in was the Newton County company of the 5th Regiment, Mississippi Infantry (State Troops) — the Minute Men — where an H. H. Stephens appears as a private in 1863, with a W. Stephens and a Z. Stephens beside him on the roll and a cluster of Joneses: Daniel, Samuel, William, Riley, H. D., A. and W., the surname he had married into twice. The 5th mustered from 31 July to 1 October 1862 and again from 17 July to 21 September 1863, and it was at Vicksburg: the regiment’s papers afterwards count the men paroled there on 5 August 1863, with the killed, the dead of wounds and the dead of disease through the siege beside them. A service file for an H. H. Stephens of that regiment, very probably his, survives: a private of Company A who enlisted at Decatur, the Newton County seat, on 5 February 1863, joined in May, was taken when Vicksburg surrendered on 4 July 1863, and signed his parole there on 10 July, swearing not to take up arms again until exchanged. His widow, swearing to his service forty years later, remembered it differently — 2nd Lieutenant in Company I of the 4th Mississippi Infantry Volunteers — but that company was the Benela Sharpshooters of Calhoun County, two hundred miles from anywhere this family lived, and no Stephens of his initials is on its roll.

He came home. By 1870 the family was back at Cuba, in Township 17 of Sumter County, Alabama — the third leg of one continuous migration. On 31 December 1869 he stood in the Probate Court at a special term before Judge J. A. Abrahams and was appointed guardian of three of his own sons, Caswell M., Calvin D. and John M., each over fourteen and each described as “a minor heir of Daniel Jones deceased,” posting bonds of $800, $800 and $1,000; the property was real enough that he was filing annual settlements two years later. On 22 June 1875 he registered to vote at Thornville, Precinct No. 3 of Sumter County, entry 25, writing “H. H. Stephens,” farmer, in his own hand. Two lines down the same page a D. C. Stephens registered the same day, and immediately above him A. J. Shelton, “Farmer & Blk Smith.”

He died on 20 October 1879 at Gaston, in Sumter County. His gravestone reckons his life to the day — born 8 August 1822, died 20 October 1879, aged 57 years, 2 months and 12 days — and carries a Masonic square and compasses inside a carved wreath. The carver spelled the family name STEAVENS.

What the war and his death left behind shows in one document, sworn a quarter of a century later. On 4 July 1904, in Sumter County, Elizabeth Stephens went before the county pension examiner R. H. Hale and swore that she was “the widow of H. H. Stevens, who served as a 2[nd] Lieutenant in company I of the 4th Regiment Miss Infantry Volunteers,” that her husband “died on the 29 day of Oct, 1879,” and “that he did not desert the service of the State of Alabama, nor of the Confederate States.” Two men of the neighbourhood swore the same day that they had known him personally and that “the services set forth in the application were rendered by said H. H. Stephens” — W. H. Stephens, very probably one of his own family, and T. J. Tucker. The Sumter County board passed it, and on 17 August 1904 the State Board of Examiners at Montgomery placed her in class 4 under the pension act of 10 February 1899. With it she filed a Schedule of Property, a printed form with a line for every kind of thing a Black Belt household could own — acres of land, town property, horses and mules, cattle, hogs, sheep and goats, pistols and guns, watches and clocks and jewelry, household and kitchen furniture, bicycles and vehicles, goods and merchandise, mechanical and farming tools. She wrote a nought on every line, and a nought at the total, and the application says she had “no children upon whom she can depend for support.”
