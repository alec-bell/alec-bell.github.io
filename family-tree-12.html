Regina Roush (b. 1788)

Ahnentafel #241 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Regina Roush was born on 30 November 1788, the second child of Jonas Roush - a private of the Virginia line who was at the siege of Yorktown and saw Cornwallis surrender - and his wife Barbara Fox.

On 18 May 1808, at nineteen, she married her cousin John Roush before Francis Watkins, a justice of the peace, who entered the couple on the first page of the county's oldest marriage book as “John Rausch to Regenna Rausch.” A Roush had married a Roush: both of them grandchildren of the same Shenandoah Valley family. Eleven children were born to them. Benjamin, the fifth, arrived at Graham Station in August 1816; Hannah, in May 1820, was born after the move across the Ohio into the Nease Settlement.

Her father came to live with her. The 1928 family history says Jonas “spent his last years in her home in the Nease Settlement near where he lies buried,” and his own Revolutionary pension file, written down from his sworn statement and quite independent of the book, records that he removed to Meigs County, Ohio in June 1837 to live with a daughter. He died there on 14 December 1850. The Daughters of the American Revolution have twice approved lineages that run through “child REGINA - spouse JOHN ROUSH.”

When she died is not known and was not known in 1928, when the compilers of the family book wrote plainly that it “has not been ascertained.” There are indications. She was not in her husband's household in 1850, and in none of the five deeds he made in 1848-50 did a wife join to release her dower, which Ohio law of the day would normally have required of a married man - so she had very probably died before the summer of 1848.

She lies at Gilmore Cemetery in Minersville, beside her husband and near her father. The granite over the two of them reads JOHN 1785 - 1861 and REGINA 1788 - 18, and there the inscription stops.

David Altman was born about 1788 in Pennsylvania and farmed in Sutton Township, Meigs County, Ohio - the Roushes' own township, and the Duckworths' county. He was already there in 1840, when the census taker wrote the name down as “Attman.”

In 1850 he was sixty-two, with a thousand dollars of land, keeping house with Margaret, fifty-six, also Pennsylvania-born, and four Ohio-born children: Jacob, twenty-three, and Solomon, nineteen, both farming with him, Mary, fourteen, and Samuel, twelve. A John Altman, about thirty-one and born in Ohio, headed a second Altman household in the same township with a wife and four sons.

He is the leading candidate to be the father of Sophia Altman, who married Benjamin Roush in Meigs County in 1839. Sophia was born in Ohio on 1 June 1823, which drops exactly into the gap between John, born about 1819, and Jacob, born about 1827; she had married out eleven years before the only list of the household that survives, which is why she is not in it. But no document says so. He was dead or gone by 1870.

George Duckworth (Bury district, Lancashire)

George Duckworth was a hatter in the Bury district of Lancashire. He is known from one line of one page: on 21 January 1839, when his son James married Ann Collins at the parish church of St Mary, Bury, the curate entered the groom's father as “George Duckworth” and his rank or profession, in the column kept for it, as “Hatter.”

Hatting was Bury's trade beside cotton and coal, and it was a skilled one; his son went down the pit instead, was entered in the same register as a collier, and signed the book with a mark. A Richard Duckworth stood as witness that day and signed with a mark too - very likely George's brother or his other son. Whether George lived to see his son and daughter-in-law take their children across the Atlantic about 1850 is not known. Nothing else of his life has been traced.

Adam Collins lived at Freetown, in Bury, Lancashire. His daughter Ann was under twenty-one when she married James Duckworth, a collier, at St Mary's church on 21 January 1839, and because she was a minor her father's consent was required - which is the only reason his name is in the register at all. Anthony Roush (b. 1792 Shenandoah Co. VA)

Ahnentafel #250 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (7 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Anthony Roush was born on 23 January 1792 in Shenandoah County, Virginia, near Solomon's Church, and was baptised there on 18 March 1792 with Michael Levermore standing sponsor. He was the seventh child of Henry Roush and his wife Dorothy - a Henry who, at twenty-three, had served eighty-eight days as a private in Captain John Tipton's company and been paid off at Romney in October 1775, and for whom his younger brother Jonas later stood substitute.

The family came down into the Ohio Valley, and Anthony with them. On 11 July 1812, when he was twenty, his father deeded him seventy-five acres for three hundred dollars. He married first Elizabeth Rickard, described in the family record as “a sister to the Rickard brothers,” about 1810, and after her a second wife, Catherine Hoffman. His children were set down in a numbered sequence, and Nancy, born on 16 February 1832, was the eleventh of them; she married George Nease in 1848 and is the ancestor of this line.

He probably fell heir to a share of his father's portion of the six-thousand-acre estate in Graham District, Mason County - land that had been fought over in the courts since a petition of 1806.

He lies, the family book says, in the Rickard cemetery in the White Church community of Mason County, under a small stone with no epitaph. No photograph of it has been found and he has no memorial on Find a Grave.

William Rasor Bell (fl. 1784 – 1803/09 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #256 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

William Rasor Bell appears out of nowhere in 1784, already styled planter, in Claremont County of old Camden District, South Carolina. In June of that year he helped appraise Isaac Higgs’s estate in Kershaw District; in April 1787 he bought Micajah Stokes’s entire personalty — an enslaved woman named Ann, the horses, the beds, nineteen cattle, for £105 15s — and the very next day began giving it all back to Stokes’s children “for love, good will and affection,” which in the language of that century usually means family. Ann was conveyed twice in two days between two households that were probably kin; she is the only person he is documented ever to have held, and he held her for a day. His own 1790 household was ten people and no one in slavery.

He had two surveys in 1785 — a hundred and fifty acres on Bare Branch in April, four hundred on Lynches Creek in June — and the grants on both issued to Samuel Boykin, who had paid the treasury fee, as was the local habit. His land lay on both watersheds at once: a later grant describes eighty acres “on Charleston Road between Black River and Lynches Creek, bounded N.W. by William Rasor Bell and Fred:k Bells Land.” A 1787 grant to Frederick Bell recites its north-east line as “Land Surveyed for Wm Rasor Bell, Frederick Bell, Jonathan Newman and Lewis Mathews” — one line of one document carrying three surnames that stay beside this family for three generations.

There is a single sheet of paper that might make him a Patriot. Book G, stub number 30, in South Carolina’s Revolutionary indents: “Issued the 22d. April 1784 To Mr. William Bell for Eleven pounds Eight Shillings and Seven pence Sterling for two Horses impressed for Militia Use in October 1780.” It is the only William Bell indent in the state that no man has ever been attached to, and no lineage society claims it. Nothing yet ties its claimant to him rather than to one of at least six other South Carolina William Bells, and the original stub, read at Columbia in 2026, carries no district, no auditor and no assignee to help.

On the day after Christmas 1803, “very sick & weak in body,” he made his will. It divides a plain farmer’s world among a wife and eleven children: the plantation and stock to Mary for her life, to be sold at her death or remarriage and divided among nine sons — “John Bell, Archibald Bell, Thomas Bell, William Bell, James Bell, Drury Bell, Jesse Bell, Benjamin Bell, & Robert Bell” — and feather beds to two daughters, Mary and Elizabeth, Elizabeth also getting “One Cow & Calf which is now call’d hers,” the only line in the document about a child’s own particular possession. The witnesses were Lewis Bell, Mary Stuckey and a Douglas.

He was dead by 1809. His executor Moses Gordon never served, and administration with the will annexed went on 15 April 1809 to Charles Williams of Georgetown District. Jonathan Newman — the man Archy Bell had witnessed for in 1805 — appraised the estate. The sale of the land that would have named all nine sons in one instrument has never been found, and the fiduciary volumes that would carry it are unfilmed paper in Columbia.

His own parents are unidentified. The leading frame is the Raccoon Swamp Bell clan of Albemarle Parish in Surry and Sussex counties, Virginia, where the patriarch’s 1746 will names five sons and no William; if he belongs there at all it is as an unnamed grandson. And the name Rasor — almost certainly his mother’s or grandmother’s family — has never been found anywhere.

William Pigford Sr. (d. 1803 New Hanover Co. NC)

Ahnentafel #290 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s mother’s father (8 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

He was, if his family's own story is true, a small boy found alive on the North Carolina coast sometime between 1735 and 1745 after a shipwreck. He could tell them only that his name was Billie Pigford and that he had sailed from England with his parents, who were not found. A Dutch family took him in. In March 1740 or 1741 the county court dealt with him formally: Rebecca Perry petitioned to be allowed to keep the boy herself, and the court bound him instead to Benjamin Fussell. He was called Lost Billie for the rest of his life, and his great-granddaughter was still telling the story in 1896.

Whatever he came from, he ended a landowner on both banks of Sill's Creek, in the Penderlea country where New Hanover County runs up against Duplin. In 1790 his was the only Pigford household in the whole state. He married a woman named Sarah, remembered in the family only as “a woman of Dutch descent,” and they raised a large family — William, Jacob, Timothy, Solomon, Edward, Martha and Margaret, and, in one transcription of his will, a daughter who had married into the Aiken or Eakins family.

On 27 March 1801 he had his will written out. It gives Sarah all the household furniture and moveable effects and everything else not otherwise left, “to be by her disposed of amongst her children as she may think proper,” together with the plantation he lived on and all the land east of Sill's Creek for the term of her life. Timothy got the land on the west side of the creek. Solomon got the home place after his mother's death — and in return was bound to help his brother Timothy build “a dwelling house, kitchen, corn crib, smoke house, dairy, and in clearing ten acres of land.” To the rest of the children he left one shilling sterling each. He could not sign it. The paper ends “William X Pigford (his mark),” witnessed by William Jones.

He died before March 1803, when the New Hanover Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions proved the will — and had to do it the slow way, taking the oath of William Bloodworth that the handwriting of the subscribing witness was genuinely his. Both the Sons and the Daughters of the American Revolution carry him as a patriot for signing a 1779 petition from the inhabitants of New Hanover, Duplin and Bladen counties asking that the county be divided so that militiamen could reach their musters; but nobody in this research has yet seen a Pigford name on that sheet, and in 1779 there were two William Pigfords in the county, father and son. He and Sarah lie somewhere in the Penderlea area in unmarked graves.

Mary Thorn died in Greene County, Alabama, shortly before 23 January 1844. She was the widow of James Thorn, who had made his will on 12 June 1837 and whose estate was proved there in October 1839; his will names his own children but no wife at all, which is one reason she is probably his second wife and not the mother of his family.

Her own next of kin were Stephenses. The court that opened her estate on 23 January 1844 cited Moore, James, Lewis and William Stephens, Esther Hitt, Lucinda Ball and Elizabeth Bevers as her nearest relations; James Stephens was appointed administrator; and when the estate was finally divided on 12 October 1846 it went in ten equal shares to that same set of brothers and married sisters, with the share of Esther Hitt — by then dead — going to her “Representatives,” which is how a court treats a predeceased child. The most economical explanation is that Stephens was her first married name and Thorn her second, making those ten her children. No document has ever used the word, so this is a strong reconstruction and not a settled fact; she may equally have been their sister or aunt.

Her inventory, taken on 27 April 1844, catalogues her entire world down to an eighteen-cent umbrella and a woman’s saddle — and then one line worth more than all the rest together: “1 Negro woman Jane — 475.00.” Jane had been hired out for seventeen months and was sold at the estate sale on 29 April 1844 to the administrator, James Stephens, for $700. The whole sale raised $1,075.86, so Jane was sixty-five per cent of everything Mary Thorn owned, and roughly $70 of Moore Stephens’s $107 share was her price. The clerk summarised the packet on its outside wrapper in two lines, and the first line is her name.

Mary had also been lending money to the men around her: her papers include promissory notes on Charles Stephens for $15.13 and $32.40 and on Lewis Stephens for $100. Her land was sold at the ordinary sale to Andrew Norwood for $127.09.

Elisha Truitt or Noel Truett (1786–1864 / 1769–1850 — disputed)

Ahnentafel #352 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Two men have been proposed as the father of the Reverend William Hampton Truett, and the tree offers no way to choose between them: Elisha Truitt, given as 1786–1864, and Noel Truett, given as 1769–1850. Nothing documented ties either of them to William Hampton.

The case for Elisha is circumstantial but it keeps accumulating. John Wesley Truett named a son Elisha Dozier Truett in 1877, and families of that time named children for grandfathers with great regularity. Truitt, 1814–1864, is buried in Oak Grove Cemetery at Chilton in Clarke County — the same small graveyard as John Wesley Truett — and the compiled trees make him William Hampton's brother, with a third brother Curtis Forby. An Elijah Truett served as a private in a Clarke County company in 1861. Against all that, “Noel Truett” has almost no independent footprint at all; the one place the name does turn up in this family is as the supposed father of Suzanna Truitt, the woman who married Daniel Wiggins in 1800 — a different person in a different generation, which may be how the name got attached here in the first place.

If the reconstruction holds, this is the man who brought the family from Darlington District, South Carolina, into Alabama. It is not settled, and it will not be settled by trees. It needs a South Carolina or early Alabama land or probate record — a will or a distribution naming a son William Hampton.

Susan Wiggins (1789 – 1860)

Ahnentafel #353 · his father’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father’s mother (8 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Susan Wiggins, 1789–1860, is carried in the family record as the wife of whichever of the two candidate men was William Hampton Truett's father — she is shown with both, because nobody has established which. She may not be a real person at all, in this slot.

There are two women of nearly this name in the family, and the tree looks to have folded them together. The other is her daughter Susan Elizabeth “Sue” Wiggins, 1825–1870, who married Elisha Elijah “Elige” Truitt and is buried beside him at Oak Grove Cemetery in Chilton, Clarke County. That second couple is a real, marked, documented Susan Wiggins married to an Elisha Truitt — one generation below the slot this node sits in.

Until a record is found that names William Hampton Truett's mother, this node stands as a name without a woman behind it.
