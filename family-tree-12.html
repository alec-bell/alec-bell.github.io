Susannah Bish (1818 Fairfield OH – 1908 Illinois)

Ahnentafel #109 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

Susannah Bish was born on 17 November 1818 in Fairfield County, Ohio, about eighteen miles east of Columbus, a daughter of John and Ann Elizabeth Bish. She was brought up among Lutherans, of Dutch or German descent, had what her family called “a very limited education in the old fashioned ‘deestrict schools’,” and learned “the various mysteries of house keeping.” In 1836 she came with her parents to Wayne County, Indiana, and settled in Clay Township.

On 23 March 1837, four months past her eighteenth birthday, she married Elias Lamb at her father’s house. Her dowry to the “joint stock company” was about twenty-five dollars’ worth of goods, including a heifer calf. They kept house first on his father’s farm, then on farms of their own three-quarters of a mile away, for thirty-one years. Ten children were born to her; one, an infant son, died in June 1844. In 1841 she and Elias left the Society of Friends — his people’s church, not hers — and joined the United Brethren by letter.

Her own family stayed close by. Her sister Mary Ann, born near Dayton, Ohio, in July 1823, married first William Petty and then Jesse Sykes and lived until 1915; her brother John married Sintha Johnson on 8 October 1842, a wedding written into the marriage page of the Bish family Bible, which a generation later recorded a second Lamb–Bish match, William D. Lamb to Pally A. Bish.

The family moved to Vermillion County in November 1870, and in 1887 into a house in Newport. Four years before that, on the night of 12 October 1883, burglars broke in on them; Elias was badly beaten and a bullet grazed Susannah’s head and split her ear. Their golden wedding, on 23 March 1887, drew fifty-eight relatives and friends, two ministers to perform the ceremony over again, and a table of presents — a tidy and a handkerchief from Mrs. Petty, three towels from Mary J. Sykes. They counted forty-eight descendants, most of them present.

She outlived Elias by eighteen years. In her last years she made her home with her son-in-law Levi S. Clendenning near Sugar Grove, Illinois, and she died there at three o’clock on the morning of Wednesday 8 April 1908, at ninety. Eight of her children survived her. The funeral was held in the church at Sugar Grove the next morning, and she was brought back to Indiana and buried beside Elias in the Thomas burying ground at Newport, under a stone that says only SUSANNAH, wife of E. LAMB.

Milo Bailey (Clay Twp., Wayne Co. IN)

Ahnentafel #110 · his mother’s father’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Milo Bailey farmed in Clay Township, Wayne County, Indiana. He was the son of John Bailey, a Virginian who came out of Kentucky about 1810 to Richmond, Indiana, moved on to Perry Township, and then in 1859 to Clay Township, where his son Milo was already living; John Bailey died in 1863, aged seventy-two. Milo held part of a tract of the Clay Township school land, sharing it with Ithamar Lamb and Lewis Strickler — which is to say the Baileys and the Lambs were neighbours on the ground.

That neighbourliness is why his name is on this chart. In one winter, two Lamb brothers married two Bailey women out of the same township: Azel E. Lamb and Sarah Ann Bailey in December 1859, and Irvin R. Lamb and Melissa J. Bailey in March 1860. Melissa was born in Wayne County on 24 July 1844, so her father was a Wayne County Bailey of exactly Milo’s generation and place — but nothing yet found names Milo Bailey’s children, so he stands here as a possibility and not as a fact.

Benjamin Roush (1816 – 1897)

Ahnentafel #120 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Benjamin Roush was born on 30 August 1816 at Graham Station, in Mason County, Virginia, the fifth child of John “Blackbird John” Roush and his wife Regina. He was the last of them born on the Virginia bank: his sister Hannah arrived in May 1820 in the Nease Settlement across the river in Meigs County, Ohio, so the family crossed in the years between, and he grew up on the Ohio side.

On 23 May 1839 he married Sophia Altman at Orange Township, Meigs County. She was not yet sixteen. The justice of the peace who joined them did not get round to filing his return until the following July, when he certified two weddings at once in a single sentence. Twelve children were born to them, nine of them still in the house in 1860.

He farmed in Sutton Township. In 1850 he was thirty-four, with seven children under his roof and an older Virginia-born woman, Mary Roush, thirty-six, living with them; four dwellings up the road was his father John, sixty-six. In August 1849 John Roush — a man who could not write and signed every deed with a mark — sold him sixty-nine acres and twenty poles in the north-east corner of Section 25, Town 3, Range 12 for five hundred dollars; on the same day, before the same justice and the same two witnesses, the rest of that same strip went to John Roush Junior. Neither deed says a word about why, or that the men were kin.

By 1870 Benjamin and Sophia were at Syracuse, the salt and coal village on the river, in the same year and the same place as the English Duckworth family whose daughter their son Moses would marry that September. By 1880 they had crossed back into West Virginia, to Waggener District in Mason County.

He died on 5 March 1897. Sophia outlived him by sixteen months. They lie together at Broad Run Cemetery under a barrel-topped stone on a rough-faced plinth: FATHER & MOTHER / BENJAMIN ROUSH / BORN AUG. 30, 1816 / DIED MAR. 5, 1897. To his son Moses, giving the names to a county clerk in 1925, he was always “Bennie.”

Sophia Altman (1823 OH – 1898 Meigs Co. OH)

Ahnentafel #121 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Sophia Altman was born on 1 June 1823 in Ohio, and her family remembered her as coming from Portsmouth, in Scioto County, sixty miles down the same river.

She married Benjamin Roush at Orange Township, Meigs County, on 23 May 1839, at fifteen years and eleven months — nine days short of her sixteenth birthday. Ohio required a parent’s consent for a bride that young, so a paper naming her father was filed at the Meigs County courthouse that May; it has never turned up, and who her parents were is still an open question.

Twelve children were born to the marriage, and nine of them were still under the Sutton Township roof in 1860: Moses, Joseph, Sarah, Livina, Zillah, Hannah, Maria and the rest. Six are known by name and fate — Moses B., Sarah Elizabeth Kauff, Mary Lovina Findley, Maria Ann Goodnite, Serepta Ann, and Dillia McDaniel — and four of her daughters lived long enough for Ohio and West Virginia to register their deaths, each of them naming her as their mother, spelled Altman by some clerks and Aultman by others.

The family farmed Sutton Township through the 1850s and 1860s, were at Syracuse in 1870, and by 1880 had crossed into Waggener District, Mason County, West Virginia. Sophia died back in Meigs County, Ohio, on Independence Day 1898, sixteen months after Benjamin. She was buried beside him at Broad Run, under a stone that calls her only SOPHIA, HIS WIFE — the granite does not give her maiden name at all.

James Duckworth (~1813/18 Lancashire – 1874/80 OH)

Ahnentafel #122 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s father (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

James Duckworth was born in Lancashire about 1813 to 1818, the son of George Duckworth, a hatter of the Bury district. He went down the pit instead. On 21 January 1839, “of full age, Bachelor, Collier, of Wood Head Hill,” he married Ann Collins of Freetown at the parish church of St Mary, Bury, after banns, by the curate J. F. Amos. Neither of them could write; both signed with a mark, and so did one of the witnesses, Richard Duckworth.

Three children were born to them in England — Mary about 1842, William about 1844, Adam in March 1847 — and the family spoke of themselves ever after as coming from Manchester, nine miles south of Bury and the name a Lancashire family abroad would naturally use. They crossed the Atlantic about 1850 or 1851: Sarah was born in Pennsylvania in 1851, Ralph there about 1856, and by about 1859, when Richard came, they had moved on to Ohio. It is the classic path of an English mining family — the Pennsylvania coalfield first, then the Ohio Valley pits.

At Syracuse, Meigs County, in 1860 he was forty-seven with Ann and six children in the house, the name written down as “Deckworth,” and he was still the head of his own household at fifty-six in 1870. Sarah married Moses Roush that September.

On 24 April 1874 the Meigs County probate court heard a case headed “In the Matter of the Insanity of James Duckworth.” James was present. On the sworn testimony of Wm Duckworth — almost certainly his own son William — and of Henry and August Bartels and David Thomas, the court found him insane, of less than two years’ duration, with a legal settlement in Sutton Township, and entitled to admission to the Lunatic Asylum of the State of Ohio at Athens, which had opened only three months before. Dr. Isaac Irwin was ordered to file his medical certificate; in the meantime the court directed that the patient be held in the county jail “separate and apart from those who are charged with crime.”

He was dead by June 1880, when Ann was a widow. Where he died, and where he lies, nobody knows; a man sent to a state asylum leaves behind him no will and no inventory.

Ann Collins (b. ~1818 Lancashire – aft. 1860)

Ahnentafel #123 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s mother’s mother (6 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established · Immigrant

Ann Collins was born in Lancashire about 1818, the daughter of Adam Collins of Freetown, in Bury. She was still under twenty-one, and needed her father’s consent, when she married James Duckworth, a collier of Wood Head Hill, at St Mary’s, Bury, on 21 January 1839. Neither of them could write; both made their mark.

Three children came in England — Mary about 1842, William about 1844 and Adam in March 1847, named for her father. Then the family crossed to America, most likely in 1850 or 1851, and Sarah was born in Pennsylvania, Ralph about 1856, and Richard in Ohio about 1859.

In 1860 she was forty-three and keeping a house of eight at Syracuse, Meigs County, in the Ohio Valley coalfield. Her children married into the English and Cornish mining families around them: Mary to John W. Treadwell, himself a Manchester man; William to Sarah Pascoe, a Cornish name; Sarah to Moses Roush, and Adam eight days after Sarah, in the same township, in September 1870.

In April 1874 her husband was committed to the state asylum at Athens, and by June 1880 he was dead. Ann, sixty-two and widowed, kept her own house in Sutton Township, five households from her daughter Sarah, who had four children under ten. Sarah died the following March, and her mother was right there when it happened. After that nothing more is known of her — when and where she died, and where she is buried, are lost.

George Nease (1819 – 1899 Mason Co. WV)

Ahnentafel #124 · his mother’s mother’s mother’s mother’s father’s father (6 generations back) · Probable — the IDENTIFICATION or the LINK rests on one source, or is in conflict

George Nease was born in Virginia on 10 December 1819 — his own death record says 21 November 1820 — into one of the Nease families of Mason County who had come down from the Shenandoah Valley with the Roushes and settled both banks of the Ohio.

On 15 December 1848 he married Nancy Roush: she was sixteen, he was twenty-nine. Their eldest, Susan M., was an infant in the house in 1850, when he was thirty-one and Nancy eighteen, and a six-year-old Lewis J. was there in 1860, in a household the enumerator spelled “Neoce.” He farmed in Graham District, and he and Nancy raised their family there; their son Lewis married Martha Love in 1877 and twenty years later gave his own daughter in marriage to a Roush.

George Nease died on 21 November 1899 in Mason County, and Nancy gave the county the particulars — she is entered as “Nancie Nease, wife.” It was one of the few years the register asked for the dead man’s parents, and the clerk wrote “Samuel and Catherine Nease,” a first name that does not match the Emanuel and Catharine Nease who were the only Nease couple of that generation in the county. His father’s name is, in the end, uncertain, and so is the marriage: nothing outside the family’s own book records his wedding day.

He lies at Broad Run. His marker is a small modern block cut GEORGE NEASE 1818 - 1898 — a year adrift at each end from everything else that records him.

Archibald Bell (~1770/75 – bef. 1839 Sumter SC)

Ahnentafel #128 · his father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Hypothesis — a candidate, not tied in

Archibald Bell — Archy to his neighbours, who spelled it that way in the deeds they swore to — was born about 1770 to 1775, probably a son of William Rasor Bell, a planter of Salem in Sumter District, South Carolina, who died leaving nine sons, two daughters and a widow named Mary. Whether the John Bell he names first among his older children is the John Bell who went out to Alabama about 1830, and who is Alec’s ancestor, has never been settled, so his place on this chart is a likelihood and not a fact.

He farmed on both banks of Lynches Creek, the water that divides Sumter District from Darlington, and his own paperwork crossed the line with him: he is “of the same place” in Darlington in 1807, “of the district of Sumpter” in 1810, and “of the State and District aforesaid” in Darlington again in 1817. He bought land in Darlington in 1806 and sold a hundred and twenty-seven acres of it to John B. Divine on 22 February 1817, between Bay Branch and Newmans Swamp. His wife Martha released her dower on his land twice, in October 1809 and again in February 1817, both times before the same justice of the peace, John Huggins, whose family would later marry into his. He was witnessing and swearing to deeds across the creek in Sumter from 1810, and in 1812 James Hutson senior conveyed land to “Archd Bell of the district of Sumpter.” In 1815 he mortgaged a 345-acre tract on Lynches Creek to Zachariah Cantey — the same ground a surveyor had laid out for William Rasor Bell thirty years before.

Martha died in the 1820s, and he married Margaret, a Dixon widow with grown sons of her own. That is why one of his children is called Ezekiel Dixon Bell, and why John H. Dixon, his stepson, was the executor he trusted with everything. They had five more children, with names that read like a roll-call of his neighbours: Benjamin Sumter, Margaret Louisa, Ezekiel Dixon, Hugh McCallum, Columbus Campbell. He was sued in 1830 for a witness fee. He was still buying land in 1836, at sixty, from Robert Severance, who had been his next-door neighbour since at least 1820.

He made his will on 4 October 1839, weak in body but of sound mind, and it was filed on the 22nd. He left Margaret the 275-acre plantation for her life or her widowhood, and after her to the five children of the second marriage; the residue he divided among six more — “John Bell, Celey Amoson, Harriet Bell, Martha Bell, William Wesley Bell, and Jane Bell.” He left a set of blacksmith’s tools and thirteen geese. And he directed that the rest of his property be sold to pay his debts, “including my negroe boy Limus” — a boy of about fifteen whom he had already pledged to Henry Scarborough nine months earlier. When the appraisers came on 26 October they valued Limus at seven hundred dollars against an estate of eleven hundred and eighteen. Sixty-three per cent of everything Archy Bell owned was one enslaved child, and after the sale the boy passes out of sight.

His widow remarried a Severance, which tripped the widowhood clause in the will and forced the partition of 1846–47 — and the “Margaret Severance” who bought the plantation in May 1847 was the widow buying her own place back.

Joseph Eakins (d. 1805 New Hanover Co. NC)

Ahnentafel #144 · his father’s father’s mother’s father’s father’s father’s father (7 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Joseph Eakins came into New Hanover County, North Carolina about 1788 or 1790, a man of some twenty-four years with money in his pocket and no kin of his name anywhere in the county. Where he came from, nobody knows; it is the oldest unanswered question on this side of the family. On 11 June 1790 James Fleming, a Wilmington merchant, conveyed to “Joseph Eakins of the same place, Gentleman,” for fifteen pounds currency, two tracts: a hundred acres beginning at a black oak below the old track path, and three hundred acres on the east side of Moores Creek.

He worked that land for fifteen years. In December 1793 he sold a parcel to Jacob Moore, calling himself “planter” this time rather than gentleman. In 1796 he went to the state land office and took out a patent of his own — grant No. 482, two hundred and ten acres on the east side of the Widow Moore's Branch, beginning at a pine on the east side of the New Road in the meadow, signed at Raleigh by Samuel Ashe, Governor, Captain General and Commander in Chief. By then the surveyors' boundary calls were running past “a pine, Parker's corner, now Eakins.”

He married, almost certainly, Elizabeth Pigford, and they had at least five children: John, born about 1795, William, Joseph, Sarah and Deborah.

He died in the first months of 1805, leaving children too young to act for themselves. At the March term the county court sent three men out to set aside his widow's year's provision, and they wrote down what they left in the yard to keep the household alive for twelve months: the corn in hand with the bacon, four cows and calves, one four-year-old steer, and the mark or right of hogs. On 11 April the rest went under the hammer — cattle, hogs, sheep, plantation tools, household furniture — and made $1,657.57. Edward and Jacob Pigford administered the estate, Timothy Pigford took the children, and Solomon Pigford stood security on its notes. He also left three people he had held in slavery, Simon, Rachel and Cesar, whose hired-out labour went on paying the estate's bills for years afterwards, including the schooling of his own sons.

The family never moved off that ground. His son Joseph bought the adjoining 138 acres in 1834, the deed running from “Joseph Eakins's own corner” along “Eakins's line”; and the Eakins Family Cemetery still stands on the Moores Creek land, in what is now Pender County. That son, born in 1797 and eight years old when his father died, lived to ninety-three; his is the oldest grave in it, and the newest is from 2019.
