Grace Estelle Truett (1917 Clarke Co. – 1992 Irondale)

Ahnentafel #11 · his father’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Grace Estelle Truett was born on 14 January 1917 in Clarke County, Alabama, the daughter of Thomas William Truette and Sennie Lawrence. In 1920 she was a toddler in her father's household at Coffeeville, listed as “Gracie E.” with a younger sister, Gladys. Her father died before she was thirteen; her mother was a widow for four years and then married again in the next county, and a third sister, Jewel Rae, is named in the family's Social Security records.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty, Grace married Willard Stephens at Fort Payne in DeKalb County, two hundred and fifty miles north-east of the Clarke County villages her family had lived in for four generations. Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last. In 1947 she married Eugene Raiford Rivers Jr. at Montgomery, and later Charles Leroy Roberts; Social Security records carry her under a fourth name, “Grace Tru Morgan,” so there was probably one more marriage still.

She outlived her daughter, who died at forty in 1982. Grace herself died on Monday 9 November 1992 at Irondale, in Jefferson County, aged seventy-five. The Birmingham papers carried her death notice on the Wednesday morning, the same morning the family drove west for the graveside service at eleven o'clock. It named the two sisters who survived her: Gladys, who had never left Clarke County and was living at Jackson, and Jewell, four hundred miles away at Lawton, Oklahoma.

She lies at Tuscaloosa Memorial Park under a bronze companion marker cut with roses and the words TOGETHER FOREVER. Her plate reads only GRACE T. — the T for the Truett name she had been born with seventy-five years earlier and had not used in half a century. Charles L. Roberts, who died in 1999, lies beside her under the other plate.

Gifford Herman Tuttle (1903 Indianapolis – 1974 Lake IL)

Ahnentafel #12 · his mother’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gifford Herman Tuttle was born on 7 October 1903, the first child of Dennis Clark Tuttle and Leona Marie Alexander, who had married on New Year's Eve nine months and six days earlier. His name was almost certainly a tribute: his father Dennis had been orphaned of his own father before his first birthday and was raised by a stepfather, Gifford H. Spear. A name that looked arbitrary for four generations turns out to be a small monument to the man who stepped in.

He grew up in Terre Haute, where the family lived through every census from 1910 to 1940 — his father a freight-car repairman on the Pennsylvania Railroad, his widowed grandmother Mary under the same roof. On 2 April 1923, at nineteen, he married Helen May Knott in Rush County, Indiana. They had two sons, Robert Dale and Richard Clark.

About 1944 or 1945 he moved the household out of Terre Haute to Evanston, Illinois — the moment this branch of the family left Indiana, where Tuttles and Fuerstenbergers had lived since the 1850s. By 1970 he and Helen were at Highland Park, Illinois. He died suddenly on 18 December 1974 at Highland Park, Illinois, aged seventy-one, and his body rested at St. Luke's Episcopal Church at Lee and Hinman Streets in Evanston, where a requiem mass was sung on the Friday evening of 20 December. The interment was private and the family asked that flowers be omitted. His father, Dennis, then ninety, outlived him by eight months. The day is known only because somebody wrote it by hand on the deaths page of the family Bible; the Social Security index records nothing finer than the month. A photograph survives of him with Helen in the early 1970s, the two of them hand in hand and wearing flowers, most likely at their fiftieth anniversary.

Helen May Knott (~1905 Indiana – 1977 Evanston IL)

Ahnentafel #13 · his mother’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Helen May Knott was born on 12 June 1905 in Indiana, the younger daughter of Joseph McGee Knott, a heater in a Terre Haute steel mill, and Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford Knott. Her sister Mildred was seven years older; a half-brother, Percy Ford, was a grown man by the time Helen could walk. The 1910 census of Terre Haute Ward 4 catches all three of them under one roof — Helen four, Mildred eleven, Percy twenty-four.

Three photographs survive of her childhood in Terre Haute about 1911 to 1913: one on the lawn beside her big sister Mildred, which their mother sent as a postcard inscribed “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen”; and two of Helen with her friend Grace, both girls in wide straw hats. The friend was almost certainly Grace Estella Schomer, born six months after Helen in December 1905, the daughter of the man who ran Terre Haute’s dancing school — a child performer the local paper once called “the miniature Pavlowa.” She lived to be ninety-seven.

On 2 April 1923 Helen married Gifford Herman Tuttle in Rush County; the marriage application names both her parents, and her son Robert Dale’s obituary named them again more than eighty years later. Two sons followed, Robert Dale in 1925 and Richard Clark — Alec’s grandfather — in 1929. They raised them in a rented house in Terre Haute through the Depression, with Helen’s widowed mother living under the same roof; the 1940 census has Martha Knott in the household with the two boys. Her father had died in January 1932 — the Cedar Rapids paper that printed his death notice named his two daughters, “Mrs. E. C. Cooper of this city and Mrs. Helen Tuttle of Terre Haute” — and he lay in Highland Lawn Cemetery across the city, under a flat granite marker cut simply “Joseph Knott 1851 1932”; her mother joined him in the same grave in 1945, under a matching stone.

About 1944 or 1945 the family moved north to Evanston, Illinois. A hand-tinted studio portrait shows Helen in her late thirties, dark hair set in rolled waves; another photograph catches her with Gifford in the early 1970s, near their fiftieth anniversary. Gifford died in 1974, and Helen lived on as a widow in Wilmette. She died on 24 April 1977 at Evanston Hospital, aged seventy-one. Her death notice in the Wilmette Life named her two sons — Robert D. of Evanston and Richard C. of Cincinnati — her sister Mildred Cooper of Cedar Rapids, and seven grandchildren. Mildred followed her six months later. Where Helen was buried is not known: she is not in the Knott plot at Highland Lawn, and no memorial has been made for her anywhere.

Alan C. Frost (1903 Paterson – 1982 Clyde NY)

Ahnentafel #14 · his mother’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alan Chalmers Frost was born on 10 February 1903 at Paterson, New Jersey, the third child of Harry B. Frost, an English-born silk manufacturer, and Katharine Chalmers of Orange. His middle name was his mother’s maiden name. The earliest picture of him is a studio photograph of about 1903 or 1904: an infant in a long white gown and bonnet, propped on a stool between his sister Katherine, six or seven, and his brother Harry, three or four.

He was two years old in the Paterson household of the 1905 state census, which kept a live-in servant. By 1910 the family had moved out to Wyckoff, in Franklin Township, Bergen County, and in 1915 he was twelve and set down as a “scholar” — the transcriber read his handwritten name as “Ellen C.”, which is why he is hard to find there.

By the time his father died in December 1930 he was living at Wilmington, Delaware, and he was still there the following January, when the Wyckoff column noticed that “Alan Frost, of Wilmington, Del., spent Sunday and Monday with his mother, Mrs. Harry B. Frost, of Wyckoff avenue.” She died three weeks later.

Some time in the early 1930s he married Velma Gladys Roush of Mason County, West Virginia; no record of the marriage has been found. Their daughter Judith was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 he was managing a five-and-ten at Mansfield, Ohio, and by 1950 the family was at Marion, Indiana. When he registered for the draft on 16 February 1942 he was at 1506 West Fourth Street, Marion, working for the S. S. Kresge Co. — five foot ten, a hundred and forty-five pounds, light complexion, brown hair, blue eyes — and the person who would always know his address was Velma Gladys Frost. He spent his whole working life in that chain, running the Marion store for many years at seventy hours a week, until Jupiter took over the location about 1962 and the Frosts moved to Flint, Michigan.

Velma died at Flint on 3 October 1964, and the cremation certificate gives his address as 316 West Dartmouth. He outlived her by eighteen years and died in May 1982, aged seventy-nine, at Clyde, a village in Wayne County, New York. What took him to the Finger Lakes has never been explained in this family, but his elder brother Harry had married a Clyde girl, Esther Baird, in the village church there in December 1933 and lies in its Maple Grove cemetery — which is very likely the answer.

Velma Gladys Roush (1907 Mason Co. WV – 1964 Flint MI)

Ahnentafel #15 · his mother’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Velma Gladys Roush was born on 21 June 1907 in Mason County, West Virginia, the daughter of John G. Roush and Bertha E. Nease. The assessor's birth register for Graham District wrote the date down that year, with her mother as the informant.

She was fifteen months old on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, when her father tried to fill a coal-miner's lamp from an oil can that had been standing by a hot cook stove, and the house at Broad Run went up. Her eldest sister, who had been ironing beside the table, died within hours; a second sister died five days later; the house and everything in it burned, including the family's clothes. Velma was the infant asleep in the room, and a six-year-old sister carried her out through the smoke to their grandparents' house, unseen and unaided. Her father died of his burns on 21 October. She can never have remembered any of it, and within six weeks the two people best able to tell her were dead.

Her widowed mother kept the four surviving girls together. In 1910 the household at Wagner, Mason County, was Bertha at its head with Iva, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia; in 1920 Velma was still in the county, about thirteen. She ran away from home at sixteen. She was five feet tall, and somebody wrote in her school yearbook, “She's small but oh my!”

On 6 September 1931 she married Alfred Hesterberg at Greenup, Kentucky, signing as Velma Roush and giving her parents as John Roush and Bertha Nease. He divorced her at Akron in October 1933, when she was living at Charleston, West Virginia. Her daughter was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 she was at Mansfield, Ohio, and by 1950 at Marion, Indiana, where her husband Alan C. Frost managed the S. S. Kresge store and worked seventy hours a week; about 1962 the family moved to Flint, Michigan, when another chain took the Kresge location over. She belonged to the Episcopal church at Flint.

She had arthritis, and the treatment of the day was aspirin in high doses, which is hard on the stomach. She died on 3 October 1964 at Hurley Hospital in Flint, aged fifty-seven, of a perforated duodenum, and was cremated at White Chapel three days later. Her paid death notice named her two surviving sisters, Mrs. Joseph Walk of Akron and Mrs. William Kirton of Boynton Beach. Two photographs of her survive: a studio portrait, and a colour snapshot of her in her fifties, laughing in a red-and-black plaid coat with the autumn leaves down behind her.

Thomas Jefferson Bell (1875/78 – 1921 Barbour Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #16 · his father’s father’s father’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Thomas Jefferson Bell — “Tom J.” to the stonecutter, “Tom Bell” to the probate clerk — farmed near Louisville in Barbour County, Alabama. He was one of the eleven children of John T. Bell and Sallie Cox, and he married Alice May Bradley on 13 August 1895, when she was eighteen. They had nine children. Seven were still living when the 1920 census found the whole household together at Louisville — Thomas J. 44, Alice 42, Henry 18, Ray 16, William 12, Ethel 10 and Juanita 2 — and two were already buried in the churchyard at Bethlehem Baptist, the congregation his people had belonged to since it was organised in 1824.

He died on 30 August 1921, at about forty-three, and was laid beside those two children. The Clayton Record called him a son of Mr. and Mrs. John Bell Sr., pioneer citizens, and said he was buried at Bethlehem “where two of his children are buried.” The granite his family set over him afterwards gives different dates — SEPT. 18, 1874 and AUG. 27, 1920, each a year adrift of the newspaper — and the two have never been reconciled.

He left no estate. The county’s probate minutes for the year after his death record no administration in his name, and the clerk’s index to seventy years of administrators’ records contains no Thomas J. Bell at all. There was simply nothing to divide — and his own father had died six years earlier worth fifty thousand dollars in lands, cotton, insurance and money.

So when his children finally received something, it came through that estate instead. In April 1925 a Barbour County court divided one deceased heir’s share seven ways among Tom’s children, naming each of them — Ellie, Ray, Ruth, William, Ethel, little Juanita, and “Henry Bell, Birmingham.” That decree is the court’s own answer to the question of who his children were, and it is the document that finally settled this family’s longest-standing puzzle.

Alice outlived him by thirty-five years, married again, and was brought home to lie beside him. Their names are cut side by side on one block of grey granite at Bethlehem, under the single word BELL.

Alice May Bradley (1876 – 1956 Montgomery Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #17 · his father’s father’s father’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alice May Bradley was born on 8 October 1876 in Barbour County, Alabama, the daughter of Henry Bradley and Elvira Loveless. She lost both parents young. Her father and mother lie together at Clayton City Cemetery, under two draped marble stones inside one iron rail, and the cemetery record reads their worn figures as 27 February 1887 for Henry Bradley, when Alice was ten, and 14 June 1891 for Elvira. That October a county court appointed a guardian for Alice, then fourteen, and her younger sister Katie, twelve, recording that the minors had no father living. The petitioner was a great-aunt, L. M. Bell, and among the seven signers of the four-thousand-dollar bond were two Bradley relations and John T. Bell — the man who would be her father-in-law four years later. Two years after that, a Eufaula paper printed the probate court’s own settlement of an earlier guardianship and listed her by name among “the following named minor children of Henry Bradley, deceased.”

She married Thomas Jefferson Bell on 13 August 1895, at eighteen, and had nine children. Her sister Katie married Tom’s younger brother George, so the two orphaned Bradley girls went into the same farming family at Louisville. Widowed in 1921, Alice married Mack K. Norton — and there is a quiet symmetry in it, because Mack’s first wife had been Katie, who died in 1924. Two Bradley sisters each married a Bell, each was widowed, and the same man married them in turn.

By 1935 she was living with Mack at 118 East North Street in Dothan, and it was that address her eldest son wrote on his draft card in 1942, when the form asked who would always know where he was. The 1940 census records her, on the supplementary line, as married more than once, eighteen at her first marriage, nine children born. Mack died in 1954; his obituary called her children his step-sons and step-daughters and named them as Bells, which is how this family was finally proved from the outside.

She outlived her first husband by thirty-five years and died at Montgomery on 29 December 1956, aged eighty. They brought her home to Bethlehem, in Barbour County, and buried her beside Tom under the name Bell — not Norton. Her dates are cut on the right-hand half of his stone: ALICE, OCT. 8, 1876, DEC. 29, 1956.

Andrew Jackson Eaken (1881 Lauderdale Co. MS – 1922)

Ahnentafel #18 · his father’s father’s mother’s father (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Andrew Jackson Eaken was born on 1 May 1881 in Lauderdale County, Mississippi, one of the youngest children of Joseph William “Joel W.” Eakins and Sarah Hudson Payne; he is the newborn “Andrew J.” in his father's household in the census of 1880. His people had come to Mississippi out of New Hanover County, North Carolina, and when he was asked in 1910 where his parents had been born he answered North Carolina for both.

About 1907 he married Maggie Dillon Swain, of York and Cuba in Sumter County, Alabama — the next county across the state line. The 1910 census finds the two of them at Kosters, in Mobile, with their one-year-old daughter Beatrice and Maggie's young brother Langford Swain lodging in the house; that lodger is the thread that, a century later, opened the entire Swain side of this family. In 1917 he registered for the draft at Princeton, in Jackson County at the far north-east corner of Alabama, and in 1920 the census caught him under yet another spelling, “Eakans.” He was a man who crossed the state repeatedly.

He died on 20 March 1922, aged forty. He was not buried at Birmingham, as the family's own compilation long had it, but at Clay Memorial Cemetery in Cuba, Sumter County — his wife's home ground, among her people. He left Maggie a widow at thirty-five. Their children included Alec's great-grandmother Margaret Beatrice; Andrew Jackson “Jack” Eaken, who became a colonel; Joseph William “Jo Will” Eaken, who rose to be a vice-president of American Airlines; and Melvin Swain Eaken.

The surname moved under him. “Our name was originally spelled Eakins,” his granddaughter wrote in 2009. “My father's grandfather stopped dotting the ‘i’ and it became an ‘e’. My father dropped the ‘s’, as did his brothers.” Andrew Jackson was the Eakens in the middle of that sentence.

Maggie Dillon Swain (1886 – 1985 Jefferson Co. AL)

Ahnentafel #19 · his father’s father’s mother’s mother (4 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Maggie Dillon Swain was born on 27 September 1886 in Sumter County, Alabama, a daughter of Joseph L. Swain and the woman the records call Dillon Simmons. Her middle name was her mother's given name, and it puzzled the family for three generations. In the 1900 census she is at Election Precinct 6, York, with her brothers and sisters — Leila, Patton, Maggie, Kirkland, Pinky and Langford.

She married Andrew Jackson Eaken about 1907. Their daughter Margaret Beatrice was born in 1908, and in 1910 the three of them were at Mobile with her brother Langford boarding in the house. Andrew died on 20 March 1922, and she took him home to Cuba to bury him, twenty miles from where she had been born. She was thirty-five.

On 6 November 1925 she married Thomas J. Bloebaum at Birmingham, and the marriage took her north — to Milton, in DuPage County, Illinois, by 1930, and to Wheaton in the same county in 1940 and 1950. That is the “Chicago area” her great-grandson remembered without being able to name it. The Bell family came north to join her, and in January 1944 her great-grandson Thomas Jackson Bell was killed in a car crash at sixteen; she had been going to give him a car for his birthday, and he took the keys a few days early.

Illinois held her for thirty-five years, but she kept coming home. In May 1951 the Sumter County paper reported that “Mrs. Bloebaum, of Wheaton, Ill., has returned home after a visit with her mother, Mrs. Dillon Swain” and her sisters, Mrs. Geo. McElroy and Mrs. M. C. Cobb. Her mother died within the next two years, and on 6 February 1953 Maggie and her two sisters were granted letters testamentary on the will in the Sumter County probate court, signing the notice to creditors together — Maggie S. Bloebaum, Pinkie S. McElroy, Merle S. Cobb. Her second husband died at Wheaton in July 1962 and was brought down to Clay Memorial at Cuba for burial.

She came back to Birmingham in the end, to the house her great-grandson still pictures, with a set of rosewood twin beds with beautiful inlay that later passed out of the family. She died there in March 1985 at ninety-eight, outliving her own daughter by two years. The Birmingham Post-Herald of 13 March 1985 called her Margaret Swain Eaker Bloebaum, listed her sons Col. Andrew J. and Joseph W. Eaker and her sisters Mrs. G. R. McElroy of Chattanooga and Mrs. Cliffton Cobb of Meridian, and sent her home for burial at Clay Memorial Cemetery in Cuba — where her first husband had lain since 1922 and her second since 1962. Her two surviving sisters are the Pinkie and Merle who had signed the probate notice with her thirty-two years before.

She is the “Ms Blevon” of family memory: Bloebaum, softened by sixty years, and written down as “Blevaughn” by a Social Security clerk who heard it from a grandson.
