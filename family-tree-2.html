Margaret Beatrice Eaken (1908 Sumter AL – 1983 Fulton GA)

Ahnentafel #9 · his father’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Margaret Beatrice Eaken was born on 10 July 1908 at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama — her mother's home ground, the small town on the Mississippi line where her father would be buried fourteen years later. She was the eldest child of Andrew Jackson Eaken and Maggie Dillon Swain, and she appears aged one in the 1910 census of Mobile, where her father had found work and her young uncle Langford Swain was lodging with them.

She was eighteen when she married John Henry Bell on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927; both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date worth a humorous paragraph. Their first son was born that November. There were six children; a daughter, Marion, died in infancy in 1932 and was buried at Forest Hill. In 1940 her husband was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support and served four months, and about 1942 he left for good.

By January 1944 she had taken the family north to Wheaton, Illinois, where her mother and stepfather were living, and there her eldest son Thomas Jackson — sixteen, and named for her brother — was killed in a car crash. She remarried in 1948, to Claude B. Smith, and the 1950 census finds her at Lineville, Clay County, Alabama, with her four surviving Bell children in the household.

Her later years were spent in Georgia. In July 1962, when her stepfather died, she was living at Fitzgerald in south Georgia and the notice called her “Mrs. Beatrice E. Smith”; her three brothers were then at Providence, Rhode Island, in Germany with the Army, and at Birmingham. Later she was at Clarkston outside Atlanta, where she retired as a saleswoman at Rich's department store and belonged to Vestavia Alliance Church. A cousin who had known her all her life remembered that she “always had a larger-than-life personality”; her grandson remembered “such a refined lady.” She died on Sunday 16 January 1983, aged seventy-four, and was brought back to Birmingham to lie at Forest Hill, in Section 19, near the infant daughter she had buried half a century before. Her flat granite marker reads MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10, 1908 — JAN. 16, 1983, GONE TO REST. Obituaries ran in both cities, naming her husband, her four children, her mother and her two brothers; neither version mentioned John Henry Bell.

Her grave is marked under her second married name: a flat granite stone carved with praying hands and a flower, 'MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10 1908 - JAN 16 1983, GONE TO REST'. A snapshot survives of her out in the cold in a dark coat, sitting on a wooden trestle in a bare winter yard with an iron fence behind her.

She worked for Rich's, the Atlanta department store, and belonged to the Vestavia Alliance Church in Birmingham; she died on Sunday 16 January 1983 at Clarkston, Georgia, and was buried at Forest Hill in Birmingham.

For a long time the family believed she died in 1985. 1985 was the year her own mother died, and the two had quietly become confused.

Jess Steve Willard Stephens (1917 York – 1971 Charleston)

Ahnentafel #10 · his father’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Jess Steve Willard Stephens was born on 29 April 1917 at York, in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of John Willis Stephens and Mary Price Williams. His parents named him for Jess Willard, who had been heavyweight champion of the world for thirteen months when he was born. The name stuck twice over: he signed himself “Willard” and “J. W.” by turns, his father’s initials exactly.

The York he grew up in was small and plain — 1,651 people in 1924, electric street lights, streets not paved and, in the blunt shorthand of the fire-insurance surveyors, “Water Facilities: None. Fire Dept: None.” In 1920 the family was living at Election Precinct 6, York, the same small precinct where the Swains — the family his daughter would one day marry into — had lived twenty years before.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty-two, he married Grace Estelle Truett at Fort Payne, in DeKalb County, at the other end of the state; she was twenty. Three months later the two of them were at the Stephens-Williams reunion at his parents’ house in Livingston, entered on the guest list as “Mr. and Mrs. Willard Stephens, Livingston.” Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last; Grace married again in 1947.

He worked as a mechanic. In April 1947, when his father died, he was in the Army and stationed at Washington, D.C.; by 1957 he was living at Birmingham. About 1958 he married again, to Jessie, born in 1914 and widowed or divorced before him — her names on their stone are Svedberg and Comstock — and with her he moved to Charleston, South Carolina.

He died at Charleston on 18 March 1971, aged fifty-three. He lies at Magnolia Cemetery there under a low granite stone he shares with Jessie, who followed him in 1990. The cutter put four words under his name that say where he came from: BORN YORK, ALA.

Grace Estelle Truett (1917 Clarke Co. – 1992 Irondale)

Ahnentafel #11 · his father’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Grace Estelle Truett was born on 14 January 1917 in Clarke County, Alabama, the daughter of Thomas William Truette and Sennie Lawrence. In 1920 she was a toddler in her father's household at Coffeeville, listed as “Gracie E.” with a younger sister, Gladys. Her father died before she was thirteen; her mother was a widow for four years and then married again in the next county, and a third sister, Jewel Rae, is named in the family's Social Security records.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty, Grace married Willard Stephens at Fort Payne in DeKalb County, two hundred and fifty miles north-east of the Clarke County villages her family had lived in for four generations. Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last. In 1947 she married Eugene Raiford Rivers Jr. at Montgomery, and later Charles Leroy Roberts; Social Security records carry her under a fourth name, “Grace Tru Morgan,” so there was probably one more marriage still.

She outlived her daughter, who died at forty in 1982. Grace herself died on Monday 9 November 1992 at Irondale, in Jefferson County, aged seventy-five. The Birmingham papers carried her death notice on the Wednesday morning, the same morning the family drove west for the graveside service at eleven o'clock. It named the two sisters who survived her: Gladys, who had never left Clarke County and was living at Jackson, and Jewell, four hundred miles away at Lawton, Oklahoma.

She lies at Tuscaloosa Memorial Park under a bronze companion marker cut with roses and the words TOGETHER FOREVER. Her plate reads only GRACE T. — the T for the Truett name she had been born with seventy-five years earlier and had not used in half a century. Charles L. Roberts, who died in 1999, lies beside her under the other plate.

Gifford Herman Tuttle (1903 Indianapolis – 1974 Lake IL)

Ahnentafel #12 · his mother’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gifford Herman Tuttle was born on 7 October 1903, the first child of Dennis Clark Tuttle and Leona Marie Alexander, who had married on New Year's Eve nine months and six days earlier. His name was almost certainly a tribute: his father Dennis had been orphaned of his own father before his first birthday and was raised by a stepfather, Gifford H. Spear. A name that looked arbitrary for four generations turns out to be a small monument to the man who stepped in.

He grew up in Terre Haute, where the family lived through every census from 1910 to 1940 — his father a freight-car repairman on the Pennsylvania Railroad, his widowed grandmother Mary under the same roof. On 2 April 1923, at nineteen, he married Helen May Knott in Rush County, Indiana. They had two sons, Robert Dale and Richard Clark.

On Saturday 8 October 1910, the day after his seventh birthday, his small friends gave him a surprise party at his home in Terre Haute. There were games and contests with prizes, ice cream and cake, and thirteen children: Grace McKee, George Hoffman, Raymond Lier, Opal Smith, Mary Hoffman, Kenneth Tucker, Margaret Dooley, Ron Dooley, Frank Hoffman, Edward McKee, Francis McKee, A. Tucker, and the honoree. The birthday cake was a gift from his grandmother. Six years on, in February 1916, he turns up again in the society column as one of twenty-one children at Gretchen Cunningham's birthday party at Twelfth and Mulberry streets, where the house and the ices were decorated in pink and white.

The earliest printed appearance of his name yet found is a line of small-town social news: in August 1914, aged ten, "Mr. and Mrs. Dennis Tuttle and children, Gifford and Dorothy, and Mrs. Mary Alexander, of Terre Haute" went out to West Terre Haute for the weekend.

About 1944 or 1945 he moved the household out of Terre Haute to Evanston, Illinois — the moment this branch of the family left Indiana, where Tuttles and Fuerstenbergers had lived since the 1850s. By 1970 he and Helen were at Highland Park, Illinois. He died suddenly on 18 December 1974 at Highland Park, Illinois, aged seventy-one, and his body rested at St. Luke's Episcopal Church at Lee and Hinman Streets in Evanston, where a requiem mass was sung on the Friday evening of 20 December. The interment was private and the family asked that flowers be omitted. His father, Dennis, then ninety, outlived him by eight months. The day is known only because somebody wrote it by hand on the deaths page of the family Bible; the Social Security index records nothing finer than the month. A photograph survives of him with Helen in the early 1970s, the two of them hand in hand and wearing flowers, most likely at their fiftieth anniversary.

Helen May Knott (~1905 Indiana – 1977 Evanston IL)

Ahnentafel #13 · his mother’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Helen May Knott was born on 12 June 1905 in Indiana, the younger daughter of Joseph McGee Knott, a heater in a Terre Haute steel mill, and Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford Knott. Her sister Mildred was seven years older; a half-brother, Percy Ford, was a grown man by the time Helen could walk. The 1910 census of Terre Haute Ward 4 catches all three of them under one roof — Helen four, Mildred eleven, Percy twenty-four.

Three photographs survive of her childhood in Terre Haute about 1911 to 1913: one on the lawn beside her big sister Mildred, which their mother sent as a postcard inscribed “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen”; and two of Helen with her friend Grace, both girls in wide straw hats. The friend was almost certainly Grace Estella Schomer, born six months after Helen in December 1905, the daughter of the man who ran Terre Haute’s dancing school — a child performer the local paper once called “the miniature Pavlowa.” She lived to be ninety-seven.

On 2 April 1923 Helen married Gifford Herman Tuttle in Rush County; the marriage application names both her parents, and her son Robert Dale’s obituary named them again more than eighty years later. Two sons followed, Robert Dale in 1925 and Richard Clark — Alec’s grandfather — in 1929. They raised them in a rented house in Terre Haute through the Depression, with Helen’s widowed mother living under the same roof; the 1940 census has Martha Knott in the household with the two boys. Her father had died in January 1932 — the Cedar Rapids paper that printed his death notice named his two daughters, “Mrs. E. C. Cooper of this city and Mrs. Helen Tuttle of Terre Haute” — and he lay in Highland Lawn Cemetery across the city, under a flat granite marker cut simply “Joseph Knott 1851 1932”; her mother joined him in the same grave in 1945, under a matching stone.

The Chicago Tribune of Tuesday 26 April 1977 carried her under her own maiden name - "Helen Knott Tuttle of Wilmette" - naming her two sons, her seven grandchildren and her sister Mildred Cooper of Cedar Rapids, and setting the visitation and a requiem eucharist for that evening in the Lady Chapel of St. Luke's Episcopal Church, Evanston, the church the family had belonged to for thirty years. The notice ends with the two words that explain why no grave has ever been found for her: "Interment private." Terre Haute had not forgotten her. On the same morning the Terre Haute Tribune ran her among its DEATHS under the heading MRS. HELEN TUTTLE, saying word had been received by a sister-in-law still living in the city of the death of "Mrs. Helen Tuttle, 71, Wilmet, Ill., formerly of Terre Haute, who died Sunday evening at Evanston, Ill." Sunday was the 24th; the evening is a detail no other record gives, and the age of 71 is the strongest evidence yet that she was born in 1905 and not 1904. When her second son was born in the last days of July 1929 the family were living at 2266 Warren Street, Terre Haute, and the paper announced him in a single line: "to G. H. and Helen Tuttle, 2266 Warren street, a son, Richard Clark."

About 1944 or 1945 the family moved north to Evanston, Illinois. A hand-tinted studio portrait shows Helen in her late thirties, dark hair set in rolled waves; another photograph catches her with Gifford in the early 1970s, near their fiftieth anniversary. Gifford died in 1974, and Helen lived on as a widow in Wilmette. She died on 24 April 1977 at Evanston Hospital, aged seventy-one. Her death notice in the Wilmette Life named her two sons — Robert D. of Evanston and Richard C. of Cincinnati — her sister Mildred Cooper of Cedar Rapids, and seven grandchildren. Mildred followed her six months later. Where Helen was buried is not known: she is not in the Knott plot at Highland Lawn, and no memorial has been made for her anywhere.

Alan C. Frost (1903 Paterson – 1982 Clyde NY)

Ahnentafel #14 · his mother’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alan Chalmers Frost was born on 10 February 1903 at Paterson, New Jersey, the third child of Harry B. Frost, an English-born silk manufacturer, and Katharine Chalmers of Orange. His middle name was his mother’s maiden name. In a photograph of about 1903 or 1904 he is an infant in a long white gown and bonnet, propped on a stool between his sister Katherine, six or seven, and his brother Harry, three or four.

In 1905, aged two, he was living in a Paterson household that kept a live-in servant. By 1910 the family had moved out to Wyckoff, in Franklin Township, Bergen County, and that is where he grew up; in 1915 he was twelve and at school.

By the time his father died in December 1930 he was living at Wilmington, Delaware, and he was still there in January 1932, when he came up to spend a Sunday and Monday with his widowed mother on Wyckoff Avenue. She died three weeks later.

Some time in the early 1930s — when and where is not known — he married Velma Gladys Roush of Mason County, West Virginia, and their daughter Judith was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 he was managing a five-and-ten at Mansfield, Ohio, and by 1950 the family was at Marion, Indiana. He registered for the draft on 16 February 1942 from 1506 West Fourth Street, Marion, working for the S. S. Kresge Co.: five foot ten, a hundred and forty-five pounds, light complexion, brown hair, blue eyes, and the person who would always know where to find him was Velma Gladys Frost. He spent his whole working life in that chain, running the Marion store for many years at seventy hours a week, until Jupiter took over the location about 1962 and the Frosts moved to Flint, Michigan.

Velma died at Flint on 3 October 1964; they were living then at 316 West Dartmouth. He outlived her by eighteen years and died in May 1982, aged seventy-nine, at Clyde, a village in Wayne County, New York. What took him to the Finger Lakes was never explained in this family, but his elder brother Harry had married a Clyde girl, Esther Baird, in the village church there in December 1933 and lies in its Maple Grove cemetery — which is very likely the answer. Where Alan himself is buried is not known.

Velma Gladys Roush (1907 Mason Co. WV – 1964 Flint MI)

Ahnentafel #15 · his mother’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Velma Gladys Roush was born on 21 June 1907 in Mason County, West Virginia, the daughter of John G. Roush and Bertha E. Nease. The assessor's birth register for Graham District wrote the date down that year, with her mother as the informant.

She was fifteen months old on the evening of Saturday 12 September 1908, when her father tried to fill a coal-miner's lamp from an oil can that had been standing by a hot cook stove, and the house at Broad Run went up. Her eldest sister, who had been ironing beside the table, died within hours; a second sister died five days later; the house and everything in it burned, including the family's clothes. Velma was the infant asleep in the room, and a six-year-old sister carried her out through the smoke to their grandparents' house, unseen and unaided. Her father died of his burns on 21 October. She can never have remembered any of it, and within six weeks the two people best able to tell her were dead.

Her widowed mother kept the four surviving girls together. In 1910 the household at Wagner, Mason County, was Bertha at its head with Iva, Florence, Velma and the baby Claudia; in 1920 Velma was still in the county, about thirteen. She ran away from home at sixteen. She was five feet tall, and somebody wrote in her school yearbook, “She's small but oh my!”

On 6 September 1931 she married Alfred Hesterberg at Greenup, Kentucky, signing as Velma Roush and giving her parents as John Roush and Bertha Nease. He divorced her at Akron in October 1933, when she was living at Charleston, West Virginia. Her daughter was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 she was at Mansfield, Ohio, and by 1950 at Marion, Indiana, where her husband Alan C. Frost managed the S. S. Kresge store and worked seventy hours a week; about 1962 the family moved to Flint, Michigan, when another chain took the Kresge location over. She belonged to the Episcopal church at Flint.

In Marion the family lived at 914 South D Street. Her husband managed the S. S. Kresge dollar store on the square, and she went with him to the staff parties: a Valentine dinner for the employees at the Marion Hotel in February 1947, and in May 1949 a dinner for both Marion stores' staff to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the Kresge chain. On a Monday morning in May 1953 she cut one of the ribbons at the opening of a new Marion store, standing in for the town's retail trade beside the mayor's wife and the wives of the president and vice-president of the Chamber of Commerce. At Christmas 1953 she and her husband put up a neighbourhood tree with the Drummonds at the corner of Ninth and D Streets, and the neighbours held a party round it; the Marion Chronicle printed a photograph of it lit up at night. In August 1955 she gave a lawn shower for a bride-elect at her own house, her daughter helping as assistant hostess, with an umbrella and a miniature bride and bridegroom over the gift table. She went away when she could: a few days with relatives at Cleveland in 1951, a whole month in Florida in the spring of 1954. Her health was failing well before the end. She was admitted to a Marion hospital twice within nine days in November 1959.

She is in the public record before she was two. In the Mason County court of May 1909 her mother "Bertha Roush qualified as guardian for Iva Roush, Florence Roush, Velm Roush and Della Roush, infant children of John G. Roush, deceased, and gave bond in the penalty of $500, with Moses Roush as surety" — the four fatherless girls, their mother, and their grandfather standing behind the bond, printed in the Point Pleasant Register.

She had arthritis, and the treatment of the day was aspirin in high doses, which is hard on the stomach. She died on 3 October 1964 at Hurley Hospital in Flint, aged fifty-seven, of a perforated duodenum, and was cremated at White Chapel three days later. Her paid death notice named her two surviving sisters, Mrs. Joseph Walk of Akron and Mrs. William Kirton of Boynton Beach. Two photographs of her survive: a studio portrait, and a colour snapshot of her in her fifties, laughing in a red-and-black plaid coat with the autumn leaves down behind her.
