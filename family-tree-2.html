John Henry Bell (1901/02 AL – 1944/56)

Ahnentafel #8 · his father’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

John Henry Bell was born on 4 August 1901 in Barbour County, Alabama, the eldest son of Thomas Jefferson Bell and Alice May Bradley, who farmed near Louisville. At home he was simply Henry, and that is how he grew up in his parents’ house through the 1900s and 1910s.

By 1925 he had left the county for Birmingham, and when his grandfather’s estate was divided seven ways among his father’s children the court set him down as “Henry Bell, Birmingham.” Two years later, on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927, he married Margaret Beatrice Eaken. Both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date worth a humorous column, and the News ran a feature on the fearless bridegroom. Their first son arrived six months later, and he named the boy Thomas J., for his own father.

He drove a truck for a dry cleaner, out of a house on Huntsville Road in Birmingham, then painted houses — in Birmingham, and in Atlanta through the middle thirties, at 71 Ormond Avenue SE. There were six children. The second, a daughter named Marion, was born and lost in 1932 and buried at Forest Hill in Birmingham — his wife’s family ground, and the one family burying place he would not be laid in either. In June 1940 he was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support and sentenced to twelve months; he served four. The prison ledger put him at 163 pounds, physical condition good, teeth bad. He was released on 17 October 1940 by order of Judge Emmett Perry.

Sometime in the year that followed, he left for good. On 23 February 1942 he registered for the draft at Cincinnati, before Local Board No. 19 on Baymiller Street — five foot nine, 160 pounds, blue eyes, brown hair, a knot on his left leg near the hip — and when the form asked for someone who would always know where he was, he gave his mother at 118 North Street, Dothan. It is the last thing he is known to have put his name to.

His wife and children moved north to Wheaton, Illinois, where in January 1944 his sixteen-year-old namesake was killed in a car crash; the certificate called the father simply “Henry Bell.” When his own mother died in December 1956 her obituary named six surviving children, and he was not among them. He lies in neither of the family cemeteries — not at Forest Hill with his wife and his baby daughter, and not at Bethlehem with his parents. What became of him is not known. He was very probably the Henry Bell, fifty-one, born in Alabama and calling himself separated, who was lodging at the Mount Airy home for the homeless outside Cincinnati in April 1950 — the only man of that description in the whole county.

Margaret Beatrice Eaken (1908 Sumter AL – 1983 Fulton GA)

Ahnentafel #9 · his father’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Margaret Beatrice Eaken was born on 10 July 1908 at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama — her mother’s home ground, the small town on the Mississippi line where her father would be buried fourteen years later. She was the eldest child of Andrew Jackson Eaken and Maggie Dillon Swain. By 1910 the family had moved to Mobile, where her father had found work and her young uncle Langford Swain lodged in the house with them; she was one year old. She was thirteen when her father died and the family took him home to Cuba to bury him.

She was eighteen when she married John Henry Bell on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927, and both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date worth a humorous paragraph. Their first son was born that November. There were six children; a daughter, Marion, died in infancy in 1932 and was buried at Forest Hill. In 1940 her husband was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support and served four months, and about 1942 he left for good.

By January 1944 she had taken the family north to Wheaton, Illinois, where her mother and stepfather were living, and there her eldest son Thomas Jackson — sixteen, and named for her brother — was killed in a car crash. She married again in 1948, to Claude B. Smith, and by 1950 she was at Lineville, in Clay County, Alabama, with her four surviving Bell children in the house.

Her later years were spent in Georgia. In July 1962, when her stepfather died, she was living at Fitzgerald in the south of the state and was styled “Mrs. Beatrice E. Smith”; her three brothers were then at Providence, Rhode Island, in Germany with the Army, and at Birmingham. Later she was at Clarkston, outside Atlanta, where she retired as a saleswoman at Rich’s department store, and she belonged to Vestavia Alliance Church. A cousin who had known her all her life said she “always had a larger-than-life personality”; a grandson remembered “such a refined lady.” In one snapshot she sits out in the cold in a dark coat on a wooden trestle in a bare winter yard, an iron fence behind her.

She died on Sunday 16 January 1983 at Clarkston, aged seventy-four, and was brought back to Birmingham to lie at Forest Hill, in Section 19, near the infant daughter she had buried half a century before. Her flat granite marker, carved with praying hands and a flower, reads MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10, 1908 — JAN. 16, 1983, GONE TO REST. Her death was announced in both cities, naming her husband, her four children, her mother and her two surviving brothers; neither notice mentioned John Henry Bell. Her mother outlived her by two years.

Jess Steve Willard Stephens (1917 York – 1971 Charleston)

Ahnentafel #10 · his father’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Jess Steve Willard Stephens was born on 29 April 1917 at York, in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of John Willis Stephens and Mary Price Williams. His parents named him for Jess Willard, who had been heavyweight champion of the world for thirteen months when he was born. The name stuck twice over: he signed himself “Willard” and “J. W.” by turns, his father’s initials exactly.

The York he grew up in was small and plain — 1,651 people in 1924, electric street lights, streets not paved and, in the blunt shorthand of the fire-insurance surveyors, “Water Facilities: None. Fire Dept: None.” In 1920 the family was living at Election Precinct 6, York, the same small precinct where the Swains — the family his daughter would one day marry into — had lived twenty years before.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty-two, he married Grace Estelle Truett at Fort Payne, in DeKalb County, at the other end of the state; she was twenty. The Montgomery Advertiser announced that “Mr. Stephens holds a position with the State” and that the couple would make their home in Livingston. By December 1941 they had a house on West Main Street there, and his sister Clara Mae was married in it. Three months later the two of them were at the Stephens-Williams reunion at his parents’ house in Livingston, entered on the guest list as “Mr. and Mrs. Willard Stephens, Livingston.” Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last; Grace married again in 1947.

He worked as a mechanic. In April 1947, when his father died, he was in the Army and stationed at Washington, D.C.; by 1957 he was living at Birmingham. About 1958 he married again, to Jessie, born in 1914 and widowed or divorced before him — her names on their stone are Svedberg and Comstock — and with her he moved to Charleston, South Carolina.

He died at Charleston on 18 March 1971, aged fifty-three. He lies at Magnolia Cemetery there under a low granite stone he shares with Jessie, who followed him in 1990. The cutter put four words under his name that say where he came from: BORN YORK, ALA.

Grace Estelle Truett (1917 Clarke Co. – 1992 Irondale)

Ahnentafel #11 · his father’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Grace Estelle Truett was born on 14 January 1917 in Clarke County, Alabama, the eldest daughter of Thomas William Truette and Sennie Lawrence. The family lived at Coffeeville, and in 1920 she was a toddler in the house there, called “Gracie E.,” with a younger sister, Gladys; a third sister, Jewel Rae, came after. Her father died before she was thirteen. Her mother was a widow for four years and then married again in the next county.

By January 1939 she was working as a clerk-typist in the Farm Security Administration’s Sumter County office, one of six people on a New Deal county payroll. On 8 April 1939, aged twenty, she married Willard Stephens; the marriage is recorded at Fort Payne in DeKalb County, two hundred and fifty miles north-east of the Clarke County villages her family had lived in for four generations, though the Montgomery Advertiser reported that it was “quietly solemnized in Montgomery.” Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last. In 1947 she married Eugene Raiford Rivers Jr. at Montgomery, and later Charles Leroy Roberts; she also went for a time by the name Grace Tru Morgan, so there was probably one more marriage still.

She outlived her daughter, who died at forty in 1982. Grace herself died on Monday 9 November 1992 at Irondale, in Jefferson County, aged seventy-five. The notice in the Birmingham paper on the Wednesday morning set the graveside service for eleven o’clock that same day — the family drove west for it — and named the two sisters who survived her: Gladys, who had never left Clarke County and was living at Jackson, and Jewell, four hundred miles away at Lawton, Oklahoma.

Gifford Herman Tuttle (1903 Indianapolis – 1974 Lake IL)

Ahnentafel #12 · his mother’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gifford Herman Tuttle was born at Indianapolis on 7 October 1903, the first child of Dennis Clark Tuttle and Leona Marie Alexander, who had married on New Year’s Eve nine months and six days before. His name was a quiet thank-you: his father Dennis had lost his own father before his first birthday and had been raised by a stepfather, Gifford H. Spear.

He grew up in Terre Haute, where his father repaired freight cars for the Pennsylvania Railroad, his widowed grandmother Mary Alexander lived under the same roof, and a younger sister, Dorothy, came along after him. On Saturday 8 October 1910, the day after his seventh birthday, his small friends gave him a surprise party: games and contests with prizes, ice cream and cake, and thirteen children — Grace McKee, George Hoffman, Raymond Lier, Opal Smith, Mary Hoffman, Kenneth Tucker, Margaret Dooley, Ron Dooley, Frank Hoffman, Edward McKee, Francis McKee, A. Tucker and the honoree. The birthday cake was a present from his grandmother. In August 1914, aged ten, he went out to West Terre Haute for the weekend with his parents, his sister and old Mrs. Alexander; and in February 1916 he was one of twenty-one children at Gretchen Cunningham’s birthday party at Twelfth and Mulberry streets, where the house and the ices were done up in pink and white. At Wiley High School he played on the line. The 1921 yearbook, The Red Pepper, photographed him standing with four other linemen in a striped jersey and moleskins, listed him as a guard on the second and third teams, and gave him one line: “It’s hard to get around him.” The next year he was on the roll of the Class of 1923, which he never graduated with: he married that spring.

On 2 April 1923, at nineteen, he married Helen May Knott in Rush County, Indiana. They had two sons, Robert Dale and Richard Clark. About 1944 or 1945 he moved the household out of Terre Haute to Evanston, Illinois — the moment this branch of the family left Indiana, where Tuttles and Fuerstenbergers had lived since the 1850s. The parish they joined was St. Luke’s Episcopal, at Lee and Hinman Streets, and they were still at Evanston in 1952. By 1970 he and Helen were at Highland Park. A photograph of the two of them from the early seventies, hand in hand and wearing flowers, was most likely taken at their fiftieth anniversary.

He died suddenly on 18 December 1974 at Highland Park, aged seventy-one. His body rested at St. Luke’s, where a requiem mass was sung on the Friday evening of 20 December; the interment was private, and the family asked that flowers be omitted. His father Dennis, then ninety, outlived him by eight months.
