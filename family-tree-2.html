Margaret Beatrice Eaken (1908 Sumter AL – 1983 Fulton GA)

Ahnentafel #9 · his father’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Margaret Beatrice Eaken was born on 10 July 1908 at Cuba, in Sumter County, Alabama — her mother’s home ground, the small town on the Mississippi line where her father would be buried fourteen years later. She was the eldest child of Andrew Jackson Eaken and Maggie Dillon Swain. By 1910 the family had moved to Mobile, where her father had found work and her young uncle Langford Swain lodged in the house with them; she was one year old. She was thirteen when her father died and the family took him home to Cuba to bury him.

She was eighteen when she married John Henry Bell on Friday the thirteenth of May 1927, and both Birmingham papers thought a couple marrying on such a date worth a humorous paragraph. Their first son was born that November. There were six children; a daughter, Marion, died in infancy in 1932 and was buried at Forest Hill. In 1940 her husband was convicted in Jefferson County of desertion and non-support and served four months, and about 1942 he left for good.

By January 1944 she had taken the family north to Wheaton, Illinois, where her mother and stepfather were living, and there her eldest son Thomas Jackson — sixteen, and named for her brother — was killed in a car crash. She married again in 1948, to Claude B. Smith, and by 1950 she was at Lineville, in Clay County, Alabama, with her four surviving Bell children in the house.

Her later years were spent in Georgia. In July 1962, when her stepfather died, she was living at Fitzgerald in the south of the state and was styled “Mrs. Beatrice E. Smith”; her three brothers were then at Providence, Rhode Island, in Germany with the Army, and at Birmingham. Later she was at Clarkston, outside Atlanta, where she retired as a saleswoman at Rich’s department store, and she belonged to Vestavia Alliance Church. A cousin who had known her all her life said she “always had a larger-than-life personality”; a grandson remembered “such a refined lady.” In one snapshot she sits out in the cold in a dark coat on a wooden trestle in a bare winter yard, an iron fence behind her.

She died on Sunday 16 January 1983 at Clarkston, aged seventy-four, and was brought back to Birmingham to lie at Forest Hill, in Section 19, near the infant daughter she had buried half a century before. Her flat granite marker, carved with praying hands and a flower, reads MARGARET E. SMITH, JULY 10, 1908 — JAN. 16, 1983, GONE TO REST. Her death was announced in both cities, naming her husband, her four children, her mother and her two surviving brothers; neither notice mentioned John Henry Bell. Her mother outlived her by two years.

Jess Steve Willard Stephens (1917 York – 1971 Charleston)

Ahnentafel #10 · his father’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Jess Steve Willard Stephens was born on 29 April 1917 at York, in Sumter County, Alabama, the son of John Willis Stephens and Mary Price Williams. His parents named him for Jess Willard, who had been heavyweight champion of the world for thirteen months when he was born. The name stuck twice over: he signed himself “Willard” and “J. W.” by turns, his father’s initials exactly.

The York he grew up in was small and plain — 1,651 people in 1924, electric street lights, streets not paved and, in the blunt shorthand of the fire-insurance surveyors, “Water Facilities: None. Fire Dept: None.” In 1920 the family was living at Election Precinct 6, York, the same small precinct where the Swains — the family his daughter would one day marry into — had lived twenty years before.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty-two, he married Grace Estelle Truett at Fort Payne, in DeKalb County, at the other end of the state; she was twenty. Three months later the two of them were at the Stephens-Williams reunion at his parents’ house in Livingston, entered on the guest list as “Mr. and Mrs. Willard Stephens, Livingston.” Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last; Grace married again in 1947.

He worked as a mechanic. In April 1947, when his father died, he was in the Army and stationed at Washington, D.C.; by 1957 he was living at Birmingham. About 1958 he married again, to Jessie, born in 1914 and widowed or divorced before him — her names on their stone are Svedberg and Comstock — and with her he moved to Charleston, South Carolina.

He died at Charleston on 18 March 1971, aged fifty-three. He lies at Magnolia Cemetery there under a low granite stone he shares with Jessie, who followed him in 1990. The cutter put four words under his name that say where he came from: BORN YORK, ALA.

Grace Estelle Truett (1917 Clarke Co. – 1992 Irondale)

Ahnentafel #11 · his father’s mother’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Grace Estelle Truett was born on 14 January 1917 in Clarke County, Alabama, the eldest daughter of Thomas William Truette and Sennie Lawrence. The family lived at Coffeeville, and in 1920 she was a toddler in the house there, called “Gracie E.,” with a younger sister, Gladys; a third sister, Jewel Rae, came after. Her father died before she was thirteen. Her mother was a widow for four years and then married again in the next county.

On 8 April 1939, aged twenty, Grace married Willard Stephens at Fort Payne in DeKalb County, two hundred and fifty miles north-east of the Clarke County villages her family had lived in for four generations. Their daughter Willie Joyce was born on 19 March 1942. The marriage did not last. In 1947 she married Eugene Raiford Rivers Jr. at Montgomery, and later Charles Leroy Roberts; she also went for a time by the name Grace Tru Morgan, so there was probably one more marriage still.

She outlived her daughter, who died at forty in 1982. Grace herself died on Monday 9 November 1992 at Irondale, in Jefferson County, aged seventy-five. The notice in the Birmingham paper on the Wednesday morning set the graveside service for eleven o’clock that same day — the family drove west for it — and named the two sisters who survived her: Gladys, who had never left Clarke County and was living at Jackson, and Jewell, four hundred miles away at Lawton, Oklahoma.

She lies at Tuscaloosa Memorial Park under a bronze companion marker cut with roses and the words TOGETHER FOREVER. Her plate reads only GRACE T. — the T for the Truett name she had been born with seventy-five years earlier and had not used in half a century. Charles L. Roberts, who died in 1999, lies beside her under the other plate.

Gifford Herman Tuttle (1903 Indianapolis – 1974 Lake IL)

Ahnentafel #12 · his mother’s father’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Gifford Herman Tuttle was born at Indianapolis on 7 October 1903, the first child of Dennis Clark Tuttle and Leona Marie Alexander, who had married on New Year’s Eve nine months and six days before. His name was a quiet thank-you: his father Dennis had lost his own father before his first birthday and had been raised by a stepfather, Gifford H. Spear.

He grew up in Terre Haute, where his father repaired freight cars for the Pennsylvania Railroad, his widowed grandmother Mary Alexander lived under the same roof, and a younger sister, Dorothy, came along after him. On Saturday 8 October 1910, the day after his seventh birthday, his small friends gave him a surprise party: games and contests with prizes, ice cream and cake, and thirteen children — Grace McKee, George Hoffman, Raymond Lier, Opal Smith, Mary Hoffman, Kenneth Tucker, Margaret Dooley, Ron Dooley, Frank Hoffman, Edward McKee, Francis McKee, A. Tucker and the honoree. The birthday cake was a present from his grandmother. In August 1914, aged ten, he went out to West Terre Haute for the weekend with his parents, his sister and old Mrs. Alexander; and in February 1916 he was one of twenty-one children at Gretchen Cunningham’s birthday party at Twelfth and Mulberry streets, where the house and the ices were done up in pink and white.

On 2 April 1923, at nineteen, he married Helen May Knott in Rush County, Indiana. They had two sons, Robert Dale and Richard Clark. About 1944 or 1945 he moved the household out of Terre Haute to Evanston, Illinois — the moment this branch of the family left Indiana, where Tuttles and Fuerstenbergers had lived since the 1850s. The parish they joined was St. Luke’s Episcopal, at Lee and Hinman Streets, and they were still at Evanston in 1952. By 1970 he and Helen were at Highland Park. A photograph of the two of them from the early seventies, hand in hand and wearing flowers, was most likely taken at their fiftieth anniversary.

He died suddenly on 18 December 1974 at Highland Park, aged seventy-one. His body rested at St. Luke’s, where a requiem mass was sung on the Friday evening of 20 December; the interment was private, and the family asked that flowers be omitted. His father Dennis, then ninety, outlived him by eight months.

Helen May Knott (~1905 Indiana – 1977 Evanston IL)

Ahnentafel #13 · his mother’s father’s mother (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Helen May Knott was born on 12 June 1905 in Indiana, the younger daughter of Joseph McGee Knott, a heater in a Terre Haute steel mill, and Martha Ellen (Lamb) Ford Knott. Her sister Mildred was seven years older; a half-brother, Percy Ford, was a grown man by the time Helen could walk. She grew up with all three of them under one roof in Terre Haute — in 1910 Helen was four, Mildred eleven and Percy twenty-four.

Three photographs survive of her childhood about 1911 to 1913: one on the lawn beside her big sister, which their mother sent as a postcard inscribed “My too sweet dauters, Mildred and Helen”; and two of Helen with her friend Grace, both girls in wide straw hats. Grace was almost certainly Grace Estella Schomer, born six months after her in December 1905, the daughter of the man who ran Terre Haute’s dancing school — a child performer the local paper once called “the miniature Pavlowa.” She lived to be ninety-seven.

On 2 April 1923 Helen married Gifford Herman Tuttle in Rush County. Two sons followed, Robert Dale in 1925 and Richard Clark — Alec’s grandfather — in the last days of July 1929, when the family were living at 2266 Warren Street and the Terre Haute paper announced him in a single line: “to G. H. and Helen Tuttle, 2266 Warren street, a son, Richard Clark.” They raised the boys in a rented house in Terre Haute through the Depression, with Helen’s widowed mother living under the same roof.

Her father died in January 1932 at her sister’s house in Cedar Rapids, and lay in Highland Lawn Cemetery across the city under a flat granite marker cut simply Joseph Knott 1851 1932; her mother joined him in the same grave in 1945, under a matching stone.

About 1944 or 1945 the family moved north to Evanston, Illinois, and into St. Luke’s Episcopal Church, which they belonged to for thirty years. A hand-tinted studio portrait shows Helen in her late thirties, dark hair set in rolled waves; another photograph catches her with Gifford in the early 1970s, near their fiftieth anniversary. Gifford died in 1974, and Helen lived on as a widow in Wilmette.

She died on Sunday evening, 24 April 1977, at Evanston Hospital, aged seventy-one. The Chicago Tribune carried her two mornings later under her own maiden name — “Helen Knott Tuttle of Wilmette” — naming her two sons, her seven grandchildren and her sister Mildred Cooper of Cedar Rapids, and setting the visitation and a requiem eucharist for that evening in the Lady Chapel of St. Luke’s. The notice ends with two words that explain why no grave of hers has ever been found: “Interment private.” Terre Haute had not forgotten her either: the same morning her old home-town paper ran her among its DEATHS, word having reached a sister-in-law still living in the city. Where she was buried is not known. Mildred followed her six months later.

Alan C. Frost (1903 Paterson – 1982 Clyde NY)

Ahnentafel #14 · his mother’s mother’s father (3 generations back) · Documented — person and parent-link established

Alan Chalmers Frost was born on 10 February 1903 at Paterson, New Jersey, the third child of Harry B. Frost, an English-born silk manufacturer, and Katharine Chalmers of Orange. His middle name was his mother’s maiden name. In a photograph of about 1903 or 1904 he is an infant in a long white gown and bonnet, propped on a stool between his sister Katherine, six or seven, and his brother Harry, three or four.

In 1905, aged two, he was living in a Paterson household that kept a live-in servant. By 1910 the family had moved out to Wyckoff, in Franklin Township, Bergen County, and that is where he grew up; in 1915 he was twelve and at school.

By the time his father died in December 1930 he was living at Wilmington, Delaware, and he was still there in January 1932, when he came up to spend a Sunday and Monday with his widowed mother on Wyckoff Avenue. She died three weeks later.

Some time in the early 1930s — when and where is not known — he married Velma Gladys Roush of Mason County, West Virginia, and their daughter Judith was born at Parkersburg in July 1934. By 1940 he was managing a five-and-ten at Mansfield, Ohio, and by 1950 the family was at Marion, Indiana. He registered for the draft on 16 February 1942 from 1506 West Fourth Street, Marion, working for the S. S. Kresge Co.: five foot ten, a hundred and forty-five pounds, light complexion, brown hair, blue eyes, and the person who would always know where to find him was Velma Gladys Frost. He spent his whole working life in that chain, running the Marion store for many years at seventy hours a week, until Jupiter took over the location about 1962 and the Frosts moved to Flint, Michigan.

Velma died at Flint on 3 October 1964; they were living then at 316 West Dartmouth. He outlived her by eighteen years and died in May 1982, aged seventy-nine, at Clyde, a village in Wayne County, New York. What took him to the Finger Lakes was never explained in this family, but his elder brother Harry had married a Clyde girl, Esther Baird, in the village church there in December 1933 and lies in its Maple Grove cemetery — which is very likely the answer. Where Alan himself is buried is not known.
